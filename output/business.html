My family and I moved back home to Kentucky from Los Angeles. It gave me more time with my dad before his sudden death.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
The author is glad her son got more time with his grandfather.

Courtesy of Tonilyn Hornung

  • My husband and I moved back to Kentucky from Los Angeles eight years ago.
  • I wanted to be closer to family, and for my son to get more time with his grandparents.
  • When my dad suddenly passed away four years ago, I was even more grateful for that time.

Eight years ago, my husband and I decided to move our family from California to Kentucky. On days we miss our LA favorites, like animal fries from In-N-Out Burger or warm sunshine when it's dreary and 22 degrees, we play the game: What would life be like if we stayed? It starts out breezy with talk of Disneyland rides and running into celebrities like Harry Connick Jr., but the conversation never lasts too long.

"If we stayed," I say softly, "I would've missed out on time with my dad."

When we lived on the other side of the country, we had less time together

That's when the fun stops, and my husband reaches for my hand. We spent our first four years in Louisville living 15 minutes away from my parents, and then my father died unexpectedly. If we had stayed in LA, family time would've been reduced to two weeks a year and short phone calls. I couldn't have predicted that coming back home would give us such a gift — more time with my dad. And it almost didn't happen.

Committing to a cross-country move was no small thing. This was why I hadn't packed up our house in the middle of a no-sleeping week during my son's colicky fourth trimester. I was ready for family dinners, sleepovers, and the support I'd craved since our kiddo was born. But even though I felt ready, my husband needed time. A big move meant big changes in careers and friends. So, we waited and considered while our kid moved out of diapers and into Pull-Ups.

I continued calling and FaceTiming my parents so I could feel more connected. And this is how our nightly story time chats became a ritual — until my son started asking to see his grandparents during the day, too.

One particular afternoon, my toddler said, "Call Papa?" So I phoned my dad at work because he always answered. After talking to his grandson about Legos, we finalized a time for an upcoming visit. That's when I confided that I was nervous that a move home wasn't in our future. With more confidence in me than I had in myself, he said gently, "You'll know when it's right." Since my dad was, you know…my dad, I believed him.

We decided to move back to Kentucky at the right time

Five years after our son was born and many discussions later, my husband and I finally found the right time — just before our son entered Kindergarten. My dad was right. Once we were back in Louisville, it took maybe two full days to realize that being home did not disappoint. I could feel my body relax in a way it hadn't for years. Moving home gave me the safety I was looking for. For my son, the closeness was life-changing in all the best ways.

"Papa, can you make soup?"

Basically, from the moment we moved, my son was obsessed with my dad's homemade chicken soup. It was an ordeal to prepare, but without fail, my father cooked it whenever his grandson asked. But that's not the only Papa-related craze my kid leaned into once we were home. Games of hide-and-seek, walks around the lake, making paper airplanes, and Papa's pancakes were a few of the favorites my kid loved about visiting his grandfather.

The best was after our weekly dinners, my son and I would climb into our car, and wave goodbye to my mom and dad from the driveway. As we drove out through the neighborhood and turned the corner, my dad would magically appear on the sidewalk, waving again. My son was astounded and laughed so hard, shouting, "Papa, how did you do that?!" I never told my kid that his grandfather had simply run through two backyards to catch us. I was happy to let him believe his Papa was magic.

Moving home gave us all more than we knew we needed — time together. Countless memories were made that wouldn't have happened had we stayed. Those taken-for-granted, everyday moments of homemade soup and silly waves are forever part of us. In the four years since my dad's sudden heart attack, this is what's helped us through our shock and grief. Our lives wouldn't be as full without them.

Contact Form to CRM: A Failure Proof Brief for Developers

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Connecting a form to a CRM is easy to demo and easy to get wrong in production. The demo covers the happy path. Production covers double submissions, CRM outages, unknown routing values, interrupted responses and credentials that expire. A brief that covers those cases before code starts saves most of the rework.

Start with definitions, not endpoints

Define what received, saved and assigned mean. A visitor message that says "message sent" should match the system state. If the CRM must confirm the record, say so. If the request is stored durably and processed later, the confirmation should describe receipt, and the team needs a way to find requests that stay unprocessed.

Map fields by business meaning. A person, a company and an enquiry are different records. An existing contact with a new enquiry needs a new activity or lead, not an overwritten message. Routing fields need allowed values and a controlled fallback for unknown values, not free text matching for an important assignment.

Design for the uncertain outcomes

The hard cases are worth naming in the brief. A submission identifier helps stop duplicate deals and tasks from double clicks and retries, but a duplicate delivery is not the same as a new enquiry from the same person. Using only an email address as a permanent suppression rule can discard a legitimate second request.

Also test the case where the CRM saved the record but the website did not receive the response. Retrying the whole flow without reconciliation can repeat actions. The right result is one preserved enquiry, with the agreed contact relationship, task and notification.

For errors, list each failure point, what the visitor sees, whether the request is preserved, whether retry is safe, and who is notified by name. Keep credentials server side, with only the permissions the integration needs, never in website JavaScript or a public repository. Use test and production webhook URLs correctly, and confirm the deployed site points to the published workflow, not a temporary listener.

An acceptance matrix covering new and existing contacts, missing and empty fields, unknown values, duplicates and failures turns this brief into a test plan. That is the difference between an integration that works in a demo and one that works when a real lead depends on it.

Edward Keegan: Planned high-rise apartments show new tendencies in the Chicago tall building

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Chicago architecture and high-rise design are sometimes seen as one and the same thing; many conventional histories of architecture spend all their time on our contribution to the design of tall buildings. But while that narrative is far too narrow to describe this city, Chicago keeps putting up tall buildings.

The biggest change in high-rise buildings in Chicago during the past two decades is in locale. Until recently, it was quite unusual to find a tall building that was not a church outside the Loop or away from the lakefront. But only one of the following six new projects falls within those geographic limits. From Fulton Market to Lincoln Park to Woodlawn, these six new projects — now in progress or under construction — show some new tendencies in the Chicago tall building.

Ground has been broken for Phase 1 of the extensive Woodlawn Central project located just steps from Jackson Park and the Obama Presidential Center. This initial portion of a larger master plan will be two 14-story-tall towers — a 104-unit apartment building and a 160-key hotel located over a shared three-story podium on the west side of the Metra tracks between 63rd and 64th streets. Designed by Gensler, the buildings are clad with a glass and metal window wall with faceted panels that create some visual interest across the facades. The City Council approved the project in July, and groundbreaking took place soon afterward.

Park Fowler Plus is the successor firm to Brininstool + Lynch, which gave us a series of smartly designed apartment buildings across the city before the untimely death of Brad Lynch in 2022. The 18-story-tall, 268-unit 214 N. Morgan in Fulton Market ably continues the firm’s legacy. The building plays with massing by continuing the expression of the base along one side and wrapping the tower at the top. The result is a vertically oriented C-shaped portion that embraces the more conventionally gridded bulk of the tower. It graphically differentiates the building from its duller neighbors while not deviating much from a conventional plan. The design was approved by the City Council late last year.

The new 28-story-tall 2800 N. Sheridan in Lakeview features a faceted façade facing Lincoln Park. Designed by Antunovich Associates, the 355-unit project is up against some tough architectural competition — the classical Elks National Memorial Building and Headquarters to the south and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe’s Commonwealth Apartments to the east. The tower’s primary façade is divided into three segments facing southeast. The not unappealing gridded face is divided into much larger elements than Mies’ forerunner across the street. Balconies are expressed at the west end of each segment; it’s hard not to imagine a much better building if these were eliminated. Construction is expected to commence after the demolition of the low-rise medical office building on the site.

Tucked into the old industrial enclave west of the former Cabrini-Green site and east of Goose Island, the 28-story-tall 1415 N. Dayton will contain 340 units. This is just the latest variation on a particular theme by the architects at bKL Architecture — a tower raised above a roof deck atop a low-rise podium. Earlier compositions include the bold, exposed truss at 300 N. Michigan and a more restrained yet still dramatic version at The Dylan Chicago in Fulton Market. The 1415 N. Dayton variation will be even more conservative with no funky angles, although that seems appropriate given the locale. The design was approved by City Council in May.

The new 7 W. Elm was designed by Goettsch Partners, the same firm that gave us the Viceroy Chicago. The project is a bit more conventional than Viceroy’s distinctive glass pleats with an L-shaped tower set on a four-story base that follows the angle of State Street and skews slightly from the typical Chicago grid at its north leg. Its glassy face leans into a zigzag pattern across its facades and from some angles looks like a retro video game. The base appears rather abstract, as if bricks have been blown up to giant size, but it’s appealing in a way that promises a relatively interesting streetscape on State and Elm, especially with intended retail and restaurant establishments on the first and second floors. The building is under construction with a tower crane permit issued last month.

The 33-story-tall 410 N. Elizabeth is tucked into a somewhat unusual (for Chicago) site between the Union Pacific and Metra tracks at the northwestern edge of Fulton Market in the West Loop. Designed by Archeo Design Studio, the two-faced tower has its south elevation inscribed with a bold metal multistory grid bracketed by corner balconies and the north elevation clad entirely in glass with a more subtle pattern. The Phase 1 tower will provide 383 units with 77 affordable. Plans call for a similar tower immediately west on the same city block. The building broke ground in August, and the tower crane is now in place.

Chicago’s first wave of tall buildings in the late 19th century — structures such as the Monadnock and Reliance that put us on the architectural map — were almost exclusively commercial. Our earliest notable tall residential structures didn’t appear until the second and third decades of the 20th century and tended to be rather conservative in their appearance — think large swaths of Lake Shore Drive on the Gold Coast.

But Mies’ 860-880 North Lake Shore Drive, completed in 1952, changed the calculus and blurred the line between commercial and residential architectures. And since Mies’ death in 1969, no one architect or architectural expression has dominated the tall-building discussion in a similar manner.

And there’s less of an aesthetic formula to our current crop of buildings than in the past. While Mies set a standard with the towers he designed in the 1950s and 1960s, most residential architects and developers chose exposed concrete frames in the following decades. Most of the new towers remain concrete structures, but they’re now typically clad in glass, metal or masonry, and the frame — while expressed — is generally whipped up into a more interesting confection than Mies would have tolerated.

And while our skyline is the better for that, there’s no clear direction discernable now.

A retired student-loan borrower is fighting for public service debt relief. Now he's named in a lawsuit against Trump.

After working in public service for over a decade, Donald Knierim is still waiting for student-loan forgiveness.

Now he's participating in a lawsuit in hopes of getting relief for himself — and thousands of others.

Knierim first spoke to Business Insider earlier in October. In 2015, the 71-year-old started working for Metra, Chicago's commuter railway. His goal was to stay on the job for a decade in order to earn Public Service Loan Forgiveness, which forgives student debt for government and nonprofit workers after 10 years.

He thought he had succeeded in November 2025, when his Federal Student Aid dashboard read, "Congratulations! You've satisfied your obligation, and no additional payments are required for this loan."

Knierim received an initial notice that he achieved Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

So he retired from Metra, with the belief that his student-loan payments were in the past. Months later, he received another notice: 15 payments that had previously counted toward his forgiveness progress were now rescinded, and his outstanding balance was reinstated.

On Friday, the American Federation of Teachers filed a lawsuit that includes Knierim as a declarant, alleging that the Department of Education illegally clawed back borrowers' PSLF payments and is stalling debt relief for thousands.

The stakes are high for Knierim; he cannot return to work because caring for his wife, who is dealing with serious health issues, is a full-time job.

"He lives on a fixed income, and diverting those funds to his student loans and away from mounting household expenses and savings puts his wife and him in a financially precarious situation," the lawsuit said.

Challenges with PSLF have mounted over the past few months, with borrowers reporting inaccurate payment counts and stalled applications to the buyback process, which allows a borrower to "buy back" time spent in deferment or forbearance if it would put them at the relief threshold.

Knierim is named in a lawsuit against the Education Department regarding stalled debt relief for thousands of PSLF borrowers.

Ellen Keast, communications director for the Education Department's undersecretary, said in a statement that the PSLF buyback process requires "case-by-case reviews and extensive paperwork."

"FSA has already begun transitioning this work to loan servicers as part of a broader effort to streamline operations, reduce administrative burden, and modernize the process," Keast said. "Rather than acknowledge these realities, the union has yet again chosen litigation and election-year theatrics."

Knierim said he's grateful to be part of the lawsuit and to have "a strategic path forward."

"I have a flawless payment history," Knierim said. "I have never missed a payment, and I paid what they told me to pay."

The burden of stalled student-debt relief

Knierim has spent months seeking clarity on his account. He called and sent letters to Federal Student Aid and the ombudsman's office, and he even offered to pay a lump-sum amount if it would qualify him for PSLF.

He said no one ever responded to that offer. Worried that any further action on his account could jeopardize his loan forgiveness, he didn't make any payments during this time, so he was marked as delinquent and was set to default in November. The consequences of defaulting can be severe, including wage garnishment and the seizure of federal benefits like Social Security, which Knierim relies on.

"Her servicer confirmed that there were no changes to her account that would explain the Department's clawback, and could not tell her why the Department took away the qualifying months," the lawsuit said.

Knierim said he's been diligent with his payments and that customer service representatives repeatedly assured him he was on track for loan forgiveness. He's hopeful he'll get the relief he's paid years toward and can continue his retirement with his student loans officially behind him.

"If I have to restore that payment, it's going to put my wife and me in a bad situation," Knierim said. "We're too tight in retirement to be shelling out money for stuff that we can't afford."

Scott Olson/Getty Images

  • Value-focused retailers are picking up market share and traditional chains are sliding.
  • Households are hunting for grocery deals to offset rising costs of other bills.
  • Fuel pressures may drive more inflation, but Walmart and Costco are typically slow to raise prices.

The grocery wars continue to heat up.

As US households continue to grapple with rising costs for gas, housing, and insurance, they're increasingly looking for savings on groceries.

That's giving value-focused retailers like Walmart and Costco a major leg up over more traditional grocery giants like Kroger and Albertsons.

Data released this week from consumer analytics firm Numerator showed warehouse giant Costco overtook Kroger's dollar share of US retail food and beverage sales. Costco has steadily gained share over the last two years as both Kroger and Albertsons shrank their positions.

The estimates are based on the last 12 months of sales, and show Costco's share ticking up to 8.4%, a half percentage point higher than the same period a year earlier.

And even though Walmart has long held the largest share of grocery spending, it managed to gain 0.2 percentage points to capture 20.3% of sales in the period.

By comparison, Kroger's share fell by 0.2 percentage points to 8.2% while Albertsons saw its share slide by 0.1 percentage points to 4.5%.

The shifting grocery landscape comes at a time when US shoppers are facing fresh inflation increases from last year's trade war and this year's conflict with Iran.

Costco has also managed to keep prices steady this year, but the wholesale club's leaders say they are closely watching the latest cost pressures.

Groceries haven't been significantly affected by rising diesel and transportation costs just yet, but a recent note from Deutsche Bank said Walmart is bracing for an impact that would outweigh the benefit the company received in tariff refunds this year. Walmart initially planned to invest some of those refunds in price cuts.

Deutsche Bank also said that when Walmart raises prices, its competitors do too, and Walmart typically remains the lower-priced option.

Traditional grocers aren't sitting quietly as Walmart and Costco pull ahead, though.

Kroger has been taking a page — and a CEO — from Walmart's book this year, adding Greg Foran, who formerly led Walmart's US business.

Foran's big focus this year is on lowering prices for consumers and tightening operations to make that happen. It's a $5 billion effort that includes significant cuts to the corporate workforce over the next few years and ending contracts with suppliers that aren't delivering a good enough deal.

And Albertsons said it is making several significant adjustments to its employee benefits package to manage its business in the face of rising healthcare costs.

Whether it's Walmart and Costco or Kroger and Albertsons it increasingly looks like the path to gain share in this market includes a sharper focus on value.

A father in Colorado pays out of pocket for Catholic preschool while his taxes fund other children’s preschool educations. One of his children has spina bifida, and the family stays in Colorado because Medicaid covers her needs better than anywhere else. An extra $6,000 would go a long way toward helping care for her.

A mother who works at a lumber company, married to a house painter whose work slows every winter, has six children and a seventh on the way. She once assumed Catholic school was only "for rich people."

Another mother started a florist business to help pay tuition. Three of her children attend a Catholic school in Denver, but she could not afford preschool there for her youngest, who now attends a Lutheran preschool in the state program.

I filed an amicus brief with the Supreme Court on behalf of these Catholic families in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, which the court will hear on Nov. 3. Dan and Lisa Sheley, parents who joined the Archdiocese of Denver and two parishes in challenging the state in court, tell a similar story. The Sheleys have seven children, five already at St. Mary’s, and have spent thousands of dollars out of pocket on a faith-based preschool education the state will not help fund.

Colorado’s "universal" preschool program was supposed to help families like them. It doesn’t, and the reason is a single clause. To participate, a school must promise "equal opportunity," a provision that covers sexual orientation and gender identity, and Catholic schools cannot agree to it without abandoning church teaching on the nature of marriage, human sexuality and identity. Colorado never has to say "Catholic." It only has to write a rule that Catholic schools cannot meet.

DEMOCRATS AIM TO KILL SCHOOL CHOICE FROM WISCONSIN TO THE REST OF THE US

America has seen that move before, under the Blaine amendments, and they are back.

In 1875, at a time of great anti-Catholic bigotry, Speaker of the House James G. Blaine proposed a constitutional amendment barring public money from "sectarian" schools. The real target was Catholic immigrants. The public schools of the day were Protestant, with daily instruction, prayers and hymns built around the King James Bible.

Catholic families that sought instruction for their children in their faith tradition built schools of their own. Blaine’s amendment passed the House overwhelmingly and fell just short in the Senate. The states took up the cause, and roughly three dozen wrote versions into their constitutions. None said "Catholic." None needed to. As Justice Clarence Thomas observed in Mitchell v. Helms (2000), it was an open secret that "sectarian" was code for "Catholic."

Colorado has built the modern version. Its program gives eligible 4-year-olds a benefit worth around $6,000 a year at the school their parents choose. The price of participation is the "equal opportunity" clause, which Catholic schools cannot pay. So they are out.

The Supreme Court has spent recent years undoing the Blaine legacy. In Trinity Lutheran (2017), Espinoza (2020) and Carson (2022), it held that once a state offers a public benefit, it cannot disqualify schools for being religious or for practicing their faith. Colorado’s mandate does the same work in modern vocabulary. Many preschools are willing to accept the state’s terms. But those whose faith includes a view of marriage, sex and the human person the state rejects cannot.

And Colorado’s rules bend for everyone else. Providers may serve only children with disabilities, only low-income children, only children of color, only gender-nonconforming children or only the LGBTQ+ community, and officials can grant further exceptions case by case. But there is no flexibility for Catholic schools. The lawmakers who built the program expected faith-based preschools to be part of it.

There’s a New Report on the Health of Global Nightlife Culture — How Did New York Rate?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amsterdam-based consulting and advocacy agency VibeLab has released a new report assessing the health of nightlife culture in six major global cities across six continents.

The report, the Nighttime Culture Index, was first presented Thursday (Oct. 22) at ADE, the dance industry gathering celebrating its 30-year anniversary this week in Amsterdam. The Index is a joint project by ADE and VibeLab, which sent representatives to Amsterdam, Lagos in Nigeria, New York, São Paulo, Seoul and Sydney to conduct research. Major takeaways from the report include the disparity in the price of a night out across cities, with nightclub entry costing approximately an hour of minimum-wage work in Sydney, nine hours in São Paulo and 25 hours in Lagos. (These figures exclude transportation, drinks and other costs.)

Additionally, inequality persists, with female and gender-diverse artists making up just 27% to 32% of line-ups across the six cities. Experts across five cities also rated diversity in venue ownership and leadership as poor. Safety is also cited as an issue, with respondents reporting feeling less safe in the streets surrounding nightlife areas. The issue is particularly acute in São Paulo, where 73% of respondents feel safe inside venues, compared with just 11% in the streets. In Lagos, the figures are 74% and 20%, respectively.

In New York specifically, the Index cites the main challenge in nightlife as how expensive it is, saying that “as with many major cities, cost was regularly raised as a barrier to entry for both promoters — particularly venue hire and permits — and audiences. It was suggested that the DIY scene was thriving, but costs were also blamed for making careers within the industry difficult to sustain. Many called for more funding for marginalized groups to put on events, as well as for informal and DIY spaces. Gentrification threatening venues were also raised regularly, as well as a need for more sustainable wages for workers at night.”

In terms of what’s working well in New York, the report says the city’s legendarily vibrant nightlife culture has something for everyone, “with 94% agreeing that there is a variety and diversity of different scenes, styles, sounds and genres in New York. When asked for up-and-coming sounds and artists in New York, no artist, sound or style was named twice, reflecting the diversity [on] offer in the city. Our focus group felt strongly that New York was a place where new artists, sounds and styles can emerge and that New York’s nightlife community has the power to shape its scene.”

The Index also notes that in terms of New York venues, “survey respondents rated free or outdoor events in public space almost as highly as clubs when asked which were the most important nightlife spaces in the city.”

“But despite their popularity,” the Index continues, “promoters recognized the financial and legal risks of holding DIY parties — and one participant said she would be concerned inviting Black attendees to a DIY party for fear of ‘overpolicing’ if it were shut down. However, audiences were generally positive about nightlife inclusivity, with 84% of survey respondents agreeing that people from diverse communities, races, social classes and other parts of the city participate in nighttime culture.”

Additionally, New York received a 30/100 on festivals with sustainability commitments, 45/100 on public funding for nighttime culture and 74.5/100 on education pathways into nightlife careers. Two major trends found in the city are block party gatherings and the jazz improv genre.

In total, the Index analyzed more than 12,000 event line-ups, assessed more than 3,000 venues and researched nearly 30,000 non-music cultural events. It had more than 700 respondents to its public survey, 67 focus group participants and 14 nighttime culture experts. 

Working with local coordinators in each city, VibeLab also employed desk research, expert interviews and data collection to assess trends in each. The Index assesses each city based on creative ecology, space and infrastructure, diversity and representation, community and belonging, policy and civic health and sustainability and environment.

“Behind the numbers is a night culture that is thriving, even as the business around it is under immense pressure, VibeLab co-founder Mirik Milan said in a statement. “New ideas often come out of crisis. Let’s give nightlife culture the space to do what it does best: bring people together.”

“You can’t have a nighttime industry without a nighttime culture, and that culture depends on people being able to take part,” added ADE managing director Jan-Willem van de Ven. “When a club night costs a day’s wages or more in some cities, that is a question for all of us, including the industry.”

Apple Music to Open State-of-the-Art Live Music Venue in London

📅 Sep 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Apple Music has announced it will be opening a brand new state-of-the-art music venue in London.

The new space, named Apple Music Hall, will open at the capital’s Battersea Power Station where the company has its London headquarters. An opening date or news of the first performing act has not yet been confirmed.

The 600-capacity space features the opportunity for artists to perform live, record the show in Spatial Audio in a broadcast-ready mix, and capture multi-camera video directly from the venue and its studios. 

Apple Music Hall features a mixture of standing and seated areas, and the 38-foot-wide stage can be reconfigured for traditional front-facing sets or more in-the-round experiences. The venue is also equipped with a 48-speaker spatial sound system.

The building operates as a full production facility with two recording and mix studios built to professional standards.

“The venue is also hardwired for 16 or more cameras that feed a purpose-built broadcast control room with infrastructure that supports iPhone video capture running in parallel alongside traditional broadcast cameras,” according to a press release.

Apple Music Radio’s London-based hosts and shows will also broadcast live out of the space in Battersea. For more information about Apple Music Hall, including how to access tickets for upcoming shows, fans can visit applemusichall.com.

Battersea Power Station, previously a coal-fired power station on the bank of the River Thames, has a longstanding connection to music. The building featured on the artwork for Pink Floyd’s 1977 album Animals, which shows an inflatable pink pig suspended between the four towers. Mumford & Sons performed a concert in the grounds of the park for Apple’s 50th anniversary celebration in March. In 2014, Sir Elton John performed a private concert at the venue to mark the building’s residential and commercial redevelopment.

The building of Battersea Power Station began in 1929 and finished in 1955, and provided coal-fire to the capital until 1978. The building remained empty for decades, although a number of investors proposed redevelopment and alternative uses, including failed plans to host a theme park. In 2012, football team Chelsea F.C. had a bid to purchase the site for a new stadium rejected.

In 2012, a group of Malaysian businesses purchased the site with plans to regenerate it into a commercial and residential building. About nine years later, Transport for London opened an extension of the London Underground’s Northern line to the site; in 2022, Apple took occupancy of a portion of the building and occupied 500,000 square feet for its London-based employees.

Oliver Schusser, Apple’s VP of Apple Music and international content, said: “Apple’s deep love for music dates all the way back to the very beginning, from the launch of the iPod to the long-running iTunes Music Festival to the debut of Apple Music.”

He continued, “Today, with the opening of Apple Music Hall, we are once again taking an unprecedented step further in our commitment to artists and fans by supporting live music in ways only Apple can do.”

Zane Lowe, Apple Music’s global creative director and lead anchor for Apple Music 1, added: “Apple Music has always been the home for artists, a creative partner, not just a platform. Our Brand New Apple Music Radio studios are the next step in our commitment to provide the best possible space for artists to express themselves from performing live, to telling their story on radio, to creating incredible content for their fans. The team and I couldn’t be more excited to open the doors to our new home in Battersea.”

Rachel Newman, head of Apple Music, said: “As well as Apple Music Hall, the surrounding campus — which includes recording studios, radio studios, photography and more — is a true creative space where artists can not only perform live, but also record an interview on Apple Music Radio, mix their music in Spatial Audio or host special events.”

She added, “We wanted to create a space that artists can use however they choose and tailor to their needs, one that reflects our continued commitment to supporting artists, championing storytelling, and giving fans a way to connect with their favourite acts.”
