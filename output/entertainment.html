Summary
  • There was a SQL injection vulnerability on the site, which allowed me to obtain user credentials.
  • A debug page had been left exposed, and it allowed for Remote Code Execution, which let me obtain a shell.
  • Using BloodHound, I found that JDgodd had WriteOwner permissions over Core Staff, and Core Staff had ReadLAPSPassword permissions over the DC. This allowed me to add JDgodd as a member of the Core Staff group and read the DC’s local administrator password via LAPS, which revealed the Administrator password and let me obtain a root shell. #### Recommendation
  • Use parameterized queries/placeholders to eliminate the SQL injection vulnerability.
  • Disable or remove debug pages that should only be used in development environments.

- Review and remove unnecessary group nesting that grants service accounts excessive privileges, and regularly audit ACLs for dangerous rights granted to non-admin principals, so that no path exists from a low-privileged account to Administrator.

“When I grew up, we didn’t have two Halloweens. We came last year and the kids asked about going again,” Vasquez said. “We get about the same load here as we do in the neighborhood but I love that the city does this.”

But recent adaptations like Armando Iannucci's 2019 movie The Personal History of David Copperfield and Jeffrey Scharf's Copperfield! However, for those taking notes on such things, Copperfield! does hew more closely to the events of Dickens' story, embracing some of its darkest moments without reserve. And yet, this new musical, now playing Off-Broadway, is a rapturous delight — perhaps in part because it doesn't shy away from its hero's hardships.

Copperfield! A New Musical is a classic story told with a raucous and sensationally talented ensemble.

Young David will be bounced on his mother's knee, then he's off to a seaside village to visit with Peggotty's charming family, before being sent to a cruel boarding school at the behest of his vicious stepfather, Mr. Murdstone (Will Reynolds).

From there, the poor boy will be orphaned then forced into hard labor, before seeking salvation from the aforementioned aunt. Even as he finds relief from one vexation, other troubles arise. But so, too, do meaningful relationships with debonair classmate Steerforth (Owen Squire Smith), scrappy debtor Mr. Micawber (Andy Taylor), a befuddled but brilliant family friend Mr. Dick (Aaron Michael Ray), and his benevolent bestie Agnes Wickfield (Tatiana Wechsler).

Now, as the story roams from the Copperfield estate to Yarmouth, London, and beyond across decades, Copperfield! keeps its cast almost alarmingly small. Around Conte's strong central performance, 13 adult actors not only play several characters each, but also pick up instruments to join in the orchestration. In this way, they turn a tiny stage — enhanced by flanking platforms and a modest but efficient set design — into a wide world that is cruel, kind, and often wondrous.

Bobby Conte leads a marvelous ensemble cast in Copperfield!

Bobby Conte and Cathryn Wake in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

As an admirer of Iannucci's adaptation of David Copperfield, I thought of it often while witnessing Copperfield! In that, I found a curious distinction. Iannucci focused on David's pursuit of identity, drawing attention to how he was forced to code-switch and even change his name depending on his company. Scharf's approach is less cerebral, focusing instead on the search for home.

The plays opening number "I Am Home" is reprised throughout the show, as David reflects on when he felt safe and thought his happy ending was assured. Of course, whether you know Dickens' work or just that this musical is unlikely to end in the first act, this reprise toys with our hearts, because we know there's more to come. While the company brings out guitars, trumpets, pianos, drums, and more instrumentation, Scharf's songs range from dastardly ("Mr. Murdstone Is the Master Now") to playful ("I Shake Your Hand Sir") to mournful ("Always the Pleasure Was Mine") to rollicking ("I Am a Rover"). In this, Scharf creates a thrilling odyssey in a simple setting, using his music and relying on this sensational cast to build David's world with their unbridled vigor.

Tatiana Wechsler, Aaron Michael Ray, Bobby Conte, and Kara Mikula in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

While the whole of the cast sings their hearts out, there are a few standouts. Cormack, who plays Peggotty and Mrs. Micawber, among others, bursts with warmth and comedic bounciness, so her every entrance is a blessing. Mikula, as Aunt Betsey, is a deranged delight, singing with the trilling enthusiasm and the force of a windstorm, to the point where I yearned to see her tackle Sweeney Todd as Mrs. Lovett. Alexander Sovronsky awed me with the sheer breadth of his characters, playing the effusively warm Mr. Peggotty, the malevolent Mr. Creakle, the dignified then drunken Mr. Quinion, and a smattering of bit parts aside. With the toss of his lush silver hair and a switch up of his mouth, Sovronsky needs no costume change to change character. And in group numbers, he is so clearly enthralled, whether singing, dancing, or playing an instrument, that he's hard to look away from.

Conte is strong in the lead role, delivering nuance and heartache whether mourning over his mother or falling in love, not once but twice. Wechsler is lovely in the role of besotted Agnes, while Wake is hilarious and surprising as David's first love, Dora. Playing his mother, Wake is serene and oft on the verge of tears, bursting with motherly adoration of him. As his sweet, giddy bride, she is unhinged in the best way. Watching her throw herself into the Olympian vocal back-and-forth of their courtship ("Do-o-or-a, Part One and Part Two"), I was reminded of Jane Krakowski and her recent Broadway stint in Oh, Mary! In this latter role Wake is explosively hilarious, deepening my appreciation for the sincerity in her first-act performance of Clara.

With so much going on, Copperfield! is a whole lot of show. And admittedly, that means the third act drags as the eponymous hero catches up to the romantic realization even newcomers to the story will clock in Act II. Still, bursting with energy, passion, and jaw-dropping talent, Copperfield! The New Musical is a wonder not to be missed.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

SEE ALSO: 'Memoirs of Amorous Gentlemen' review: Duncan Sheik's musical adaptation gets kink right, but falters elsewhere

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 will remove real-world footage of the 2022 Seoul crush incident after severe backlash in South Korea. Bereaved families issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of these videos, and Activision has since vowed to cut them from the title.

The game is set in the Korean Peninsula, and real-world footage of several events is shown in the opening cutscene as a means to ground its narrative in reality.

Among the videos are scenes of the 2022 Seoul crush incident, which happened during a Halloween celebration on Oct. 29 in Itaewon, when a crowd suddenly surged and began to trample or crush those within it. Around 160 deaths were confirmed, most of whom were young adults, making it one of the deadliest events in South Korean history.

Families of those who perished or were injured in the incident issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of this footage, telling the BBC they were “appalled.”

“We cannot help but be appalled that the chaotic and desperate scene, in which many citizens at the site performed CPR to save one another and victims lay on the freezing street, was used to create a scene in a game,” they said.

“Using such an event as material for a game made for entertainment is in itself highly inappropriate and senseless,” they added.

Modern Warfare 4 is largely set in the Korean Peninsula during a “New Korean War.” Image via Activision

Activision has since responded to the backlash, promising to cut the scenes entirely in the game’s next update on Nov. 12. The company told the BBC it never “intended to reference or trivialize the Itaewon disaster or any other real-world tragedy in any way, shape or form,” but merely used real-world footage to frame the game’s fictional story.

The Modern Warfare franchise, both the original trilogy and these new games, is well known for its shocking and mortifying scenes. However, the difference here is that, while the most horrific moments in the games were all fictional, Infinity Ward used real footage of a tragedy. KATSEYE, MAZZEL Producer Taka Perry Talks Career From Sydney to Tokyo & Appeal of Japanese Music: Unbound Japan Interview

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Unbound Japan is an interview series featuring entertainment industry professionals based outside the country and those sending Japan’s music out into the world. This instalment features music producer Taka Perry. Born and raised in Australia to an American father and a Japanese mother, he built his career as a producer there before expanding his work to Japan. In 2024, “Touch” by global girl group KATSEYE, which he wrote and produced, became a massive hit. The 28-year-old producer spoke about his career and creative process in this interview with Billboard Japan and shared how Japanese music looks to him as someone born in Australia and raised on Western music culture.

Your launched your career in Australia, where you’re from.

I was born and raised in Canberra and lived there all the way through high school. Canberra is the capital, but it has a relatively small population. I’d wanted to do music ever since high school, but in Australia, most people who want to pursue music go to Sydney or Melbourne in order to do it. I chose Sydney and moved there shortly after graduating high school.

When did you first become interested in music production?

I played clarinet from elementary school, but in junior high I started teaching myself guitar because I wanted to be in a rock n roll band. From there I branched out into bass and piano, and formed a band with friends covering Red Hot Chilli Peppers and Nirvana. I wanted to record our own songs, and after my parents bought me a DAW for my 12th birthday, I got hooked on songwriting and production. I then gradually became more drawn to working behind the scenes than to being in a band.

When did you start branching out to Japan?

I started seriously thinking about working in Japan around 2019–20, right before COVID. I’d been a producer in Australia for four or five years by then and was getting some great results. But the industry there is limited in size, so once your career reaches a certain point, a lot of people move to L.A. or London, or more recently, South Korea. I speak Japanese and I’m familiar with Japanese culture, so I felt it would be a waste not to get involved in the Japanese scene.

Back then, I looked up to JP THE WAVY and WILYWNKA, and started doing sessions in Japan with the goal of becoming a hip-hop beat-maker. I’d come to Japan three or four times a year, for three or four weeks each time. I’d DM artists, do sessions with them, then fly home. As I kept that up, I ended up with more work from Japan than from Australia. I decided I needed to live in Japan, both for my career and personally, so I moved my base here last year.

After working in the music scene in Australia, did you face any difficulties when you began working in the Japanese one?

By the time I decided to start making music in Japan, I was lucky enough to have already had results and credits as a producer in Australia. If I’d taken that to the U.S., say, it would have carried over to some extent, since both are Western music and both are English-speaking. But when I came to Japan, a lot of the time it didn’t register with Japanese artists or industry people. So the hardest part for me and my longtime management, was rebuilding the career I’d built up from scratch. Doing so with no publisher to help navigate such a unique industry is something we’re incredibly proud of.

We’d also like to hear about some of the songs you’ve produced. Australian artist Ruel’s “Younger,” released in June 2018, won the Breakthrough Artist award at Australia’s ARIA Music Awards and was certified multi-platinum by the Australian Recording Industry Association (ARIA). How did you come to be involved in producing it?

I met M-Phazes, who had been Ruel’s producer for years, once at a co-writing camp. After that, I regularly sent him demos and playlists. When he was working on Ruel’s EP, he invited me along, like, “If you’re up for it, why don’t you come to the studio for a couple of days and we’ll make some stuff together?” I didn’t know how the things we made in those two days would be used, but later he told me, “We’ve decided to use your parts from this song and this song.” Those were “Younger” and “Not Thinkin’ Bout You.” After the release they started charting, and “Younger” was certified multi-platinum when I was 20.

And then there’s KATSEYE’s “Touch,” which became a huge hit worldwide. Did you have a sense of the song catching on worldwide as it was happening?

I did. I was a NewJeans fan, and I wanted to make some NewJeans-style tracks, so I did a session in Sydney with a songwriter friend to write some toplines over some musical ideas of mine. One of the songs from that session was the demo version of “Touch.” My friend sent it to producer Cashmere Cat, who liked it too. He played it for the head of HYBE, whom he knew. He liked it as well and said he wanted to use it as the signature song for a pre-debut group called KATSEYE. But since they hadn’t made their debut yet, I had no idea at that point how big the release would be. So until it came out and started going viral in various countries, I had no way of predicting what would happen. I was watching social media in real time every day and was like, “They’re the most-streamed groups in the world again today!”

So it was a big hit born out of personal connections. Do you work on a lot of K-pop projects like that?

I produce in South Korea regularly too. Right after KATSEYE’s “Touch” came out, I was going there for sessions about as much as I was doing them in Japan. Some of that work has led to releases, but in Korea almost everything goes through competitive pitches. And since K-pop has grown into a global operation, it’s not just Korean producers and songwriters. I think Americans, Brits, Swedes and others are doing sessions in Seoul every day.

Japanese artists have become increasingly globally minded lately. Do you think about including elements aimed at overseas markets?

It’s not something I do deliberately, but I make music that keeps a Japanese feel while still having a truly global sound and easy for overseas listeners to take in. It’s a balance I’m personally happy with.

Where do you think that “Japanese feel” comes from?

In concrete terms, it’s things like packing in a lot of information, or having the key change in the chorus. I think Japanese music is strong lyrically. Overseas, it’s almost unheard of for the lyrics to change between the first and second chorus. Or for the key to change only in the third chorus. Japanese music is complex. I think that’s a good thing, because it’s part of its identity.

We’re hoping that people aiming for the world stage will read this series and come away encouraged. If you were to give advice to someone who wants to take on a challenge abroad, what would you say?

First of all, if you want to take on a challenge abroad, I think it’s really important to actually go to the place where you want to do it. These days you can build all kinds of relationships on social media, but if you want to work in L.A., for example, go to L.A. and make connections there. In terms of music production, collaborations can now happen entirely remotely. Even so, I think working face to face, where you can see each other’s expressions, produces songs with more creative soul. It’s important to go to all kinds of places, connect with all kinds of people and make songs together.

Is there anything you’d like to take on next?

I’d be really happy if I could play an active part in bringing the Japanese music scene further onto the global stage. I don’t think there are many bilingual producers based in Japan that have a huge focus on this. I’d like to put my unique strengths to use in these situations and forge more collaborations between Japan and other countries and introduce more Japanese music to artists overseas.

YOSHIKI Reflects on Music, Life, Legacy & His New Collaboration ‘Forever Love (Live)’ With Josh Groban: Interview

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

YOSHIKI and Josh Groban‘s collaborative recording of “Forever Love (Live)” was released for streaming worldwide on Aug. 28. The ballad, originally released in 1996 as X JAPAN’s 14th single, gets a new rendition, one born from a live performance which the two artists gave together at YOSHIKI CLASSICAL 2026 IN LOS ANGELES AT WALT DISNEY CONCERT HALL “VIOLET NIGHT” on July 17, 2026. 

Billboard JAPAN sat down with YOSHIKI to talk about how the collaboration came together, the raw emotions behind the original song, his ongoing commitment to philanthropy and what’s next.

Could you explain what led to Josh Groban singing “Forever Love” with you, and then to releasing the song?

I first met Josh at an event during the Sundance Film Festival in Park City in 2016, when the documentary “WE ARE X” premiered there. I’ve spent over half my life living in LA, so when I put on YOSHIKI CLASSICAL 2026 IN LOS ANGELES, I thought it would be good for the show to include friends that I had a personal connection with. This year is the 30th anniversary of the release of “Forever Love,” and I had this intuitive feeling that if Josh sang it, it would turn out amazingly. I added English lyrics to the verse, making kind of a “bilingual version.” I wanted to create a perfect balance for listeners everywhere, easy to understand for both English listeners and Japanese listeners.

When Josh came to Japan and performed at Bunkamura Orchard Hall earlier this year, on February 21, you appeared as a special guest, right?

Right. I just happened to be in Japan myself at the time, and the decision for me to perform was last minute, the day before the show. We played “ENDLESS RAIN,” and Josh sang some of the lyrics in Japanese. That’s when I realized that he was good at singing in Japanese, too.

In the new release, the listener can feel the power of his singing voice and your delicate piano playing like they’re right there at the show. Was there anything you paid special attention to in the concert to create that kind of dynamic sound for the listener?

First off, I freely change up the key of my music to match different vocalists. “Forever Love” was originally written for X JAPAN, so it was in a very high key. I lowered it by a seventh for Josh. I listened to dozens of his songs to find the right range, and I thought that this would be around the right range for him, so I suggested it to him, and he sang it in that range.

What kind of emotions were you feeling when you first wrote the song, three decades ago back in 1996?

I’d been living a really chaotic life. Honestly, at the time, I didn’t expect I’d be living for very long. I had a very pessimistic view on life, partly because of the fact that my father took his own life. I figured that one day I’d just vanish somewhere. But, my music might just live on forever. That’s why I named the song “Forever Love.” I remember, when writing the song, thinking that while everyone will eventually disappear from this Earth, perhaps love was eternal. I never imagined that I’d still be here, alive, thirty years later.

It seems clear that you poured a lot of emotion into the song, because it’s still touching people’s hearts even now, three decades later.

I find the whole thing profoundly moving. On the one hand, I’ll wonder to myself, “How have I managed to stay alive so long?” But I feel like I’ve lived because of the support everyone has given me. I feel so thankful. I think that’s why the song could cross the language barriers and national borders through this performance with Josh.

In addition to your music, you’ve been heavily involved in philanthropic activities. You’ve donated generously and you’ve taken part in support activities to help people around the world. What are your feelings on the world today? Is there anything you’d like people to do?

I think this connects to what I was talking about just now, wondering why I’m still alive, but when I think “Why am I still here? Why am I alive?” I’ll often slip into thinking “Should I even be alive?” This applies to creative work, too. But then I’ll think “Maybe this song will help someone out there,” and it really motivates me to create. I think that’s why I’ll have these runs of releases. I write a lot of music, and the same kind of concept applies (to my philanthropy). I feel like if what I do through my charity work helps someone, then my life has meaning. That, in turn, saves me. When I think that my existence helps others, then I feel like it’s okay for me to live. Doing charity work for others saves me, in turn.

In a video interview before the concert, you said that “life was a series of challenges.” So, are you constantly taking on challenges in order to live, and in order to keep making your music?

I think my fans and my friends have given me a second or a third lease on life. I feel like if you’re going to live anyway, you’ve got no choice but to really live your life. For me, that covers a lot, but if I can, I want to live my life giving it full gas and really take all that life has to offer. I think the very fact that we’re granted a life means that we’ve got a mission to accomplish. I’m not sure if life consists of finding that mission, but I want people to know that it is for everyone too, not just me. If you’re going to live, you have no choice but to live your life to the fullest. I think that philosophy is part of “Forever Love,” too.

We’re all looking forward to what you do next.

I’ve been writing music for years, and even now, I’m writing at breakneck speeds. I’ve collaborated with all kinds of artists over the past few years, crossing over genres three or four times, including pop and rock. If I try to release something to represent a culmination of all of that, I’ll never end out putting it out. Instead, the approach I’m trying to take is suddenly putting out a piece of music, like I did this time. And eventually making all of those individual releases come together. For example, I’ve been an artist for decades, and I still find the concept of an album really important. But I’ve trapped myself by that idea, and for this reason or the other I haven’t been able to put out an album. It’s been like that for a few years, and it’s very frustrating. So I’ve started feeling, quite strongly, that I need to free myself from this fixation on the concept of albums, and instead just keep putting out individual songs. I’m writing all these new songs, but then I’m also putting out a song that I wrote 30 years ago. But I think that’s totally fine, too. For me, “Forever Love (Live)” feels like the first breakthrough in a process of freeing myself from these preconceptions and fixations.

Kroi’s new song “SPIN” was written as the opener for the anime Steel Ball Run: JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure. A rousing melody and beat that evoke the American Old West, where the story is set, blend with Kroi’s signature chaotic funkiness, resulting in a highly original and exhilarating track. The song took off on streaming right after release, including the U.S. and debuted at No. 44 on Billboard’s Hot Rock & Alternative Songs chart (dated Oct. 10, 2026), the band’s first-ever entry on a Billboard U.S. chart.

Talking with the band revealed the relentless spirit of inquiry and creative urge behind it — an attempt to depict a new world no one has ever seen by digging into cultures closely tied to the band, from the anime song, a uniquely Japanese form of pop, to The Ventures, part of vocalist Leo Uchida’s roots. Drummer Hidetomo Masuda was unfortunately absent due to illness, but the remaining four members shared the story behind the making of “SPIN.”

Your new song “SPIN” is the opening theme for the anime Steel Ball Run: JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure. What does Steel Ball Run, and by extension the JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure series, mean to the members of Kroi?

Yuki Hasebe (Gt.): Of all the members, I’m the one who especially loves JoJo, and I’ve read quite a lot of it. Steel Ball Run was my favorite of them all. I’ve always been drawn to the Old West aesthetic, so pairing that with JoJo is, for me, a collaboration between two things I love. When we got the offer to do the opening theme this time, I was really happy and surprised.

Uchida: I hadn’t experienced JoJo yet, but it’s a series that people around me are deeply passionate about, so I’d been wanting to check it out someday. This was a good opportunity in that sense, too.

What did you feel after experiencing the world of JoJo?

Uchida: It really is amazing. It pulls you in. The works I love make me feel that there’s an atmosphere and a sensation you can’t experience unless you come back to them. JoJo is exactly that. There’s a feeling you can’t get unless you come back here. I was moved by how incredible that world-building is.

“SPIN” has elements reminiscent of Ennio Morricone’s film scores along with Kroi’s signature funkiness, making it a strikingly original song. What concept were you working from?

Uchida: The concept was something like, having no choice but to keep moving through a wilderness where you can’t see what’s ahead, still believing there’s a goal. There’s a goal in your heart that you want to move toward, but don’t know if it’s really the right direction. Still, you have no choice but to keep going. I think that feeling exists in Steel Ball Run, and it exists in the journey of making things, too.

You spoke of “a goal in your heart,” and the line “The frontier that still remains is within” really does give the sense of locating the goal you should aim for inside yourself.

Uchida: Right. When I write lyrics, for example, I can’t write with things brought in from the outside. I can only write with what’s inside me. So in the end, all I can do is dive down into myself.

Images of “wilderness” and “sand” have appeared in Kroi’s songs before, haven’t they?

Uchida: Yeah, it’s strange. We made a song called “Funky GUNSLINGER” a few years ago, and when we were studying up on things like that, thinking, “Let’s try writing a song with sandy terrain as its field” with no tie-in involved, we got an offer from a work with an Arabian flavor, then an offer from a work set in the desert, and this time an offer from a work set in the Old West. It feels like something is descending on us.

As a result, “SPIN” ended up with an extremely original sound, even within today’s Japanese music scene.

Uchida: There’s no one like this in Japan’s major-label music scene right now, with this kind of Morricone-esque feel. I also had an image of The Ventures in mind. The first band I was ever in was a Ventures cover band that an older guy I knew made me join when I was a kid. [Laughs] I played drums in it, so The Ventures are part of my foundation. A lot of people may associate The Ventures with surf music, but ever since I was a kid, I’d listen to songs like “Apache” and think, “This sounds like a Western.” I wanted Hasebe to take on a song with that kind of feel, and besides, a lot of people who play instruments in Japan have The Ventures in their foundation. The first thing I ever played on guitar was “Pipeline,” too. But there isn’t much music now that puts that front and center. So I also wanted to deliver that kind of thing to everyone once again.

Hasebe, Seki and Chiba, how did you approach the making of “SPIN”?

Hasebe: Within the ensemble, the guitar was in charge of evoking a deserted landscape, so I tried putting in a sound like a horse’s whinny and quoting memorable phrases from Morricone. There was a lot of room to mix in things I love, and it was a lot of fun.

You’ve sometimes performed live in Western-inspired outfits, and you’re quite fond of this kind of Western flavor, aren’t you?

Hasebe: Yeah. It’s a bit like Leo’s The Ventures anecdote just now, but sometimes you realize later on what you loved when you were little. When we made “Funky GUNSLINGER,” which came up a moment ago, I watched Western flicks to study them, but long before that, back in junior high, I loved Stevie Ray Vaughan. So it’s always been a good fit for me.

Seki, how about the bass?

Masanori Seki (Ba.): This time Yuki took on the role of expressing the Western feel and the world of Steel Ball Run, so I decided to keep a hi-fi quality in the sound and carry the power and momentum of an anime opener. Within that, though, I tried adding nuances like the sound of a horse’s hooves, so there were also parts where I was conscious of that sense of getting it right.

Chiba, what about the keyboards and the mix?

Daiki Chiba (Key.): What I prioritized on the keyboards was momentum. I remember it being hard to figure out the rhythmic phrasing, what kind of note placement would fit the beat and create momentum. As for the mix, the version that plays in the anime is my mix, but the one being released was mixed by someone else. We asked Nathan Boddy to do it. Our label boss suggested, “Do you want to try asking someone else to mix it?”

I see. Listening to “SPIN,” there’s a sense of scale, like a vast landscape opening up, and at the same time a claustrophobic chaos, like peering into an inner world. That chaotic feel really leaves an impression.

Uchida: This time I also wanted to face the peculiarity of the anime song, something that’s been built up over time within Japanese culture. Layers stacked on top of layers like this, fast-moving changes, and on top of that, it’s a vocal song. I don’t think you find music like this much outside Japan. I think it became the most chaotic song we’ve made recently, one that also carries that essence of the Japanese anime song. There were a lot of tracks, too. We brought back the horns at the very end, like, “Let’s put them in after all,” after cutting them at the demo stage, and the arpeggiator Masuda added is a nice touch as well. I think having that mechanical sound conveys that this song isn’t simply a recreation of a Western, but a story of a new world based on one.

“Not a recreation of a Western, but a story of a new world” is exactly what comes across when listening.

Uchida: We crammed in all kinds of things, each one of them has meaning, and the sum of them became a song that connects Kroi and Steel Ball Run. That’s how it feels. Part of what I came to love about JoJo is that “chaos.” The premise is fantasy, but because that chaos is there, people become entangled with one another, the story twists and winds, and the work as a whole becomes realistic. That strikes me as being very much like life itself. I hope we were able to express that in this song, too.
