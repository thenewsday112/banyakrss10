I run an 8-person startup that uses AI agents. We don't let our agents run on vibes — here's the playbook system we created

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Rachel Woods created playbooks full of AI agents to replace additional hires at her company.

Courtesy of Rachel Woods

  • Rachel Woods taught herself AI while studying business in college, later landing a role at Facebook.
  • She left to start her own business, which helps people integrate AI into their everyday workflows.
  • She said the company has saved itself at least 4 new hires by using AI agents and playbooks.

This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Rachel Woods, CEO and cofounder of AMP. The following has been edited for length and clarity.

My first official role in AI was at Facebook. I started as an intern and then joined full-time in 2018. I worked on AI research and development for the ads system that's powered by AI.

I don't have an engineering degree or background; I studied management information systems at The University of Texas at Austin. Part of why I think I got my role at Facebook was because I spent time building my own side projects and teaching myself how to train AI models and do data analysis.

I left Facebook in 2020 to start my own business in the wine industry, and after that company sold, I founded an AI company. I would need to double the number of people on my team if we didn't use AI playbooks.

If you're being thoughtful about setting up your AI operations, it's no different than building a team that you can trust.

That's something a lot of people are missing: how to actually make these systems things you can take your eyes off and not keep yourself up at night in the process.

Business Insider wants to talk to founders of AI-powered companies with fewer than 10 workers, and employees working alongside AI agents and bots, to understand what the "Tiny Teams" era really feels like — the wins, the fears, and the human skills that stand out. I've been an entrepreneur since the age of 15

My first business was a photography business, and I was too young to even drive to meet my clients. I would always catch rides with people, and my parents hated this.

I got into tech because in college, I was exposed to colleagues doing data analytics and working with coding and AI at summer internships. That led me to look online and try to take related classes, and I started to see that if you break a business problem into small enough pieces, you can get AI to handle parts of it: predicting, sorting, generating.

Later, when I was applying to Facebook, I used AI and data analysis to build a report of coffee shops in Austin that were less likely to be too busy for me to study at. I think that project helped me land my role.

After I left Facebook, I started a wine business called Vinebase in 2020; we built a tech platform that used AI to help winemakers sell their wine online and manage the digital side of their business.

Because this was before AI became more mainstream, we didn't market it as AI. We called it our "auto magic" system. I think that if we had said it was AI, it could've been a turnoff for our customers. Vinebase was acquired by Corksy in fall 2022.

I use my playbooks and AI agents to replace additional staff

My sister and I started AMP, originally called The AI Exchange, before ChatGPT was released in November 2022, because I was using AI in Vinebase and saw how the technology was progressing. ChatGPT came out about a month later and started really accelerating the AI timeline, so we went all in.

We have four full-time employees and four part-time contractors right now, but we'd need double if we weren't running as much as we are on AI. Given the company's current size, we'd need at least four to five additional people, including a sales and marketing manager, customer support specialist, executive assistant, and operations coordinator.

The way we structure these systems is based on something we call playbooking. Every department in the company has its own system. Each system is a collection of playbooks with AI agents that can run independently or together.

How we build our AI playbooks

To build an AI playbook, pick one piece of work you do over and over, and write down what starts it, what goes into the task, and what should come out at the end. Next, write the steps the way you'd train a new hire: each step, the decisions in it, and what "good" looks like.

Start running it with AI, and while you watch, compare the result to how you would've done it. Then, fix the playbook, not the output, until you trust it to run without you.

I always think through how I want a playbook to work and outline it by hand. Whenever you're making an AI playbook, you want to own the thinking and the logic. Otherwise, you can fall into the trap of including something that you don't really understand, and then you're not going to be able to trust that system anyway.

We have an AI agent running our sales playbook to review customer calls and ensure they meet our standards, rather than having a sales manager review them manually. Also, instead of sales team members needing to ask their manager questions, they just ask the playbook, which then pulls the answers from our guide for that team.

There are two types of AI costs we have in the business. The first is the subscriptions to Claude and ChatGPT for every person on the team. The other is because the majority of our operations are these AI playbooks that are running in the background without us being the ones starting or triggering it. In September, we spent $8,510 on Claude Code to run our playbooks in the background, which is billed by usage and is separate from the team's monthly Claude and ChatGPT subscription costs.

Own the playbook, rent the tech

We say "own the playbook, rent the tech," which means that if you are creating really good playbooks for your AI to follow, it actually doesn't matter which tool you're running it in.

Right now, we have a setup that's running with Claude Code in the background. But I could run all of these playbooks in ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, or Microsoft Copilot.

We try to avoid a lot of the risks that people are concerned about with AI going rogue by creating these detailed outlines. We don't just want the agents to run on vibes. There still needs to be a person to decide and design how the work gets done; AI just speeds up everything else. That's the job security we have in this AI era.

Start small, and get everyone involved

A few of us on the team who are more advanced with AI own the bigger, team-wide playbooks that the business depends on, but everyone playbooks their own personal workflow, and then we use that as the testing ground to graduate things into becoming more part of official systems.

My advice would be to just start with something small and really easy, because once you get your first win and see how AI can do that repetitive work for you, it's kind of like a snowball.

The latest update on the grocery wars is here, with Walmart and Costco gaining ground

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
walmart

Scott Olson/Getty Images

  • Value-focused retailers are picking up market share and traditional chains are sliding.
  • Households are hunting for grocery deals to offset rising costs of other bills.
  • Fuel pressures may drive more inflation, but Walmart and Costco are typically slow to raise prices.

The grocery wars continue to heat up.

As US households continue to grapple with rising costs for gas, housing, and insurance, they're increasingly looking for savings on groceries.

That's giving value-focused retailers like Walmart and Costco a major leg up over more traditional grocery giants like Kroger and Albertsons.

Data released this week from consumer analytics firm Numerator showed warehouse giant Costco overtook Kroger's dollar share of US retail food and beverage sales. Costco has steadily gained share over the last two years as both Kroger and Albertsons shrank their positions.

The estimates are based on the last 12 months of sales, and show Costco's share ticking up to 8.4%, a half percentage point higher than the same period a year earlier.

And even though Walmart has long held the largest share of grocery spending, it managed to gain 0.2 percentage points to capture 20.3% of sales in the period.

By comparison, Kroger's share fell by 0.2 percentage points to 8.2% while Albertsons saw its share slide by 0.1 percentage points to 4.5%.

The shifting grocery landscape comes at a time when US shoppers are facing fresh inflation increases from last year's trade war and this year's conflict with Iran.

Costco has also managed to keep prices steady this year, but the wholesale club's leaders say they are closely watching the latest cost pressures.

Groceries haven't been significantly affected by rising diesel and transportation costs just yet, but a recent note from Deutsche Bank said Walmart is bracing for an impact that would outweigh the benefit the company received in tariff refunds this year. Walmart initially planned to invest some of those refunds in price cuts.

Deutsche Bank also said that when Walmart raises prices, its competitors do too, and Walmart typically remains the lower-priced option.

Traditional grocers aren't sitting quietly as Walmart and Costco pull ahead, though.

Kroger has been taking a page — and a CEO — from Walmart's book this year, adding Greg Foran, who formerly led Walmart's US business.

Foran's big focus this year is on lowering prices for consumers and tightening operations to make that happen. It's a $5 billion effort that includes significant cuts to the corporate workforce over the next few years and ending contracts with suppliers that aren't delivering a good enough deal.

And Albertsons said it is making several significant adjustments to its employee benefits package to manage its business in the face of rising healthcare costs.

Whether it's Walmart and Costco or Kroger and Albertsons it increasingly looks like the path to gain share in this market includes a sharper focus on value.

Apollo is sharing its private-equity playbook with nonprofits

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
The Apollo Global Management LLC office.

Bloomberg/Getty Images

  • Apollo Global Management gives portfolio companies operational guidance and leadership training.
  • It also provides funding and operational support to 28 nonprofits, including a biennial training summit.
  • The summit included discussions about the labor market and advice on making high-stakes decisions.

Keeping up with AI and an uneven labor market is challenging enough for corporate leaders. For nonprofit executives working with tighter budgets and leaner teams, it can be extra daunting.

"It's tricky for everyone right now, but for those guys, it's actually very tricky," Scott Kleinman, co-president of Apollo Asset Management and chair of the Apollo Opportunity Foundation Board.

The foundation is addressing the challenges nonprofits face by providing 28 organizations with a version of the playbook Apollo uses with its over 190 portfolio companies, which include Yahoo Inc. and Shutterfly.

That includes access to experts, leadership training, and a built-in network. The foundation also provides funding based on factors such as the organization's budget size and impact metrics. Apollo has so far provided over $13 million to grantees and committed to $100 million in funding over the next decade.

The foundation, which launched in 2022, offered Business Insider an exclusive look at its biennial summit in October, where grantee leaders mingled with Apollo executives and sat in on panels led by subject-matter experts.

Applying its portfolio playbook

Kleinman said the foundation provides the same type of support it provides its portfolio companies, including operational guidance, data and digital transformation, talent management, and recruitment.

"That is the crux of our foundation. Of course, it involves capital." Kleinman said, adding that the "real-value add" is the deal team.

Each nonprofit is paired with a deal team of five to 10 employees from Apollo or Athene, which merged with Apollo and provides retirement services. Employees build relationships with the grantees to perform pro-bono projects, source board opportunities, and provide strategic advice.

The foundation also holds monthly virtual training sessions to discuss topics like AI, leadership transitions, culture, real estate, fundraising, and succession planning.

Beyond the funding

At the October leadership summit, Apollo's chief economist Torsten Sløk discussed the labor market and how organizations can adapt to it, while the firm's chief financial officer talked about the factors nonprofits should consider when growing their organizations. The summit also included working sessions for grantees and their Apollo deal teams.

The support Apollo offers grantees can be anything from fundraising skill-building to succession planning, said Lauren Coape-Arnold, Apollo Global Management's global head of citizenship and executive director of the Apollo Opportunity Foundation.

She said the partnership starts with reorganizing boards to ensure strong governance, because an organization's success ultimately comes down to strong leadership. That may include sourcing candidates to consider for the board of directors or advisory board members.

"Who is the leader? Do you believe in the leader? Are they someone inspiring the team to move forward?" Coape-Arnold said.

What nonprofits need

Jane Veron, cofounder and CEO of Acceleration Project, a nonprofit that helps small business owners said that support included identifying 35 processes the organization could eliminate or modify, from onboarding and technology to client data capture.

For example, instead of having her team read through notes on a client's business challenges, Apollo is helping build an AI tool to do that work. It's also building asynchronous training tools to streamline consultant onboarding.

"Apollo helped us identify priorities," Veron said, adding that the list includes "very micro and tactical" fixes.

Reuben Ogbonna II, the cofounder and executive director of The Marcy Lab School, a one-year program that is an alternative to four-year college, said that meant revamping his school's curriculum to reflect the shifting roles of software engineers.

He said Apollo's deal team helped them recognize that technologists need to work more closely with business leaders and integrated more business and industry context into the curriculum. The program also included additional cloud fundamentals and foundational DevOps knowledge, based on the team's input, he said.

That sort of hands-on support is the goal, said Kleinman. "Not just to write some checks and get some photo ops, but to really make this a two-way street with our employees."
