How Pures 24-hour chat timer forces daters to skip the endless banter

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

One of the major annoyances of online dating is how often matches devolve into pen pals rather than translate into IRL dates. Those initial, flirty conversations are great, don't get us wrong, but most of us aren't jumping onto Tinder or Hinge just to endlessly text. And yet, in modern dating, where both people often feel afraid to make the first move and take the match into the real world, endless texting is all too often the only result of a once-promising match. 

But it doesn't have to be this way. Dating app Pure concocted some clever solutions to the never-ending chatting, and in our own experimentation, they work like a charm. 

Here's how Pure introduced a sense of urgency into its dating mechanics, and how that helps users translate matches into real-life encounters. 

Opens in a new window Credit: Pure Pure   Learn More

How Pure leverages a sense of urgency to benefit its users

One unique feature of Pure is that it doesn't use traditional dating profiles, which means its matches operate differently than on basically every other dating app. 

Instead of having a permanent, public-facing profile, Pure users can post "ads," temporary personal advertisements shouting out who they are or what they want, whether that's for a long-term relationship or a short-term connection. It's only when someone has posted an ad that they're discoverable to other users, and only then are your basic profile elements (age, gender, sexual orientation, and photos) visible. Once your ad expires, so does access to your profile.

The result of this mechanic is a sense of urgency, the very real possibility that you're seeing someone you may never see again, so if you want a connection, you have to act fast. That's a very welcome change from the dating app norm, where profiles are always visible, and you can browse them at your convenience, whether on your morning commute or between sets at the gym.

But Pure takes things a step further with how you actually interact with other people. 

The disappearing chat mechanic

From the moment you initiate a chat on Pure, you're under an artificial time constraint, since every chat automatically self-deletes after 24 hours, unless both parties agree to suspend the chat timer. 

While at first this seems like an unnecessary constraint, in practice it's actually very healthy and helps daters avoid the endless banter that plagues so many online dating interactions. 

Think about what would happen if you approached a cute stranger in the real world. You may see them at a bar or coffee shop, going about their day, but no matter how smooth your introduction, you know in the back of your mind that you can't monopolize this person's time and attention for very long. 

For an in-person approach, you have to be concise in your communication: I think you're cute and want a chance to get to know you better — would that interest you? But on an app, where there's no sense of urgency, you can endlessly waffle, delaying the most important question of all in favor of a never-ending series of mundane questions. 

This is why Pure's disappearing chat mechanic is so clever, even when both parties agree to disable it, since even that act forces a mutual acknowledgment of the connection. And after using the app over an extended period, we can confirm that Pure's culture really encourages in-person interactions at the expense of endless chatting or swiping.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

SEE ALSO: 'Copperfield! The New Musical' review: A rollicking, big-hearted Dickens adaptation that is not to be missed

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

SEE ALSO: 'Memoirs of Amorous Gentlemen' review: Duncan Sheik's musical adaptation gets kink right, but falters elsewhere

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button is now playing at the Public Theater until Dec. 20.

AI Is Changing the Junior Developer Career Path. Here's How CS Students Can Adapt

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ten years ago, the standard advice to a computer science student was simple: learn a language, build projects, collect a few frameworks, and apply for junior roles. Junior roles were where people got paid to learn the craft.

AI coding agents are putting that ladder under pressure. Today's tools don't just autocomplete a line. They read a whole repository, edit several files, run commands, and retry when tests fail. The routine work that used to fill a junior developer's first year is exactly the work these tools handle best.

I'm a computer science student, and I feel this shift every time I open an AI coding tool. It can scaffold a feature in minutes that would have taken me an afternoon. But I've also caught myself accepting code I couldn't explain, and then getting stuck when it broke. That gap between "it runs" and "I understand why it runs" is what pushed me to dig into the research, and to change how I study.

This article looks at what the evidence says and what a student can do about it.

What the Data Says About Early-Career Developers

Researchers at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab analyzed payroll records from ADP, the largest US payroll provider. They found that workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations saw a 16 percent relative decline in employment, even after controlling for firm-level shocks. Employment for more experienced workers in the same occupations stayed stable or kept growing. Software developers were one of the example occupations the paper highlights.

Two details matter for students. First, the researchers found that adjustment happens mainly through employment rather than pay, so the problem is fewer openings, not lower salaries. Second, the declines were concentrated in occupations where AI is more likely to automate work than to augment it. A summary from ADP Research walks through the same findings in plain language.

Read that carefully: it doesn't say programming is over. It says that tasks that are easy to specify and easy to automate are the ones disappearing from entry-level hiring. Your goal is to be useful at everything around those tasks.

The Trap: Shipping Before Understanding

Here is the uncomfortable part. The tools that make you fast can also stop you from learning.

In a randomized controlled trial with 52 software engineers learning an unfamiliar Python async library, the AI-assisted group scored about 50% on a follow-up quiz, versus about 67% for the group that coded by hand. The biggest gap was on debugging questions. The AI group finished only about two minutes sooner, a difference that was not statistically significant.

The outcome also depended on how people used the assistant, not just whether they used it. People who asked for explanations and worked through the reasoning retained more than people who handed over the whole task.

The sample is small and the task was narrow, so treat it as a warning sign rather than a law. But it matches what many learners feel: you can complete an assignment without being able to redo it tomorrow.

The Second Trap: Code That Works but Isn't Safe

A working feature and a safe feature are different things. Veracode tested more than 100 language models on 80 coding tasks. The models produced functional code but introduced security vulnerabilities in 45 percent of cases. Java was the worst, with failure rates above 70 percent, and models failed to defend against cross-site scripting in 86 percent of relevant cases. Help Net Security's coverage summarizes the details.

The finding worth remembering: Veracode found that larger models did not perform meaningfully better than smaller ones, which points to a systemic issue rather than something scale will fix. The AI will happily write a login system. It won't reliably tell you what's wrong with it. That judgment has to come from you.

Where You Can Still Stand Out

If generating routine code gets cheap, these skills gain value:

  • Reading code critically. Reviewing a diff you didn't write is now a daily job. Practice by reading open-source pull requests.
  • Debugging from first principles. Form a hypothesis, find evidence, narrow the cause. This is exactly the skill the trial above found weakest in heavy AI users.
  • Security basics. Know the OWASP Top 10 well enough to spot injection, broken access control, and bad session handling by eye.
  • Turning vague problems into clear specs. "Users leave at checkout" isn't a ticket. Someone has to make it one.
  • Testing. Write tests that try to break the AI's code, not tests that confirm it.
  • Explaining your decisions. In interviews and code reviews, "why does this work?" is the question that separates understanding from copying.

A Learning Routine That Keeps You Honest

Try a simple loop I call Sketch, Delegate, Break, Rebuild:

  1. Sketch. Before opening any AI tool, write your own plan on paper: routes, data model, who can access what, what could go wrong.
  2. Delegate. Ask the AI for an implementation, and ask it to explain its choices and list edge cases.
  3. Break. Try to make it fail. Send bad input, log in as the wrong user, delete a required field.
  4. Rebuild. Close the chat and re-implement one important piece from memory.

Steps 3 and 4 feel slower, and that's the point. They are where the learning the trial measured actually happens.

What to Build Now

Portfolio projects that show only "I generated an app" are getting less convincing. Stronger signals include:

  • a project with a written design note explaining your decisions
  • a test suite you wrote to challenge generated code
  • a short security review of your own app
  • a commit history that shows you debugging real problems

Employers and clients can't easily fake-check understanding, so show it openly.

The Bottom Line

AI won't remove the need for people who understand software, but it is raising the bar for what an entry-level person must bring. Use the tools heavily for speed, exploration, and drafts. Just make sure the understanding stays in your head, not only in the chat history. The students who do both will be the ones who look like low-risk hires when the ladder gets narrower.

Turn Customer Questions Into a Marketing Research System

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hero image: Original AI-assisted illustration created for this article.

A sales team answers the same implementation question in several calls. Support explains a related limitation in tickets. Marketing publishes another broad trend article because the repeated question never becomes a research brief.

The organization already has useful signals. What it lacks is a process for turning them into public knowledge without exposing private information, mistaking a loud customer for the whole market, or allowing an AI summary to erase the original concern.

Disclosure: I am the founder and CEO of INSIDEA, an Elite HubSpot Partner. This article reflects practical experience and independent research. It is not sponsored by HubSpot or any other company mentioned.

I would build a question-to-research workflow with separate collection, protection, clustering, and validation stages. The model below is a proposed editorial system. It does not require copying all customer communications into a model, and no private customer material was accessed to develop the examples.

Collect questions for a defined purpose

Begin with an approved research purpose: identify recurring uncertainties that useful public content could address. That is narrower than collecting everything customers say or using private conversations as an unrestricted source of marketing material.

Select appropriate inputs through the organization's access and information-handling process. A reviewed question submitted by a salesperson can be enough to start. A full call recording or ticket export may contain far more information than the editorial task needs.

Define what should not enter the research queue: credentials, personal details, account-specific secrets, confidential commercial terms, and material whose permitted use is unclear. When the useful question can be separated from private context, retain the general question rather than the entire interaction. When it cannot, hold it for appropriate review.

Preserve the question before summarizing it

A summary can make a specific concern look generic. A buyer asking how reporting history survives a CRM migration is not simply asking for a migration guide. A support question about an unexpected billing association is not merely interest in automation.

Retain an approved, appropriately protected version of the question and a separate editorial interpretation. Record the context needed to understand it: the decision being made, the audience role, the relevant constraints, and the source type. Avoid retaining identifiable detail that does not serve the purpose.

The W3C provenance overview offers a general foundation for tracing origins and transformations. A question record inspired by that idea should explain where the editorial interpretation came from, without exposing restricted source material to everyone who reads the content backlog.

Separate frequency from importance

Repeated questions can reveal a content gap, but repetition is not the same as market prevalence. A large customer, a busy account team, or one support incident can dominate the available record. A rare question can also concern a high-consequence decision.

Track counts with their denominator and collection window where feasible. Distinguish repeated messages from distinct people, accounts, and underlying problems. A thread with ten replies should not automatically become ten independent expressions of demand.

Add consequence and decision relevance as separate review dimensions. A question that affects security, migration integrity, or contractual understanding may deserve careful explanation even when it appears infrequently. The research agenda should combine audience need and consequence rather than rank topics solely by mention count.

Original AI-assisted illustration created for this article.

Cluster by uncertainty, not merely vocabulary

Two questions can use similar words while requiring different answers. How do we automate a handoff and how do we prove a handoff completed are not the same problem. Conversely, different terminology can describe the same underlying uncertainty.

Ask the clustering process to identify the decision, missing knowledge, relevant constraints, and expected answer type. Use AI to propose groupings, then have a domain reviewer inspect them. Keep the original approved questions available for that review so a tidy label does not hide a false merge.

Allow a question to belong to more than one research category when that reflects the problem. A migration concern may involve identity, reporting, and change management. Forced single-label classification can create artificial boundaries that make the resulting content less useful to the person who asked.

Turn clusters into research briefs

A useful brief states the audience, the decision, what existing material fails to explain, the evidence needed, the proposed contribution, and the limitations. It should also state what the article will not attempt to answer.

For a hypothetical reporting-history question, the brief might compare migration approaches, explain what needs testing, and provide an acceptance checklist. It should not promise to resolve every account's data architecture or imply that a generic import procedure preserves every historical relationship.

Google's people-first content guidance gives a usefulness-oriented editorial reference. A question-derived brief still needs original analysis and suitable evidence. The existence of a customer question does not establish the correctness of the answer the team wants to publish.

Research independently of the source conversation

A customer or salesperson may describe a product capability inaccurately. An internal anecdote may omit conditions that change the conclusion. Treat the question as a reason to investigate, not as proof of the proposed answer.

Verify product claims through current primary documentation and clearly label account-specific behavior. If the public evidence is insufficient, narrow the article or state the uncertainty. Do not imply firsthand testing when the work is documentary analysis.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a general risk-management perspective. Applying review to generated summaries and research claims is a local proposal, not a certification. The team should record what it verified and where a qualified specialist or account-specific test remains necessary.

Protect people when converting examples

Removing a name is not always sufficient to make a story anonymous. A distinctive industry, timeline, system combination, or numerical result may identify a customer to people familiar with the situation. Review the whole example, not only the obvious identifiers.

Where permission is absent, write a synthetic scenario that illustrates the mechanism and label it explicitly. Do not combine real customer details into an invented case study and present it as practical experience. A useful hypothetical example does not need an implied client pedigree.

Keep original evidence restricted to its appropriate audience. Public drafts should contain only approved material. If a writer needs to verify a sensitive detail, route the question to the owner rather than expanding access to the entire source conversation for convenience.

Keep AI access narrower than editorial ambition

HubSpot's AI-settings guide describes feature access and data-source controls. A setting that permits AI use does not settle whether a particular conversation should be used for public research.

Start with an approved, minimized input set. Test the workflow using synthetic questions before adding real material. Check whether summaries retain unnecessary identifiers, whether clusters reveal restricted details, and whether generated briefs invent frequency or customer outcomes.

Do not change CRM access, model-training settings, or data-sharing permissions merely to make topic generation easier. Those are separate governance decisions. If the research workflow needs broader access, identify the missing requirement and obtain the appropriate review rather than treating it as a normal editorial shortcut.

Prioritize by usefulness and ability to answer

A topic can be commercially attractive and still be a poor choice if the team lacks evidence to answer it well. Use a review matrix that separates audience relevance, decision consequence, available evidence, originality, and maintenance burden.

Keep those judgments explainable. An arbitrary weighted score can make subjective assumptions look objective. A short written rationale often tells the editorial team more than a decimal ranking nobody can defend.

Prioritize questions where the organization can add a distinct contribution: a clear explanation of a confusing boundary, a practical test, a comparison of tradeoffs, or a reusable worksheet. Avoid turning every cluster into another broad introduction. Several questions may be answered better by improving one existing resource than by publishing several overlapping articles.

Return the answer to the teams that supplied the question

Public publishing is only one destination. Sales and support teams should be able to find the approved explanation, understand its limits, and use it where appropriate. A content program loses value when useful answers remain buried in a marketing calendar.

Provide a short internal summary of the resource's purpose, suitable audience, date checked, and situations requiring specialist review. Do not instruct teams to send the link automatically to every contact who uses a matching word. Context and relationship still matter.

Collect feedback on whether the answer resolves the actual concern. A salesperson may find that buyers still need a decision table. Support may identify an omitted edge case. Treat that as evidence for revision, while verifying any new factual claim before it enters the public resource.

Measure resolved uncertainty, not just topic volume

Useful measures include briefs accepted for research, questions answered by existing content, recurring misunderstandings after publication, verified reuse by internal teams, and qualified conversations where the resource helped clarify scope. Define the evidence for each measure.

Do not claim that every reduction in support questions was caused by the article. Product changes, staffing, customer mix, and seasonality may also matter. Where the relationship cannot be established, report the observation and its limitations.

The deeper objective is a learning system: customer uncertainty informs research, research produces an inspectable answer, and subsequent questions test whether the explanation was sufficient. AI can help organize the material. Human judgment must still decide what deserves publication and what the evidence can support.

Build a small loop before automating collection

Begin with one approved source team, a narrow problem area, and a small set of protected questions. Create a few research briefs, verify the answers, and test whether the resulting resources are useful. Only then consider expanding collection or automation.

This avoids creating a large sensitive archive before proving the editorial workflow. It also lets the team refine the distinction between a question worth answering publicly and one that belongs in a private customer conversation.

The future of AI marketing is not limited to generating more content from public trends. A business can use AI to organize what its audience is struggling to understand, then invest in answers that deserve attention. The value comes from the quality of that learning loop, not the size of the question database.

Matt Robertson wins record $1.25 million fishing prize, declares 'trailer trash for life'

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

NASHVILLE — Matt Robertson just became $1.25 million richer. But don't expect the Kentucky bass fishing pro to pack up and move into a mansion anytime soon.

"We ain't moving out the trailer, baby. Trailer trash for life!" Robertson declared during a Saturday evening news conference after winning World Bass Enterprises' inaugural The Champions tournament on Old Hickory Lake in Tennessee.

The Bassmaster Elite pro backed up a week's worth of trash talk by bringing 20 pounds, 2 ounces of bass to the scales on Championship Saturday, claiming the largest first-place prize in professional bass fishing history.

And despite all that confidence, even Robertson seemed a little overwhelmed by the moment.

"Dude, it's freaking insane. I don't think it's really hit me yet," he said.

Robertson, of Kuttawa, Kentucky, dominated the first two days of competition, then delivered another monster bag Saturday after the weights reset to zero. Pake South finished second with 13 pounds, 7 ounces, taking home $500,000, while Wesley Strader earned $300,000 for third place.

The historic tournament brought together top anglers from the Bassmaster Elite Series and Major League Fishing's Bass Pro Tour, along with two amateur qualifiers, to compete for a record $3.25 million purse.

But for Robertson, the payday is about much more than the money.

A millionaire who's staying in his trailer

Robertson might not be upgrading his own home, but he already knows where some of that $1.25 million is going.

His wife is getting a new vehicle. And he's planning to buy a couple more trailers.

Before the final weigh-in, Robertson told OutKick OutDoors that he'd been eyeing two trailers for sale near his home, one priced around $60,000 and the other around $80,000.

"I'm for sure buying a couple more trailers," he said. "They're bought, and my wife, she gets her new ride."

MATT ROBERTSON HAULS IN 20-POUND BAG TO LEAD THE CHAMPIONS AFTER DAY 1 ON OLD HICKORY LAKE

And after securing the championship, he doubled down on those plans.

"I hate to tell anybody, but them things are sold," he joked.

Behind Robertson's larger-than-life personality, however, is a man who hasn't forgotten where he came from.

"I didn't grow up with much," he explained during his post-victory press conference. "I worked so much paycheck to paycheck ... I went through real struggles in life."

Those experiences, he said, helped prepare him for the pressure of competing for life-changing money.

Robertson admitted that the money will drastically change his life, but he insisted he wasn't particularly nervous about the stakes.

In fact, when OutKick OutDoors asked him what winning would mean before the results were officially announced, he had something else on his mind.

"I want that damn trophy. It's what I want," he said. "Everybody talking about the 1.25. Like, I want the money too, but I want to get my hands on that trophy."

A honey hole, a navigation lock and an unlikely friendship

While Robertson's victory might have looked easy from the leaderboard, it took some unconventional strategy to get there.

Rather than fishing the main lake, he traveled through the navigation lock at Old Hickory Dam to target smallmouth bass in the tailwater below the dam.

Robertson discovered his productive fishing area during practice and made the gamble that the bigger fish would be there when it mattered.

"If I hadn't found that spot, I wouldn't have won this thing," Robertson said.

The strategy certainly paid off. Robertson caught the tournament's only 20-pound bag on Thursday, then added another strong day Friday to secure the top qualifying position for Saturday's championship.

But his honey hole wasn't exactly a secret.

MLF pro Ron Nelson had also discovered productive water below the dam, and the two anglers spent much of the tournament fishing in close proximity.

The two were among just a handful of competitors who ventured below the dam. In fact, Nelson deliberately avoided casting into the area where Robertson had been catching his fish, despite the enormous money at stake.

And Robertson noticed.

"Never knew Ron before this week. Got to know him real well, and dude, there is not another person in the world with as much integrity as him," Robertson told OutKick OutDoors during Saturday's weigh-in.

"Anybody else fishing for this kind of money, he saw where I was catching a lot of fish, and he never came in on me."

As water levels and currents shifted, the two eventually ended up sharing the productive area.

But tournament rules prohibited them from discussing how many fish they had caught, creating an unusual dilemma.

Robertson said he had no idea Nelson was struggling to fill his limit and felt terrible afterward.

"If I'd knew Ron only had two ... I wouldn't even have pulled up down there," he said. "I feel awful about it."

Robertson had been hoping they could both walk away with enormous paydays.

"I was wanting us to win $1.75 million from down there," he said.

Nelson, who ultimately finished sixth and earned $75,000, had equally kind words for his new friend.

"Matt's a heck of a dude," Nelson told OutKick OutDoors. "I don't know him, but I got a new friend now, I guess."

Nelson explained that changing currents had made it difficult to relocate the school of smallmouth he'd found the previous day. And he believed Robertson would have given him more room had he known how badly Nelson needed a few additional bites.

"If he knows I'd needed a couple bites, he'd have probably slid over," Nelson said.

The mutual respect was especially fitting for a tournament that brought together competitors from rival professional fishing circuits.

Robertson and Nelson had never met before The Champions. Three days later, they'd walked away with substantial paychecks and a new friendship.

'The Super Bowl of bass fishing'

Robertson was also quick to credit his faith and a little help from the lock operators for his historic victory.

He recalled learning that the navigation lock was undergoing maintenance early in the tournament, threatening to keep him from reaching the area where he'd found his fish.

Knowing how much his strategy depended on getting through, he said he prayed for the lock to reopen.

When he checked his phone, he discovered the lockmaster had already tried calling him twice. The lock was open, and the operators were waiting for him.

"From that moment, I was stressing before that," Robertson recalled. "And after that, I just ... gave it all to Him and let it go."

Once on the water, Robertson relied on his familiarity with the Tennessee River and its navigation locks to adjust to changing conditions. He caught his winning fish using a small swimbait on a jighead.

The victory capped a tournament that also saw Bassmaster's Team Stars defeat MLF's Team Stripes for the team championship, giving the inaugural event both individual and team bragging rights.

Robertson had nothing but praise for WBE and CEO Brian Bird, saying the organizers had paid attention to both the big details and the small ones.

"This is the Super Bowl of bass fishing. I don't care what anybody says," Robertson declared.

And while he can now claim the sport's richest championship prize, Robertson says he doesn't plan to change who he is.

A new vehicle for his wife? Absolutely. A couple more trailers? Already on the shopping list.

But moving out of his own? Not a chance.
