This Year Has Fifty Three Weeks and Last Time Our Reports Lost One of Them

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Most of our reporting is by week. Depots are measured on weekly sales, buyers forecast in weeks, and the board pack compares each week with the same week last year. Under the standard week numbering that most of our systems use, a year has fifty two weeks, except now and then, when it has fifty three. This year is one of those. Week fifty three of 2026 starts on Monday the twenty eighth of December.

The last such year was 2020, and we found out the hard way. Our sales data warehouse had a table of weeks built in 2015, with fifty two rows for every year, and the overnight load looked up the week for each sale in that table. For the last week of 2020 it found nothing, and it quietly discarded the rows. Five trading days, about nine million pounds of sales, were missing from the warehouse for three weeks.

The board saw December down eleven percent on the year before. Two depots were questioned about a collapse in sales that had not happened. When we found the cause and reloaded the week, the comparisons were still wrong, because the warehouse system and our trading calendar disagreed about which week was the first of 2021, and every year on year figure for January was compared with the wrong week.

None of our systems were faulty in their own terms. Each one had been given a definition of a week by whoever built it and had applied it faithfully. There were simply several definitions.

This summer we listed every system that numbers weeks. There are eleven. Four did not handle a fifty third week, and two use a different rule for where the year starts. Finance, the buyers and IT have now agreed one trading calendar, held in one table and read by all eleven systems instead of their own copies. We loaded a test year with fifty three weeks into each of them in September, and the warehouse load now stops and raises an alert when it meets a date it cannot place, instead of dropping it.

The fault we had in 2020 will come back every five or six years, and the people who remember it will not always be here.

A calendar is reference data like any other, and we had let every system write its own.

– Serguey Shinder

How to build a mortgage calculator in React

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every real estate site I have built for a client ended up with the same request about two weeks after launch: "Can we show buyers what the monthly payment would be?" The first time, I wrote the calculator from scratch. The second time, I copied it and fixed the bugs I had shipped the first time. By the fourth site I packaged it as the Mortgage calculator in Facade. This article walks through how it works, so you can build your own or install mine and know what is inside.

The payment formula, explained once

A fixed rate mortgage payment is one formula. Everything else in the component is presentation.

With a loan amount P, a monthly interest rate r (the annual rate divided by 12 and by 100) and a number of monthly payments n (years times 12), the monthly principal and interest payment is:

M = P * r * (1 + r)^n / ((1 + r)^n - 1)

Two edge cases matter in code. When the rate is zero, the formula divides by zero, so you return P / n instead. When the down payment reaches 100 percent, the loan amount is zero and the payment is zero. Both happen the moment a visitor drags a slider to the end, so handle them before you touch any animation.

In TypeScript:

export function monthlyPayment(loan: number, annualRate: number, years: number) { const n = years * 12; if (loan <= 0) return 0; if (annualRate === 0) return loan / n; const r = annualRate / 100 / 12; const f = Math.pow(1 + r, n); return (loan * r * f) / (f - 1); }

Principal and interest are only part of what a buyer pays each month. US buyers expect property tax, homeowners insurance and, for condos, HOA dues on the same screen. Tax is an annual percentage of the home price divided by 12. Insurance is an annual dollar amount divided by 12. HOA is already monthly. The total is the sum of the four, and that total is the number the visitor remembers.

Controlled inputs with sliders

The calculator has four inputs: home price, down payment, interest rate and loan term. The first three are range inputs with the formatted value printed next to the label, so a buyer always sees the exact number the slider is on. If you add a text input next to each slider, make both controls write to the same piece of state, which keeps them in sync without effects.

Down payment is the input that trips people up. Buyers think in percent, lenders think in dollars, and the slider has to show both. Store the percent, derive the dollar amount, and clamp the percent between 0 and 50 so the slider stays useful. A 90 percent down payment is technically valid and practically never happens.

A few details that make the inputs feel right:

  • Format the displayed values with Intl.NumberFormat, not with string concatenation. $1,250,000 reads, $1250000 does not.
  • Pick a sensible step for the price slider, a few thousand dollars at least. Pixel precision on a 2 million dollar range is useless.
  • Label every range with a visible <label> and an aria-valuetext that reads the formatted value. Screen readers announce "650,000 dollars", not "650000".

The 15 or 30 year term toggle

Most calculators put the term in a select. A segmented toggle with two options is faster and shows both choices at once. In Facade it is a small two button group that moves a sliding pill behind the active option, and the payment re-renders the moment it changes. Buyers click it back and forth to feel the difference, which is exactly what you want them doing on a listing page.

Under the hood it is a role="radiogroup" with two role="radio" buttons, arrow keys to switch, and the defaultTerm prop set to 15 or 30. Nothing fancy, but the arrow keys matter: a toggle that only works with a mouse is a bug.

A donut breakdown with motion

A single total hides where the money goes. The donut shows four segments: principal and interest, tax, insurance and HOA. Each segment is an SVG circle with a strokeDasharray for its share and a strokeDashoffset to position it after the previous segment.

Animating the segments is where motion/react earns its place. Each circle's strokeDasharray and strokeDashoffset are animated when the values change, so dragging the price slider makes the segments breathe instead of jumping. The total in the middle of the donut uses the same count-up logic as the free Animated counter: it tweens from the previous value to the new one over a few hundred milliseconds, formatted as currency on every frame.

Two rules I follow for this kind of animation:

  • Animate on change, not on mount only. The first render can animate in, but the interesting moment is the slider drag.
  • Respect prefers-reduced-motion. useReducedMotion() from motion returns true for those visitors, and the component then sets values directly instead of springing to them.

Install it with the shadcn CLI

The Mortgage calculator is a Pro item, so it installs from the private registry once your license key is in components.json (the installation docs show the one time setup).

npx shadcn@latest add @facade/mortgage-calculator

Then drop it on a listing page with the listing's price as the default and your market's tax rate:

import { MortgageCalculator, type MortgageBreakdown } from "@/components/facade/mortgage-calculator"; export function ListingPayment({ price }: { price: number }) { return ( <MortgageCalculator defaultPrice={price} defaultDownPercent={20} defaultRate={6.5} defaultTerm={30} propertyTaxRate={1.1} annualInsurance={1800} monthlyHoa={0} title="Estimate your monthly payment" onChange={(result: MortgageBreakdown) => { // result.total, result.principalAndInterest, result.propertyTax, result.insurance, result.hoa, result.loanAmount }} /> ); }

The onChange callback hands you the full breakdown on every change. I use it to update a "Payment from $X per month" line in the sticky header of the listing page, and some agents use it to prefill a pre-approval request.

If you only need the count-up number without the calculator, the Animated counter is free and MIT licensed:

npx shadcn@latest add https://facadeui.com/r/animated-counter.json

Where it belongs on a real estate site

On a listing page, the calculator goes directly under the key facts, before the photo gallery ends and the agent card appears. Buyers scroll to the price, then to the payment, then decide whether to keep reading. On a brokerage home page it earns less, because there is no price to anchor it to. The block order I use for agent sites is on the real estate agent page.

One thing I do not do: pull live rates from an API. Rates change daily, the calculator becomes a compliance question, and buyers understand that 6.5 percent is an example. Set a sensible default, let them change it, and put a one line disclaimer under the donut.

FAQ

Does the calculator include PMI?

No. Private mortgage insurance depends on the lender and the loan program, and a wrong PMI number is worse than none. If your market needs it, add it as a fifth monthly line in the onChange consumer or extend the component; the source is yours after installation.

Can I use it outside the US?

Yes. The formula is universal for fixed rate loans. Pass monthlyHoa={0}, set propertyTaxRate to your local rate or 0, and format currency with your own Intl locale in the consuming component. The labels are props, so "HOA" can become "Service charge" or disappear.

Why not use a mortgage calculator embed from a lender?

Embeds load a third party script, carry the lender's branding, and often break Core Web Vitals on mobile because they inject an iframe above the fold. A component you own renders as part of the page, inherits your theme tokens and sends leads to you, not to the lender.

Test Automation with agent-device (Part 3)

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This post covers saving test flows as agent-device .ad scripts and running them again, using a Flutter app. It goes through writing an .ad file, getting the app into the right state before a replay, reading the report when a replay stops and fixing it, and the token cost of agent-driven QA. It is based on agent-device 0.21.31 (2026-10-25).

Installation and the basic loop are in Part 1, and real-device setup is in Part 2.

What an .ad file is

When an agent checks an app, it reads the screen, picks the next thing to tap, and reads the result, over and over. Exploring the same flow this way every time keeps costing time and tokens. An .ad file pins down the result of that exploration as a list of commands, and replay runs the list from top to bottom.

Each line has the form <command> [positional...] [flag...], the same as an agent-device command in the terminal. Quoting follows shell rules too, so a selector containing spaces is wrapped like 'label="..."'. Below is the start of a flow that opens the app fresh, taps a button on the first screen, types into an input, and checks the result screen.

# First-run flow — the app ID comes in via -e APP= open ${APP} --relaunch wait 'label="<first-screen button>"' 30000 press 'label="<first-screen button>"' fill 'id="find-input"' "<text to type>" press 'label="<search button>" role=button' wait 'label="<result title>"' 60000 wait text "<text the result should show>" 10000 back close

A few notes:

  • # comments work. The docs do not say so, so the first line was a comment in a test replay, and it passed.
  • Do not hard-code the app ID. Leave it as ${APP} and pass -e APP=<bundle id> at replay time, and the same file works for builds with different IDs, such as a dev build and a release build.
  • The number after wait is a timeout in milliseconds. Give a long one to steps that wait on the network or on model inference.
  • Device-selection flags stay out of the file. --platform, --serial, --device, and --settle are not part of a script step; they go on the replay command.

There are two ways to make an .ad file. You can start recording with open <bundle id> --relaunch --save-script=<file>.ad, go through the flow, and save with session save-script, or you can write it by hand. The 24-step flow in this post was written by hand, based on a flow the agent had already passed manually.

Getting to a "first launch" state before replaying

replay only runs the commands written down; it does not reset the app. If the flow starts by checking the first-launch consent dialog, an app that still remembers an earlier consent stops right at step 2. So a separate runner script deletes and reinstalls the app before each replay.

# Android emulator adb -s <serial> uninstall <bundle id> adb -s <serial> install -r <apk path> adb -s <serial> shell cmd locale set-app-locales <bundle id> --locales en-US agent-device replay <file>.ad --platform android --serial <serial> -e APP=<bundle id> --timeout 240000 # iOS simulator xcrun simctl uninstall <UDID> <bundle id> xcrun simctl install <UDID> <Runner.app path> agent-device replay <file>.ad --platform ios --udid <UDID> -e APP=<bundle id> --timeout 300000

On Android, set-app-locales opens just this app in English while leaving the device language alone. On the iOS simulator no per-app language command was used, so the app follows the simulator's language setting. replay has a default work budget of 60 seconds, so raise it with --timeout for longer flows.

Deleting the app also deletes its data. So the runner script accepts only the designated test emulators (checked by name with adb -s <serial> emu avd name) and simulator UDIDs, and stops with exit code 2 if it gets any other device or an app ID outside your own. An emulator serial such as emulator-5554 is assigned in boot order and does not tell you which AVD it is, which is why the check uses the name.

With that in place, the 24-step flow finished with Replayed 24 steps on both runs on each platform: 8.8 and 9.4 seconds on the Android emulator, 13.9 and 14.0 seconds on the iOS simulator.

When a replay stops

When a replay gets stuck partway, it fails with REPLAY_DIVERGENCE. The report includes the step number and its line, the actionable elements on screen at that moment, and selector suggestions for that screen. So you do not have to start over after a fix, it also tells you how to resume with replay --from <step> --plan-digest <value>. When no automatic repair is possible, it says Repair hint: manual.

A first-launch system dialog. The flow that passed on Android stopped at step 2 the first time it ran on the iOS simulator.

Error (REPLAY_DIVERGENCE): Replay failed at step 2 (wait "label=\"<first-screen button>\"" 30000): wait timed out for selector: ... Current surface: Allow "<app name>" to track your activity across other companies' apps ...

The report's Current surface showed the App Tracking Transparency (ATT) dialog. It is a system dialog that appears on first launch only on iOS, so the flow written against Android had no step for it. After closing it with agent-device alert dismiss and replaying again, the run got past this step. The runner script does not handle it, so on builds that show this dialog, close it before replaying.

Input role names that differ by platform. Next, the replay stopped at the fill in step 5. The first selector was role=text-field, which matched on Android, but the iOS simulator exposed the same input as an other element with a label. To find one selector that works on both platforms, these were tried in order:

  1. editable=true — passed on Android, Selector did not match on iOS
  2. role=textfield || role=text-field — || is the "or" syntax that appeared in the report's selector suggestions. It still did not match on iOS.
  3. Adding Semantics(identifier:) to the Flutter widget — passed on both platforms

The last fix is one line of app code: identifier: 'find-input' added to the existing Semantics around the input, with no visible change on screen. The selector becomes id="find-input". As noted in Part 1, a Flutter Key does not show up in the accessibility tree, so adding an identifier is the reliable way to run the same script on both platforms.

Token cost and the rules for cutting it

Token cost was the reason for using .ad in the first place. Most of the cost of agent-driven QA came not from output but from cache reads. A cache read is the earlier conversation the agent re-reads on every response. The first real-device check of two apps took about 40 minutes and came to 121 responses, 77,985 output tokens, and 30,225,404 cache-read tokens, roughly 250,000 tokens re-read per response.

The QA agent was already on Sonnet, so the model was not the cause. In the same stretch, 11 screenshots were read into the conversation, and as the context grew, every response re-read more. So five rules went into the QA agent's instructions:

  1. Save a flow as .ad the first time it passes, and use replay from then on. When it gets stuck, fix only that step using the divergence report.
  2. Act with --settle and look only at the returned diff. Do not read the full screen tree.
  3. Save screenshots to files only, and read at most one per item into the conversation.
  4. End the session per item: one app, one thing to check. Past 40 responses, it is time to stop.
  5. Read logs and build output with grep or tail, only the lines you need.

The batch after the rules (three emulator checks, an iPhone attempt, an Android real device, about 30 minutes) came to 105 responses, 44,113 output tokens, and 19,171,162 cache-read tokens, about 180,000 cache-read tokens per response. The items checked were different, so the two sets of numbers cannot be compared directly, and the 40-response limit was not met. Tokens after adopting .ad have not been measured separately yet, and neither replay on a real device nor running it in CI has been tried.

Introduction

The page title says "Welcome to FUEL CMS." Not a generic landing page, not a logo with no text — the literal CMS name, in the <title> tag, for anyone running curl to read. Escalation

Standard CodeIgniter/Fuel CMS structure places database credentials in a predictable config file:

cd /var/www/html/fuel/application/config cat database.php $db['default'] = array( 'hostname' => 'localhost', 'username' => 'root', 'password' => 'mememe', 'database' => 'fuel_schema', ... );

MySQL root password: mememe. Worth testing immediately against the Linux accounts — database and system credential reuse is depressingly common.

First attempt — sudo su with the DB password:

sudo su # [sudo] password for www-data: mememe # Sorry, try again. (x3)

www-data has no valid sudo password matching this — expected, since www-data typically has no interactive login credentials at all.

Second attempt — su directly to root:

su # Password: mememe root@ubuntu:~# cat root.txt b9bbcb33e11b80be759c4e844862482d

The MySQL root password and the Linux root password are identical.

Exploitation — The Full Chain

  1. Page title identified Fuel CMS directly; searchsploit found CVE-2018-16763
  2. EDB-50477 exploit → pseudo-shell, no authentication required
  3. wget from pseudo-shell → staged a full PHP reverse shell in the webroot
  4. HTTP trigger → reverse shell as www-data → user flag
  5. application/config/database.php → MySQL root password mememe
  6. su (not sudo su) → password reuse → Linux root → root flag

Rabbit Holes

sudo su before plain su. The first escalation attempt used sudo su, which failed three times with the correct-looking password. The distinction matters: sudo asks for the calling user's own password (here, www-data, which has no usable interactive password), while su asks for the target user's password (root's own password). Since the leaked credential was reused as root's actual login password — not as a sudo grant for www-data — only the second form worked. A useful general reminder: when a found password doesn't work with sudo, try it with su before assuming it's the wrong credential entirely.

Duplicate file downloads via the exploit's wget command. Running the wget command once through the pseudo-shell apparently issued the underlying HTTP request multiple times internally (resulting in php-reverse-shell.php.1 through .12), based on the directory listing immediately afterward. This didn't cause any functional problem — the original un-suffixed file was still valid and triggered the shell correctly — but it's a quirk worth expecting when using this particular exploit script.

Key Takeaway

When a discovered credential fails against sudo, try it against su before discarding it — the two commands check entirely different passwords (the caller's vs. the target account's), and a credential leaked from an application config file is far more likely to match a direct account password than a sudo grant for an unrelated service account.

How We Built a 10-Language QR Templates Library (With Full RTL Support)

A look at how we localized an interactive, client-side React application into 10 languages without using a backend server.
If you build web tools, you know that keeping them accessible is a constant challenge.

When we launched the RahaLune QR Code Templates Library, it was a fully functional, client-side React application with over 100 pre-designed templates. Users could browse templates, open a modal editor, customize colors, and download high-resolution QR codes.

But there was a glaring problem: it was English-only. For a tool designed to help global businesses, this was a massive barrier to entry.

Today, we rolled out a massive update. The entire library and editor are now natively translated into 10 languages (English, French, Spanish, German, Turkish, Chinese, Indonesian, Malay, Arabic, and Urdu), complete with full Right-to-Left (RTL) layout support.

Here is a look at how we handled it.

The Translation Architecture

Since RahaLune is a 100% client-side application, we didn't want to rely on an API to fetch translations. We wanted the site to remain incredibly fast, even if the user switched languages multiple times.

We used standard JSON files for each language (e.g., en.json, fr.json, ar.json). When the user selects a language from the header dropdown, or visits a localized URL (like ?lang=fr), the app dynamically imports the corresponding JSON file and updates the React context.

This keeps the initial bundle size small, as we only load the translations the user actually needs.

Handling the RTL Layout Challenge

Supporting Arabic and Urdu wasn't just about translating the text—it required a complete layout flip.

If you are building an app with RTL support, the golden rule is to avoid hardcoding directional CSS. Instead of using margin-left or padding-right, you should use CSS logical properties:
margin-inline-start instead of margin-left
padding-inline-end instead of padding-right

We also dynamically update the tag based on the selected language to trigger the browser's native RTL rendering:

document.documentElement.setAttribute('dir', currentLanguage === 'ar' || currentLanguage === 'ur' ? 'rtl' : 'ltr');

This single attribute ensures that flexbox layouts, grid systems, and text alignment seamlessly flip for RTL languages. The template gallery, the editor inputs, and the floating icons all mirror perfectly without needing separate CSS files.

Fixing UI Breakage with Longer Text

German and French text is often 20-30% longer than English. When you have tight UI components like pill-shaped category tabs or small badge labels, this causes major layout breakage.

To fix this, we had to ensure our CSS had flexible containers. We used text-wrap: balance and allowed certain buttons to grow gracefully rather than clipping the text. For the template cards, we ensured the description containers had a minimum height so the grid didn't look jagged when some cards had longer translations than others.

What a real user's USB capture found that my synthetic tests didn't: two bugs in usb-rea

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For weeks every test of usb-rea ran on captures I generated myself, on protocols documented by liquidctl and rivalcfg, and on a few public Wireshark samples. All of that passed. Then a real user shared a real capture, and within a day it had found two bugs that none of my tests could have found.

This is the fifth post about usb-rea, an open-source tool I built that diffs USB captures per labelled click in a vendor app and writes a pyusb driver. The first one covered commands, the second one naming what the device reports back, the third one handshakes and session tokens, the fourth one video streams to case LCDs. This one is about what happened when the tool met hardware I don't own.

The capture

On liquidctl issue 936 the owner of an ASUS TUF Gaming LC III AIO (the one with an LCD on the pump) shared a USBPcap capture from Windows while changing the screen brightness in the vendor app. The full capture is big: about 76,000 packets, nearly all of them a 320x320 JPEG video the app streams to the screen at around 70 frames per second, plus four tiny brightness writes hidden in between.

That capture is what pushed me to add image stream detection in v0.7.4 (the fourth post). After that, the full capture gave the right answer: brightness is byte 4 of a 440-byte message on interrupt EP 0x01, the video goes to EP 0x03 with a 08 nn 00 80 header, and the device acks each frame with 08 81 on EP 0x84. That matched what the owner had worked out by hand.

Then I asked if I could keep a trimmed copy as a regression test. They said yes, and turning it into a test is where the two bugs showed up.

Making a fixture you can publish

A raw capture is not something to commit to a public repo. It has other devices on the same bus, and the video frames are literally whatever was on the screen. So the stored copy:

  • keeps only this device's packets: the setup, the first 8 frames and two frames on each side of each brightness change (589 packets, 6.5 KB gzipped)
  • zeroes the image data in every frame, keeping only the JPEG headers and the end marker, so nothing that was on the screen is in the file
  • shifts the timestamps to a fixed date and replaces the IRP ids with counters

The test in tests/test_user_capture_lc3.py then checks the VID:PID, the brightness command and the stream end to end. The first run failed twice.

Bug 1: USBPcap puts the control reply in the "complete" record

usb-rea finds the vendor and product id from the GET_DESCRIPTOR reply at the start of a capture. On this file it said there wasn't one. Here is what v0.7.5 printed at the top of the report:

Device: `unknown (no GET_DESCRIPTOR in capture)`

But the reply is right there. This is the real output of usb-rea transfers on the fixture (first two lines):

1 dev 2 ep 0x80 control IN 8006000100001200 | 2 dev 2 ep 0x80 control IN 12 01 10 01 00 00 00 40 05 0b 7b 1c 51 00 01 02 03 01

USBPcap logs a control transfer in stages. Frame 1 is the setup stage (80 06 00 01 ..., GET_DESCRIPTOR for the device descriptor). The descriptor itself (12 01 ..., with 05 0b 7b 1c being 0B05:1C7B little-endian) arrives in a separate record for the "complete" stage, stage 3. My parser only took control IN data from the stage it expected, so on captures where the data comes in stage 3 the descriptor was simply dropped. My own synthetic USBPcap files never did that, which is exactly why the tests never caught it.

v0.7.6 reads control IN data from the complete record too. Same file, new report:

Device: `0b05:1c7b`

Bug 2: counting the ack over the whole capture

The second failure was quieter. v0.7.5 found the video stream and the brightness command, but no frame ack. Worse, the 08 81 acks didn't just disappear: they ended up in the report as a "reply to brightness", because a couple of them happened to arrive a few milliseconds after a brightness write.

The old rule was: a fixed device-to-host message is the ack if it shows up about once per frame over the whole capture (between 0.5x and 1.2x the number of frames). On the full capture that held. On the trimmed one it didn't: the fixture keeps the acks for frames I cut out, so it has 66 acks for 24 frames. That's an artefact of trimming, but the same thing happens on a real capture whenever the app keeps polling while it pauses the video, and a global ratio can't tell the difference.

v0.7.6 counts the ack per frame instead: a fixed device-to-host message between one frame's start and the next (and no more than 1 s after the frame's last packet). It's the ack when at least half of the frames have one and most of those have exactly one. The new frames_acked field says how many frames it was seen for. The report now has this line in the stream section (real output on the fixture):

- Ack: `08 81 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00` on EP 0x84, 66x (frames 30, 52, 94, 116, 138)

And because the acks are set aside with the stream, the bogus "Reply to brightness" section is gone. Summarised from the usb-rea infer JSON of both versions on the fixture, same labels:

0.7.5 vid/pid None None ack None responses 1 0.7.6 vid/pid 2821 7291 ack EP 0x84, frames_acked 18 responses 0

(2821 and 7291 are 0x0B05 and 0x1C7B.)

What the trimmed copy can't tell you

Some numbers in the fixture report are about the trimming, not the device, and I'd rather say so:

  • The frame rate shows as about 0.2 frames per second, because most frames were cut out. The full capture is around 70.
  • The brightness command comes out at 60% confidence from two clean samples (brightness 0 and brightness 50); the third action is ignored because it sent a differently shaped transaction. On the full capture it's 100%.
  • The image content is zeros, so the fixture can't test anything that depends on the pixels.

What it does test is everything the bugs were about: the device descriptor in a USBPcap complete record, the stream headers, the per-frame ack, and the brightness byte on its own endpoint.

pip install -U usb-rea # 0.7.6 usb-rea demo --device lcd

gunzip -c tests/data/asus-tuf-lc3-lcd.pcap.gz > lc3.pcap usb-rea report lc3.pcap -l "brightness 0;brightness 50;brightness 100" -o lc3.md

The lesson for me: synthetic captures test the inference, real captures test the assumptions about how capture tools write files. If you have a USB device whose app only runs on Windows, one USBPcap capture while you change one setting is the most useful thing you can send. The service exposes its metrics for Prometheus to scrape every fifteen seconds, with the default scrape timeout of ten. Two years earlier we had added a gauge for the number of dispatches waiting to go out, implemented as a custom collector that ran a count query against the database each time the endpoint was read. Every one of eight pods ran it, and both of our Prometheus replicas scraped every pod, so the database answered that same question about once a second. On a normal day it took a third of a second.

When the database slowed down that query took around fourteen seconds. Prometheus gave up at ten, and a scrape that times out is a failed scrape, all of it. The target's up series went to zero, and Prometheus marked every series it had collected from that target as stale, the request counters and latency histograms as well as the one gauge that was slow. Our error rate alert divided one rate by another, and with no samples on either side there was nothing to compare with the threshold. The alert that did fire was TargetDown, five minutes in, routed as a ticket because a missing target is usually a deploy.

Meanwhile sixteen count queries every fifteen seconds were adding load to a database that was already struggling.

The pending count now comes from one place. A small background task in a single instance runs the query every thirty seconds with its own timeout and publishes the result, and the service's metrics endpoint serves only values it already holds in memory. We alert when scrape_duration_seconds for any target passes half its timeout. And TargetDown for a service carrying live traffic now pages, because we learned it can be the only thing still speaking.

A metrics endpoint that depends on the system it measures will go quiet exactly when that system is in trouble, and it can take everything else it reports down with it.

635 paragraphs on our site cannot contain a link, because the page body is data and not JSX

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Our marketing site has 72 URLs in its sitemap. 67 of them render their body from this type:

export type ContentSection = { heading: string paragraphs: string[] points?: { term: string; detail: string }[] }

A page file contains an array of those and nothing else that is visual. The component that turns them into HTML is about thirty lines: an h2, a stack of p, and an optional dl for the points. There is no className prop on a section, no heading level argument, no slot for arbitrary children.

The five pages that do not use it are the homepage, /about and the three legal documents, which are hand written.

What that produced, measured from the live HTML

I fetched all 72 pages and counted the rendered markup rather than the source:

pages with sections 67 sections 388 body paragraphs 635 points lists 21 terms inside them 166 most sections on one page 8

1.64 paragraphs per section. 21 of 388 sections use the optional list, which is roughly the proportion I would have guessed when writing the type: most of this is prose, and the list is for the handful of things that are genuinely enumerable.

The four pages with eight sections are all guides. Nothing on the site has nine, which is not a limit anybody implemented.

The constraint I did not design on purpose

paragraphs is string[]. A string cannot contain an anchor. So a page written through this type cannot have a link inside its prose. Not "should not", cannot: there is nowhere to put one.

I checked the rendered output for all 72 pages, looking inside every paragraph the component emits:

body paragraphs rendered by the component 635 ...containing an <a> 0

The only body paragraph on the whole site with a link in it is on /about, which is one of the five hand written pages. 635 to nothing is not a coincidence, it is a type.

That cuts both ways and I want to be fair about both.

What it buys: the typography lives in one component, so no page can invent a new heading size, and a section with a heading and no words under it is visible as paragraphs: [] in the source. It also means the copy is countable. Every audit I have run on this site, word counts, banned phrases, duplicate sentences, exclamation marks, was possible because the prose is data in a file rather than text interleaved with markup.

What it costs: in-prose contextual links are the single most frequently recommended internal linking tactic there is, and we have none. Every link on these pages is structural, in the header, the breadcrumb, the related-pages block at the bottom, or the call to action. A crawler reading one of our guides sees a page that mentions nine related concepts and links to none of them from the sentence doing the mentioning.

That is a real cost, and it was not a decision. It fell out of a type I wrote to solve a typography problem.

The keys are the content

One detail in the component that I would not write again:

{sections.map((section) => ( <section key={section.heading}> ... {section.paragraphs.map((paragraph) => ( <p key={paragraph} ...>{paragraph}</p> ))}

The React key for a section is its heading. The key for a paragraph is the entire paragraph. The key for a point is its term. So two identical paragraphs inside one section, or two sections with the same heading on one page, are a duplicate key warning and a reconciliation bug waiting for an editor to write the same sentence twice.

I measured that too, across all 388 sections: zero sections contain a repeated paragraph. The content satisfies a constraint nobody wrote down, which is the most fragile kind of correct.

The fix is boring. These lists are static, server rendered, and never reordered, so the index is a perfectly good key and cannot collide:

{section.paragraphs.map((paragraph, i) => ( <p key={i} ...>{paragraph}</p> ))}

Using content as a key is a habit people pick up from the lint rule that complains about index keys. The rule is right about reorderable client lists and wrong about a prerendered document.

What I would actually change

Not the type. The temptation is paragraphs: (string | ReactNode)[], and the moment that lands, the prose stops being data: no word count, no duplicate detection, no banned phrase test, because half of it is now a React element.

The version I would ship instead keeps the strings and adds a narrow seam:

type ContentSection = { heading: string paragraphs: string[] /** Phrases in this section's prose to link, once each. */ links?: { phrase: string; href: string }[] }

The renderer splits each paragraph on the phrase and wraps it. The prose stays a string, the audits keep working, and a page can say "we explain this in the hosting guide" with a link on those words. It is maybe twenty lines of component and one more field, and it is the thing I would do next if internal linking on this site needed a push.

Check it yourself

URL=https://pub-trivia.app/guides/how-to-be-a-good-quizmaster # Sections on the page curl -s "$URL" | grep -o '<h2 class="text-foreground text-2xl' | wc -l # Body paragraphs, and how many of them contain a link curl -s "$URL" | python3 -c " import re, sys ps = re.findall(r'<p class=\"text-muted-foreground leading-relaxed\">(.*?)</p>', sys.stdin.read(), re.S) print(len(ps), 'paragraphs,', sum('<a ' in p for p in ps), 'with a link') "

Eight sections, 25 paragraphs, and not one link in any of them. Match the <h2 prefix rather than the class on its own: the class name appears a second time inside the RSC flight payload at the bottom of the document, so a looser grep returns sixteen.

Then open pub-trivia.app/about, the hand written page, and notice that its paragraphs do contain links. One page in five that does not use the type, and it is the only page that can do the thing the other 67 cannot.

A type is a policy. If you encode your page body as data, write down what the data cannot express, because in a year that list is going to be a list of things your site does not do.
