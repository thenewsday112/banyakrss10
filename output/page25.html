Deep Purple’s Ian Gillan Hospitalized Following Medical Emergency, Two Shows Postponed

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Deep Purple frontman Ian Gillan has been hospitalized following a medical emergency, forcing the legendary British rock band to postpone two concerts in Germany.

In a statement shared on Instagram Saturday (Oct. 31), Deep Purple announced “with great disappointment” that its concert at QI Arena in Leipzig that evening, as well as its upcoming performance at Sporthalle Hamburg, had been postponed.

“This comes as a result of a medical emergency involving Ian Gillan, which has resulted in his current hospitalisation,” wrote the band, which also features guitarist Simon McBride, bassist Roger Glover, drummer Ian Paice and keyboardist Don Airey.

Deep Purple did not provide further details about Gillan’s condition or indicate when he might be released from the hospital.

“We understand that this announcement comes at short notice and share the disappointment of all our wonderful fans that we are unable to perform in Leipzig and Hamburg.”

The band added that it is working to find a “suitable time to reschedule” both concerts. Tickets purchased for the original dates will remain valid for the rescheduled performances.

Deep Purple has been on the road for its SPLAT! world tour since late summer, with additional dates scheduled across Europe, the United Kingdom, South America and Mexico through late December.

Gillan has served as Deep Purple’s lead vocalist during multiple stints, first joining the band in 1969. He is the voice behind some of the British rock group’s best-known hits such as “Smoke on the Water”and “Highway Star.”

Gillan’s hospitalization comes just weeks after former Deep Purple bassist and vocalist Glenn Hughes underwent 10-hour open-heart surgery. Hughes, who announced his retirement from touring earlier this year, shared an update in mid-October, saying he was recovering at home following the procedure.



Binance Lost $6.3 Billion to Its Own Signing Machine

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Thursday evening, 29 October, a Binance hot wallet on Tron sent 612.7 million USDT and 46.4 million TRX to an address the exchange didn't control.

It took less than two minutes. Ethereum followed. So did Bitcoin, Solana, Optimism, and five other chains. By the time Binance paused withdrawals that night, an attacker had moved roughly $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes, according to TRM Labs.

Nothing in crypto's history comes close. The previous record belonged to Bybit for just over 20 months, and the FBI put that theft at $1.5 billion, a little under a quarter of this one.

"It's the biggest hack in history."

Aravind Srinivasan, head of the systematic OTC business at market maker GSR, quoted by RTÉ

The size of the number isn't what kept me reading, though. This line from Binance did: the exchange said it had "found no evidence of any wallet private key leak." Every one of those transfers was validly signed. Bitcoin, Ethereum and Tron did exactly what they were built to do.

So how do you lose $6.3 billion without losing a key?

How an exchange actually holds your coins

A blockchain accepts any transfer that carries a valid signature for the sending address. It has no way to tell whether that signature came from the rightful owner or from someone who has taken over the process that produces it. Hold on to that point; the whole story turns on it.

Large exchanges keep most customer assets in cold storage (offline, and slow to move on purpose) and a smaller float in hot wallets, which sign automatically so your withdrawal clears in seconds. "Smaller" is relative. Binance held about $144 billion in custody before the attack, so this loss works out to roughly 4% of it.

A hot-wallet signer is, by design, an obedient machine. A request comes in, passes a policy check, and gets signed. The key itself sits in a hardware security module (HSM) or a multi-party computation (MPC) setup and never leaves. That keeps the key safe from theft. Whether each incoming request deserves a signature is a separate problem, and a harder one.

Step one: a DevOps engineer's laptop

Binance's first explanation was that the incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop," which let the attacker reach an internal service container able to submit withdrawal transactions.

The story grew the next day. Co-CEO Richard Teng said investigators had found additional intrusion points, that the attacker used a self-destruct mechanism to erase tools and traces, and that Binance is now reviewing endpoints, cloud systems, key management and signing processes across the whole company. It still hasn't said how the laptop was compromised.

"It involved a more complex attack process."

Binance co-CEO Richard Teng, in a post on X reported by Forbes

If that sounds familiar, it should. The Bybit theft also started on a developer's machine: attackers got into Safe{Wallet}'s environment and replaced a JavaScript file so that Bybit's signers approved a transaction they couldn't see clearly. Attackers who target crypto firms have spent years luring crypto developers into fake job interviews whose "coding tests" install malware. Nobody has confirmed that's what happened at Binance. It's the obvious first place to look.

Step two: fifty signatures and one wallet

On Ethereum, the attacker used EIP-7702, a feature that went live with the Pectra upgrade on 7 May 2025. It lets an ordinary wallet (an externally owned account, or EOA) point to smart-contract code and run that code as if it were its own. Wallet teams wanted it for batched transactions and sponsored gas fees. Turning it on needs exactly one thing: a signature from the wallet's own key.

At 20:20:59 UTC, the attacker submitted a single transaction carrying 50 EIP-7702 authorizations, all signed by the hot wallet labeled Binance 14. Each authorization is tied to the account's nonce, a counter that goes up by one with every transaction. A busy exchange wallet's nonce moves constantly, so the attacker prepared 50 consecutive ones; whichever matched at the moment of submission would work. One did.

That authorization pointed Binance 14 at a sweeper contract that took orders only from the attacker. Three minutes and 24 seconds later, the wallet sent out 167,464 ETH and 63 tokens, including 1.615 billion USDT. The delegation stayed in place afterwards, so customer deposits that kept landing at the same address were swept six more times over the next three and a half hours.

Two details matter here. “Fifty sequential signatures” means sustained access to the signing path, well beyond one phished approval. And Teng has said the delegation bypassed Binance's usual approval process; the signer produced something its own policy layer never reviewed.

The technique itself wasn't new. A month after Pectra shipped, Wintermute found that more than 80% of EIP-7702 delegations pointed at copy-pasted sweeper contracts that drain wallets with leaked keys. A December 2025 arXiv paper on EIP-7702 phishing showed that one malicious authorization can hand an attacker persistent control of an account. The Binance attacker ran the same playbook at exchange scale, with Binance's own signer doing the signing.

Security researcher Taylor Monahan made the key point back in 2025, when those sweeper delegations first spread:

"It's not actually a 7702 issue."

Taylor Monahan, speaking to The Block about wallets drained through EIP-7702 (June 2025)

The feature only does what a valid signature tells it to. The weak point is whoever controls the signing.

Why "no key leaked" is cold comfort

TRM's reading of the chain data points the same way. It saw ordinary signed transfers leave Binance wallets on several chains, across different key types, within minutes, and concluded that the attacker could make Binance's hot-wallet signing infrastructure authorize transfers of its choosing.

That's why Binance's reassurance doesn't reassure me much. If an attacker can submit requests from inside the trusted zone, or quietly edit the checks those requests pass through, the HSM can stay sealed and still sign away the balance. Binance's statement is very likely true. It answers a different question from the one customers are asking. Fireblocks, which builds custody infrastructure, put the practical rule well: treat any signature produced outside your signing platform as an incident.

The race to freeze the money

The response was fast, at least for some assets. Within about an hour, Tether had frozen 975.1 million USDT and Circle 823 million USDC in the main receiving wallet, roughly $1.8 billion between them; Circle said it acted in coordination with law enforcement. Tether's total later rose to more than 1.567 billion USDT. Binance now puts everything frozen across issuers and partners at around $3 billion.

"In coordination with law enforcement, we have frozen more than 800M USDC."

Circle CEO Jeremy Allaire, posting on X, via Decrypt

The attacker clearly knew the clock was running. TRM traced about 325 million USDT sold through UniswapX and other intent-based contracts for roughly 122,300 ETH. That implies about $2,650 per ETH at a time when ether traded between $2,300 and $2,350; paying a premium of more than 10% is what urgency looks like on-chain. A second wallet deposited 60 million USDT into Aave and borrowed about 19,760 ETH against it before Aave froze the affected markets. By Bitquery's count, the attacker's ether pile has since grown to 490,361 ETH, mostly from converting stablecoins.

There's an awkward lesson in that split. The most centralized assets in crypto, dollar stablecoins with an issuer who can blacklist an address, clawed back close to half of the loss within hours. BTC and ETH have nobody to call. Even a freeze isn't the end of it: TRM's Ari Redbord points out that getting frozen funds back requires a separate legal process.

Who is behind it

Nobody has been officially named. As of 31 October, no government has publicly attributed the attack, Binance hasn't named a suspect, and its root-cause findings aren't out yet. Analytics firms have shared early assessments based on on-chain patterns; until law enforcement or Binance's post-mortem confirms anything, I'm leaving the question open.

What the chain data does show is planning. According to TRM, preparation began at least 36 hours before the theft: the attacker's wallets were funded through the Tornado Cash mixer, and transaction signatures were collected in advance. Whoever did this was patient, and had deep access long before the first coin moved.

What Binance has promised

Binance says it will cover every loss with company money. It has reserved more than $6.34 billion, about $5 billion of it already held in the same tokens that were taken, and is buying the rest on the market and over the counter. Its emergency SAFU fund alone is worth roughly $1.17 billion.

Withdrawals are still paused. Binance's 31 October update targets a phased restart within about 34 hours, subject to security checks, and proof-of-reserves results by Monday, 2 November. A technical post-mortem is promised but has no date.

What has to change

Bybit's breach began on a developer's machine at a supplier; Binance's began on a DevOps engineer's laptop. Any laptop that can reach production deserves the protection production gets: a dedicated, locked-down device, credentials tied to a hardware key, access that expires when the task is done, and a firm rule against running code from strangers (that recruiter's "take-home assignment" included).

Binance's signer trusted whatever its internal services handed it. A second approver, running on separate infrastructure that a hijacked container can't touch, should match every large withdrawal to a real customer request before anything is signed. Fireblocks makes a related point: hot-wallet keys should never be usable from a developer machine at all.

EIP-7702 needs its own rules. A treasury wallet that suddenly delegates to contract code is about as loud a warning as custody gets, and it shows up on-chain for anyone watching. Exchanges should refuse to sign 7702 authorizations unless a human expects one, and a single request for dozens of them, one per upcoming nonce, should page someone immediately.

Then there's containment. One intruder emptied wallets on ten chains in about 20 minutes, which tells me the signing paths shared far too much. Give each chain and wallet tier its own signer, cap how much value can leave per hour, and halt outflows to unfamiliar addresses automatically. Binance paused withdrawals at about 22:15 UTC, roughly two hours after the first transfers at about 20:05 UTC, and the hijacked Ethereum wallet kept sweeping new deposits even after that.

If you hold funds on Binance, three things. Expect a wave of "refund" and "compensation" phishing; Binance will never ask for your 2FA codes or recovery phrase. When the proof-of-reserves report lands, use Binance's verification tool to check your balance is included. And once withdrawals reopen, ask yourself whether long-term holdings belong on any exchange at all.

Four years ago I argued in Regulating Chaos that IoT security would only improve once the law caught up with its weakest devices. Crypto custody has reached the same point, and its weakest device turned out to be a laptop. Monday's reserves report will tell us whether Binance's customers are whole; the post-mortem will tell every other exchange which of its own engineers' desks to check first.

Europa Universalis 5’s Across the Pillars DLC adds nice Iberian flavors but lacks a little spice

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Europa Universalis 5‘s new DLC, Across the Pillars, focuses on adding flavor to the Iberian Peninsula, specifically Castille (Spain), Granada, and Morocco. It tremendously increases the value of playing in this wonderful part of Europe but ultimately falls short of giving you enough unique events and decisions that really turn the gameplay around.

I spent most of my time playing as Castille, looking to proclaim the Spanish Monarchy after uniting the crown of Aragon with my own, following the historical path that led to the eventual rise (and fall) of the Spanish Empire. The DLC opens the game up with a unique situation, the Battle for the Strait, in which Morocco and Castille vie for control over the last remnants of Al-Andalus—the Emirate of Granada. Castille, aka myself, wants to conquer and integrate it into the later Spain, while Morocco has its own agenda and seeks to either expand itself or reclaim the glory of the Umayyads in Iberia.

The new situation is kind of dry, but still good to have. Image via Paradox Interactive

It took several tries, but I eventually conquered Granada fully. It declared an independence war, which I had trouble containing long enough for Morocco to sweep in, proclaim Granada its subject, and take over as the war leader. The independence war no longer being the main CB, I was allowed to conquer Granada’s territories. It was rather easy since I had total continental dominance compared to Morocco, even if they held the upper hand on the sea at the time.

But to my surprise the situation just… ended? The events leading up to it were cool and flavored, but the situation itself simply had very few decisions you could make as Castille. For example, I could only dismantle Granada’s forts, reduce its sovereignty, and demand Muslim scholars from it and convert them to Christianity. That was about it, though I could also pressure its court to gain greater control over the country. Furthermore, Granada simply wants to be independent from you, and nothing really helped. Even at +150 relations, Granada was severely disloyal and declared numerous independence wars against me.

The new expeditions were an actual blast, though. Image via Paradox Interactive

Read what’s behind the latest news in gaming (with a bit of snark) by subscribing to the Destructoid Checkpoint newsletter!

Morocco and Tlemcen also were super warlike and kept declaring wars, disrupting my gameplay, for no particular reason. They couldn’t take any territories, seldom had naval supremacy, and never, and I mean never, had any upper hand on land. The Battle for the Strait thus devolves into a waiting game whereby you actually want to give Granada over to Morocco and then conquer it willy-nilly, since Granada’s countless independence wars do not allow you to do basically anything against it in a peace deal, forcing white peace.

A much stronger side of the DLC are the new expeditions, which all tie into your historical status as a colonial nation searching far and wide for mythical cities and locations. These expeditions are a much better way of exploring than simply sending out exploration missions as they come with a ton of flavor and unique events and can lead to some incredible finds and rapid expansion into the New World. As someone who absolutely adores colonial gameplay in every EU game, I found this to be the strongest and most interesting part of the DLC.

The Iberian Union did not work out well for me since for some reason Aragon kept taking it over despite my ruler being the only son of the king and queen who formed it. I eventually gained control somehow and started integrating the Catalonians into the ultimate Spanish state. A few of my runs fell through because of France, whose levy-spam strategy still works in the game, and because I am completely dumb in economics.

Overall, though, I think this DLC definitely improves the state of affairs in Iberia. It’s better than not having it, even if the main situation is a bit dry. The DLC spans hundreds of in-game years, through many different eras and ages, and it will keep you engaged and entertained for a very long time indeed.

Every programmer eventually encounters that moment of quiet confusion during a late-night debugging session: you cast a signed 8-bit -1 to hexadecimal, expecting something clean and human-readable, only for the debugger to spit back 0xFF. Widen it to a 32-bit register, and it suddenly balloons into 0xFFFFFFFF.


Where did the minus sign go? Why didn't it output -0x01? And why does running 0x20000000000001 through your standard JavaScript parser silently yield the wrong integer altogether?


Number base conversion is often treated as a trivial Computer Science 101 exercise—something solved with a simple parseInt(val, 16) or a textbook division-by-radix loop. But beneath high-level abstractions, radix shifts are inextricably linked to hardware registers, two’s complement mathematics, IEEE-754 rounding traps, and data-encoding strategies.


Here is what is actually happening under the hood when bits change their radix, and why understanding these low-level mechanics prevents catastrophic bugs in production.

1. The Illusion of the Minus Sign: How Two's Complement Works

Hardware processors don't have dedicated silicon cells reserved for storing an aesthetic - glyph. In CPU registers, negative numbers are encoded via Two’s Complement.


In an NNN-bit register, the most significant bit (MSB) acts as a negative weight:

Value=−bN−12N−1+∑i=0N−2bi2i\text{Value} = -b_{N-1} 2^{N-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{N-2} b_i 2^iValue=−bN−1​2N−1+i=0∑N−2​bi​2i


When you assign -1 to an 8-bit signed integer (int8_t):

  1. Invert all bits of positive 1 (0000 0001 →\rightarrow→ 1111 1110).
  2. Add 1 (1111 1110 + 0000 0001 = 1111 1111).


In hexadecimal, each character represents an exact 4-bit nibble. Thus, 1111 1111 maps directly to 0xFF.

c #include <stdio.h> #include <stdint.h> int main() { int8_t small_neg = -1; int32_t wide_neg = small_neg; // Sign extension occurs here printf("8-bit hex: 0x%02X\n", (uint8_t)small_neg); // 0xFF printf("32-bit hex: 0x%08X\n", (uint32_t)wide_neg); // 0xFFFFFFFF return 0; }

The Sign Extension Trap

Notice what happened when widening int8_t to int32_t: the single byte 0xFF became 0xFFFFFFFF.


If the hardware merely padded the higher 24 bits with zeros (0x000000FF), the value would evaluate to +255, silently mutating your application state. To preserve the mathematical value of -1, the CPU performs Sign Extension: it duplicates the MSB (which is 1) across all newly allocated upper bits.


Pro-Tip for Packet Inspection:

When inspecting raw network packets or memory dumps, you can instantly determine if a hexadecimal value represents a negative signed integer without converting to decimal: look at the leading character.


Because 8 through F have their highest bit set to 1:

  • 0x8... through 0xF... →\rightarrow→ Negative (in signed contexts)
  • 0x0... through 0x7... →\rightarrow→ Zero or Positive

2. Arithmetic Shift (>>) vs. Logical Shift (>>>): A Subtle Production Hazard

Understanding two’s complement makes the difference between bitwise shift operators crystal clear:

  • Arithmetic Right Shift (>>): Preserves the sign bit. Vacated positions on the left are backfilled with whatever the MSB was.
  • Logical Right Shift (>>>): Agnostic to sign. It unconditionally backfills vacated positions with zeros.
typescript const value = -42; // Binary: 11111111 11111111 11111111 11010110 (in 32-bit) console.log(value >> 1); // Output: -21 (MSB remains 1; arithmetic integrity preserved) console.log(value >>> 1); // Output: 2147483627 (MSB replaced with 0; immediately converts to large unsigned integer)

In telemetry, hashing routines, and protocol deserialization, accidentally using >>> instead of >> (or vice versa) produces bugs that unit tests with small positive integers never catch.

3. The 53-Bit Wall: Why JavaScript Silently Corrupts Large Radixes

Consider this seemingly benign conversion snippet:

javascript const highSeqId = "9007199254740993"; // 2^53 + 1 console.log(Number(highSeqId).toString(16)); // Expected: "20000000000001" // Actual: "20000000000000" (Precision collapsed!)

Standard JavaScript Number types conform to the IEEE-754 double-precision floating-point specification. Double floats allocate:

  • 1 sign bit
  • 11 exponent bits
  • 52 explicit fraction bits (providing 53 bits of precision with the implicit leading 1)


Any integer larger than Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253−12^{53} - 1253−1, or 9,007,199,254,740,991) cannot be uniquely represented. When you convert an arbitrary-large hexadecimal hash or a 64-bit Snowflake ID via standard floating-point numbers, the least significant bits get silently rounded to the nearest representable floating-point neighbor.

The Fix: BigInt and Limbs

Arbitrary-precision arithmetic operates outside the Floating-Point Unit (FPU). Instead of relying on a single hardware register, runtimes allocate heap arrays of 64-bit integer chunks (often called machine limbs):

javascript // Lossless 64-bit / 128-bit Radix Transformations const safeVal = BigInt("0x20000000000001"); console.log(safeVal.toString(10)); // "9007199254740993" (Exact) console.log(safeVal.toString(36)); // "2000000000001"

4. Radix 36: The Workhorse of URL Slugs and Distributed IDs

While base-2 (binary), base-8 (octal), and base-16 (hexadecimal) map directly into CPU word architectures because they are powers of two (21,23,242^1, 2^3, 2^421,23,24), higher non-power-of-two radices serve specialized architectural purposes.


Chief among them is Radix 36 (0–9, a–z).

While Base-64 yields the highest raw density, its reliance on uppercase versus lowercase characters makes it vulnerable to collisions on case-insensitive filesystems and legacy databases. Furthermore, standard Base-64 requires URL-safe variations (RFC 4648) to avoid collision with URI reserved characters.


Radix 36 hits the sweet spot: it is totally case-insensitive, yields compact strings, and requires zero URL encoding.

python def to_base36(num: int) -> str: alphabet = "0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz" if num == 0: return "0" arr = [] while num: num, rem = divmod(num, 36) arr.append(alphabet[rem]) return "".join(reversed(arr)) print(to_base36(1685894123512)) # "hicw5f3g" (Clean slug ready for routing)

When designing custom bitmasks, verifying register overflows, or calculating variable-width two's complement strings, hand-calculating bit shifts invites human error.

Summary Checklist for Systems Developers

  1. Check your signedness: 0xFF is 255 in an unsigned 8-bit byte, but -1 in a signed two's complement register.
  2. Be cautious with bitwise shifts in JavaScript: JS converts operands into 32-bit signed integers before running bitwise operators. >> sign-extends; >>> zero-extends.
  3. Beware the 53-bit boundary: Never parse 64-bit IDs or large hashes with parseInt() or floating-point primitives—always enforce BigInt.
  4. Use Base-36 for safe slugification: When you need human-readable, URL-safe, compact sequential keys without case-sensitivity bugs, reach for Radix 36 before Base-64.
