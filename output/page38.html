'Social Media on Steroids.' Advocates for Children Call for Guardrails Around AI

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
—Olga Pankova—Getty Images

“We are living through the greatest transformation that we have seen in our lifetimes, and likely for a significant time before that,” two-time Surgeon General Vivek Murthy told an audience at New York City’s flagship public library on Oct. 28, comparing the advent of artificial intelligence to the advent of nuclear weaponry in terms of the need for carefully thought out safety standards. “We're at a branch point,” he added. “What should the future really look like, what are we trying to protect and preserve?”

Murthy was not alone in sounding the alarm. Jim Steyer, founder and CEO of Common Sense Media, the organizer of the conference, told the mostly supportive crowd that recent history had proved that the industry leaders or, as he called them “tech broligarchs,” could not be relied upon to create digital information systems that were safe or healthy for children. “We did not get out in front of social media in time,” he told the gathering. “This is the moment. AI is a bigger deal. It's basically social media on steroids.” 

New York’s governor Kathy Hochul seemed to underscore the point when she made an unscheduled stop at the daylong summit to announce new regulations around chatbots interactions with children. The bill was designed to ensure that AI agents do not impersonate humans or communicate in a way that suggests they have a relationship with a child. “It's happening so quickly,” said Hochul of the technological changes. “It's almost impossible for parents to know what's going on, for elected officials to stay ahead of it, and we certainly have learned the lesson that we can't count on the companies to put out products that are responsible and that put children first.”

Specifically, the new law, which goes into effect in January, will ban chatbots from encouraging suicide, self-harm, secrecy, or isolation and from discouraging children to seek help from other humans. They will also be banned from engaging in sexually explicit interactions and restricted from using a child’s past personal or health information in later conversations.

For the attendees and speakers at the summit, who ranged from Chelsea Clinton and Columbia law professor Tim Wu (onstage) to Uma Thurman (in the audience), the announcement was a good start, although many bemoaned the lack of federal regulation, around both AI and social media. “To this day, 2026, our federal government and Congress has still—nearly a quarter century after the advent of social media—failed to announce anything to protect our kids,” said Murthy, who is now chair of the advisory board of Common Sense Media’s Youth AI Safety Institute, which studies, tests, and rates AI platforms for children. 

Panelists called for a range of protective and preventative remedies for what they described as the coming tsunami of AI, including requiring more data transparency from tech companies by enabling regulatory agencies and researchers to get more access to the information they’re holding, using consumer-protection laws to ensure that AI models have been rigorously tested for potential negative outcomes, adopting the European model of not allowing AI companies to deploy their platforms until they have proved they are safe, or even instituting criminal penalties for AI companies who unleash unsafe technologies. “As a former prosecutor, I can tell you one thing,” said New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez, who earlier this month won a $25 billion judgment against Meta. “When you bring out handcuffs, suddenly everything changes.”

Not all the issues around AI can be solved by the law, however. One concern is the socialization of very young children by AI, including via voice-activated assistants like Alexa or very agreeable AI-enabled toys. “What do kids start to learn to expect from social practice? Do they expect someone to always agree with them? Is it always easy? Is it always like a transaction?” asked Dana Suskind, an early-childhood expert and professor at the University of Chicago. Human relationships are a lot messier, she noted. “They're chaotic, and that sort of mess is where a lot of learning happens.” Parents, who were caught off-guard by the influence of social media and digital devices, need to be educated about AI and encouraged that their imperfect parenting, as well as other human interaction, is vastly more beneficial to children than an AI companion, she said.

The summit also touched on the enormous implications AI is having on education and students’ ability to work and learn, when answers are in such ready supply. “MIT just did a report saying every assignment at MIT could be done by generative AI,” said John King, a former U.S. Secretary of Education and current chancellor of State University in New York. King is considering major changes to student assessment. “We have to make sure we are seeing the students do the work, and we have to make sure they're getting those foundational skills,” he said. “It literally means every faculty member reassessing what they assign and how they measure student learning.” And, as Clinton pointed out, this is an equity issue too. “We know that in families with less than $30,000 of household income, teenagers are three times more likely to either largely or entirely do their homework through artificial intelligence than those households with family incomes of $75,000 or more,” she said. 

At the same time, students need to be prepared to enter a workforce in which AI skills are not optional. For those who have mastered them, the future is very bright. “The people coming in learn much more rapidly because AI gives them more capacity than they've had before,” said Gene Sykes, chairman of global mergers and acquisitions at Goldman Sachs of the banking giant’s new recruits. “So somebody, within a month, can be doing incredibly productive work.” If good education in AI is distributed unevenly, some graduates will be vastly better prepared for a career than others, further exacerbating wealth inequities.

While there was little consensus on the immediate steps to take as AI arrives, there was no argument that it will be consequential for children, families, and generations to come. “As we go into this age of transformation,” Murthy noted, “the question forced upon us is the following: What aspects of human existence do we want to protect and preserve at all costs?”

Fanmade Star Wars game 19 years in the making is finally here, and it’s based on the greatest space sim ever made

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

FreeSpace Open project Fate of the Galaxy, a Star Wars-based total conversion of the classic space sim FreeSpace 2, has finally made it out of the modding hangars, having first started development in 2007.

Now, if you were ever involved in modding circles, you know how many ambitious projects can sprout in a community, many of which will never make the finish line—and the bigger the project and the longer the development cycle grows, life inevitably tends to get in the way. So the folks behind Fate of the Galaxy deserve a massive, massive round of applause for getting this past the finish line.

To underline how ridiculous this timeline is: FreeSpace 2 itself was released eight years prior, and Fate of the Galaxy was not even the first attempt at making such a mod for the game. (I see you, Star Wars Conversion Project! I was there!)

As the total conversion’s page explains, the “goal is to deliver starfighter combat that captures the look and feel of the movies.” It certainly got the look and the sound right, based on the trailer:

Eagle-eyed readers are likely flabbergasted by the fact that this nifty little thing is running on the engine of a space sim first released in 1999. I will for sure revisit the story of this incredible title and the community that it sparked someday, but the short version is that Volition made what is still considered to be the best game of its kind, but Interplay’s poor marketing decisions led to poor sales, the developers were spun off to THQ while they kept the franchise rights, and their subsequent financial collapse left the series in limbo, with studio itself closing its doors in 2023.

However, the game’s source code was released for noncommercial use, and the FreeSpace Open project that emerged from the ashes of a fantastic game that would never get another sequel has kept polishing and upgrading the underlying engine, producing absolutely incredible content to this day. The total conversion is completely free, ready for you to take to the stars after just a few clicks on its ModDB page: you don’t even need a copy of FreeSpace 2 itself, though I would strenuously recommend that you give that a try too. It’s a treasure trove of great sci-fi.

I don’t think we can ever have enough Star Wars space games, and Fate of the Galaxy is filling a surprisingly large hole. I never really got the hang of Star Wars: SQUADRONS, precisely because it didn’t have the  FreeSpace physics I grew up with, so I’m going to be all over this one once I have a bit of free time.

Poignantly, the work is dedicated to a team member who “tragically passed away in 2023:” to my shock, it was a fellow traveler—and fellow countryman, even—from my own FreeSpace modding days, and we even worked together on a campaign-that-ballooned-into-something-like-a-TC for a long while.

So I really hope the Force is strong with this one.

Darkbloom, which allowed Mac users to rent out their GPUs for AI inference, warns of low payouts

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Declining token prices have prompted Darkbloom, a distributed inference network, to reassess its business plans.

Darkbloom, a project from Seattle-based tech biz Eigen Labs, has developed software that allows anyone with a sufficiently capable macOS device to rent their GPU through the Darkbloom network for interference jobs. In return, they get paid.

Through this network, 800 or so participants – including as of two days ago this reporter – have been able to collect a few cents to a few dollars per day by having their hardware handle AI model prompts submitted through OpenRouter, an AI model gateway.

In theory, someone running one of Apple's recently released Mac minis with an M5 Pro chip and 64 GB of RAM could earn as much as $192 per month handling incoming inference jobs, per the company's estimate.

That assumes full 24/7 utilization during this period on a sought-after model like Qwen3.6 35B A3B, which sells for $0.065/M input tokens, $0.75/M output tokens. But not every network participant has fully surrendered their Mac hardware to the network.

The median earnings of the 50 people on the leaderboard amount to about $5.26 per day, $159.86 per month, or $1,918.50 per year. So the best case estimate presented by Darkbloom looks plausible. But it may not be sustainable.

The price of tokens – the unit of AI work – has declined, reportedly down 54 percent since May. Ramp's AI Token Price Index, which tracks the price of tokens from Anthropic and OpenAI, shows similar slippage. For Anthropic, the price per million tokens went from $2.65 on May 1, 2026 to $0.62 on October 22, 2026, a decline of 77 percent.

In a message shared with Darkbloom participants on the company's Slack channel, Kydo, one of the Eigen Labs researchers working on the distributed inference network, celebrated the network's processing of its trillionth token while attempting to moderate expectations about the project's viability.

"Earnings have been low," said Kydo. "For most providers, I don't expect that it is going to recover soon."

Pointing to the computational prowess of DeekSeek's DeepInfer framework, he explained that structural changes in the inference market have affected the supply of tokens.

"Data centers have become cheaper," he said. "Output per NVIDIA cluster is up ~20x this year, and their margins are wide enough to cut prices 90 percent and still profit at 50 percent."

The flood of tokens in turn has washed away the price advantage Darkbloom had previously. "The market caught up to our price," Kydo explained. "We could go lower, but then most of you would earn even less nothing."

Finally, he said, the inference routing market is changing, with OpenRouter moving away from price-based routing and putting more emphasis on decode speed, quantization, and prefill speed.

For network participants with older or less powerful Mac hardware – those without top of the line Max or Ultra Apple Silicon – "inference won't be worth running," he said.

Kydo went on to outline how Darkbloom aims to adapt to the commoditized token market. This involves analyzing model demand to steer participants toward more sought-after models, and looking at ways to provide other kinds of compute work, like distributed CI/CD jobs.

For Mac hardware, the focus now appears to be on clusters of high-end Mac Studios (now available with up to 512 GB of RAM), using Apple's Remote Direct Memory Access (RDMA) over Thunderbolt.

The CI/CD work, based on the observation that AI agents now make pull requests faster than CI/CD pipelines can handle them, will require sandboxing — something the biz expects to begin testing soon. And Darkbloom also aims to improve the performance of its network (in terms of time to first token) and to look for other token buyers beyond OpenRouter.

"The next stretch will be a fight, and I expect many machines will leave and Slack will get quieter," Kydo concluded. "But those who stay will be first in line when new earning opportunities come along. We are a driven team and well capitalized. We will not give up." ®

Over 25% of people lie to get antibiotics—and their lies aren't even good

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A significant number of patients in the US lie to their doctors to get antibiotics they likely don't need, according to two new studies. While that may not be a startling revelation, the studies drills down on the phenomenon, finding that the lies patients tell are generally bad ones—and yet, they work almost all the time.

In the two linked studies published Friday in JAMA Network Open, a team of researchers led by Alistair Thorpe at the University of Utah analyzed online survey data from over 1,000 adults. They found that over a quarter of people reported having lied to a clinician at least once to get an antibiotic prescription for themselves or a child in their care. Patients lied by either exaggerating symptoms, making up symptoms, or—most often—both.

But interestingly, the lies people told to get antibiotics involved making up or exaggerating symptoms of infections that wouldn't be treated with antibiotics. For example, the most common falsehoods were symptoms associated with colds, such as fever, cough, and sore throat. That's despite the fact that colds are caused by viruses, not bacteria—which antibiotics treat.
