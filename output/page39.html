Shocking: OpenAI head honcho Sam Altman thinks your gaming rig’s RAM would be better off with him

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI bubble continues to inflate and swallow up everything and anything that it needs, even if you, the person, need it more. Now, the chief of OpenAI—the company directly responsible for the shortage in the DRAM market—said it would be better if he had your PC’s RAM, because AI data centers need it.

As spotted by Tom’s Hardware, Sam Altman, the chief executive of OpenAI (the company behind ChatGPT), said on the Cheeky Pint YouTube channel that RAM sitting in so many personal machines around the world, oftentimes at low or no utilization, is “quite terrible” and that AI data centers would make better use of it.

“From a global allocation perspective, actually, it’s quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world’s limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer,” Altman said. To combat the constrained supply of DRAM, which his own company caused through mass purchases of undiced wafers, you would need “maximum utilization,” meaning your PC’s 5 percent RAM usage is, put simply, a waste.

Image via Andrey Matveev/Pexels

“You want maximum utilization in the cloud if you’re trying to deal with this very supply-constrained environment. And somehow many people’s intuition is the opposite of that,” he said.

As Tom’s Hardware points out, Altman’s OpenAI makes the most of its money through closed-source software which, surprise surprise, needs a ton of AI data-center horsepower to run. With RAM being generally unavailable to everyone, now even to AI companies since there are so many of them, Altman seems to want to put his hand in your pocket too. Or rather, wants to dig into your PC and grab all the memory he can find, because why would you, the limited, aging, feeble human, ever want to use personal machines when the infinite ethereal CLOUD can do it for you?

HTTP/3 is faster with some work and help from Cloudflare

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When you're ordering food from your phone, even time spent navigating the app is time wasted. At the P99 2026 virtual conference, Elvis Chidera, a staff engineer at Germany-based global delivery service Delivery Hero, explained how the company moved most of its user base to HTTP/3, and consequently cut its apps’ total end-to-end response time of the home screen by 17 percent.

Chidera, who managed the project, explained that, as promised on the tin, HTTP/3 delivers the web faster than HTTP/2, though it has slow bits that need to be adjusted to work properly.

Uber eats Delivery Hero

Delivery Hero is all but unknown in the US, which is dominated by Uber Eats and DoorDash. But it is a company with global reach outside the US, with an estimated user base of 60 million across Europe, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. Uber is currently acquiring Delivery Hero for $14.8 billion, with plans to unite the company with Uber Eats.

With this purchase, Uber would expand the number of markets where it offers both ride-hailing and delivery services, though not necessarily under the Uber Eats nameplate. Delivery Hero centrally manages separate apps for different regions, all of which it built or acquired. Asians can order poke bowls and sundries through Foodpanda. Turks can order kebabs through Yemeksepeti. Throughout Spain, Italy, Poland, Romania, Ukraine, and much of Africa, the Glovo app is where to ring up the late-night deliveries. And in Latin America, users dial in to PedidosYa to get what they need from nearby farmacias, restaurantes, and mercados.

All these regions have widely varying network connectivity, a problem Delivery Hero wanted to minimize.

Last mile networking was most often the bottleneck. Apps in Pakistan, for instance, could take nearly a second (at p90) to establish a secure handshake with a server. The engineering team looked at HTTP/3 as a way to lower, even eliminate, that latency.

A QUIC-er HTTP

The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) formally introduced the third version of the internet’s Hypertext Transfer Protocol in 2022. HTTP is the application layer protocol that connects browsers and other resources connected to servers over the Internet.

The previous versions of HTTP were built on the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), which offered guaranteed delivery and flow control. In conjunction with the Internet Protocol (IP), TCP/IP built the modern internet.

HTTP/3 replaced TCP with Quick UDP Internet Connection (QUIC), developed by Google, to address some of TCP’s perceived sluggishness as a transfer protocol.

UDP stands for User Datagram Protocol and, unlike TCP, it does not guarantee packet delivery or flow control. UDP is faster because it doesn’t wait around for a signature (QUIC adds in its own flow control on top of UDP).

But the UDP-based HTTP/3 reduces, and sometimes eliminates, the round trips needed to secure connections. The app can cache a session ticket and, on a later connection, use QUIC's 0-RTT capability to start sending eligible application data while the handshake is still underway, rather than waiting for it to finish.

“Essentially, you can eliminate that one second” of latency that plagued slower networks, Chidera said. “You don't want to pay the cost of two round trips just to exchange data, if you could do it faster.”

And unlike HTTP/2, HTTP/3 doesn’t get hung up when packets are lost or delayed. There’s no head-of-line blocking because packets are sent in multiple streams, not just the single string that HTTP/2 offers.

Multitask during initialization

Setting up HTTP/3 turned out to be surprisingly easy, Chidera found. Delivery Hero used Cloudflare’s content delivery network to serve its global user base, so moving to HTTP/3 involved a simple reconfiguration.

Adding HTTP/3 support to the clients, mostly mobile, proved almost as straightforward. Apple has supported HTTP/3 since iOS 15 and most native browsers already support HTTP/3 out of the box.

Android proved a bit trickier. The engineers used Google’s Cronet library for the networking stack, initially wired into their Android apps with the Cronet Transport for OkHttp wrapper to minimize rewriting their existing code, originally written for the OkHttp HTTP client.

Out of the box, Cronet helped support the HTTP/3 rollout, which reduced network-level request failures. But it also came with a significant catch: the engine took approximately 800 milliseconds to initialize at P90, eating into some of HTTP/3's 0-RTT speed benefits. So the team forked the code into their own bridge using a library called OKCronet. They also did some additional engineering work, which involved overlapping Cronet initialization with other background tasks.

Overall, they slashed the P95 latency time from 1.2 seconds to 120 milliseconds, achieving a 10x improvement in read latency.

Choose your congestion control algo wisely

Overall, the engineering team moved 95 percent of the users over to HTTP/3. Those devices that couldn’t use the protocol (largely due to networks blocking UDP traffic) automatically fell back to HTTP/2. Handshake latency between the apps and the server was cut by 51 percent. Pakistan’s response time was cut in half.

One hurdle remained, however. Secondary screens on some of the apps (pages/screens that aren’t the home page) experienced latency issues. These issues only happened in certain markets that were known to be prone to packet loss, such as Pakistan.

Delivery Hero’s engineers attributed the issue to Cloudflare’s choice of the congestion control algorithm for HTTP/3, Cubic, which historically has not handled packet loss well. The engineering team tested some of its traffic with Google HTTP/3-aware servers, which use another congestion protocol, Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR), and did not get hung up on the packet loss.

Delivery Hero is currently working with Cloudflare to resolve this specific congestion algorithm issue, Chidera said.

Crim spent $7K+ on AI services in extortion attacks, then left evidence for all to see

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A n0n ransomware operator tried to break into 23 organizations across 14 countries, but couldn't secure its own front door, leaving its server exposed with all of its methods on view.

The criminals used a mix of new (AI) and old (stolen credentials) tooling during their data-theft spree, and in some cases not only stole and encrypted files, but also deleted data and backups, eliminating victims’ ability to recover their information.

And despite the fear and hype around the breachpocalypse, with AI finding and developing exploits for critical vulnerabilities affecting most organizations across the globe, these crooks did not exploit a single bug to gain entry into any of their victims’ IT environments.

“In every documented compromise in this investigation, access relied on weak or stolen credentials,” said Ensar Seker, chief information security officer of threat intelligence firm SOCRadar.

“What stands out is the combination of AI-assisted development and very conventional entry points,” Seker told The Register. “We found no evidence of vulnerability exploitation in the cases examined.”

The gang breaks into organizations at scale using widespread Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) scanning and automated FortiGate SSL-VPN credential stuffing. To access US-based IT managed services provider ChiBiTek, the crew used a 15-thread residential SOCKS5 proxy pool to test credentials (ip:port:user:password) against FortiGate portals (GET /remote/login).

The operators also gained entry using exposed cloud credentials and reused hosting portal sessions. In one case linked to e-commerce site Fanatics, SOCRadar found that n0n used an exposed AWS access key with permissions to list, read, and delete objects in S3 buckets. The criminals then used this key to pull data from 40 S3 buckets, with up to 110 GB per bucket, and ultimately deleted data, including bank-transaction archives, and purged keys across 12 sensitive buckets.

AI-assisted extortion

While the threat hunters didn’t find any evidence of a fully autonomous attack system, the ransomware operator did use AI to assist with these intrusions, incurring more than $7,216 in AI usage costs by October 22.

Specifically: the criminal or criminals used OpenCode, an open-source AI coding agent, configured with 154 model entries across seven providers. Models included Gemini, Mistral, Nemotron, Llama, Claude, GPT-5.x, Grok, Kimi, GLM, DeepSeek, MiniMax, Qwen, and many, many others, some with safety training removed and others marketed as “uncensored” models.

“The operator used AI to write and debug hundreds of scripts. That shows how AI can help an attacker turn basic access into a broader, tailored operation,” Seker said.

“The destructive behavior is equally significant,” he added. “The operator did more than steal and encrypt files: in some cases, it deleted data and backups, and in one case changed firewall policies to block traffic. That creates a recovery problem that a decryption key alone cannot solve.”

SOCRadar’s threat hunters found the operator’s workspace - a publicly accessible Python HTTP file server hosted in Sweden at 185.157.161[.]148 on port 8877 - last month, and documented the exposed directory in snapshots on September 25 and 26.

“Across those snapshots, we identified 1,719 files, including attack scripts, ransomware source code, extortion infrastructure, and an AI coding agent environment,” Seker said. “This gave us visibility into the operation behind the victim listings, including how the operator prepared attacks and handled stolen data.”

Meet n0n

It also provided a close look at n0n, a newcomer to the data-theft and extortion game that has claimed 108 victims since it emerged last month.

On October 12, the group added 38 maritime, port and logistics organizations across 18 countries to its leak site in a single batch. Researchers have linked at least 21 of them to customers of South Korean maritime software provider CyberLogitec, although the software provider has not confirmed an intrusion.

The crooks set an October 19 deadline for the maritime firms, with ransom demands ranging from $400,000 to $28 million, payable in Bitcoin. After the deadline, however, none of the 38 listings were marked “settled” and none of their payment addresses showed any transaction. All affected organizations’ stolen data was published.

While a handful of the gang’s claimed victims have been marked “honored - data destroyed” on n0n’s leak site, indicating the victim orgs paid the ransom demand, SOCRadar hasn’t validated any transactions on-chain. In its profile details, the security shop rates the gang a 10 out of 10 for "aggressiveness" and a zero out of 10 for “lethality.”

The group is believed to be based in Russia, although the server uncovered by SOCRadar, which it attributed to a single operator or small team, didn’t provide any definitive geographic or national attribution.

Every case that the exposed server documented followed the same lifecycle, moving from initial access to establishing persistence and credential collection via hidden admin accounts disguised as services, covert proxies, credential and session-token theft, mailboxes and emailed multi-factor authentication codes.

The crooks then collected sensitive data and sent everything to the operator’s central storage with the attack impact shifting between data-theft, encryption, destruction, and network disruption, depending on the targeted organization.

After breaking into Transcom, a Netherlands-based financial services provider, the crims sent a secondary ransom alert directly to PayPal's security team, even though the compromised data was only on Transcom's network.

Stupid mistakes and n0n security basics

Finally, the crims send ransom notes, post a countdown clock on the leak site, establish negotiation rooms and emails for victims - and then leak the stolen files or destroy backups when the organizations don’t pay up. In some cases, and to apply increased pressure on victimized companies, n0n also broadcasts automated countdown warnings to businesses’ service providers, clients, and media outlets.

Despite its aggressive tactics, the operator in this case - as with other stupid criminals The Register has gleefully documented in earlier stories - still struggles with basic security hygiene.

“There is a striking gap between operational ambition and security discipline,” Seker said. “The operator invested thousands of dollars in AI services while leaving its own workspace exposed. The locker also contained hardcoded encryption keys, with evidence suggesting reuse across victims. An operation can have serious destructive capability while still making fundamental mistakes.”®

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 will remove real-world footage of the 2022 Seoul crush incident after severe backlash in South Korea. Bereaved families issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of these videos, and Activision has since vowed to cut them from the title.

The game is set in the Korean Peninsula, and real-world footage of several events is shown in the opening cutscene as a means to ground its narrative in reality.

Among the videos are scenes of the 2022 Seoul crush incident, which happened during a Halloween celebration on Oct. 29 in Itaewon, when a crowd suddenly surged and began to trample or crush those within it. Around 160 deaths were confirmed, most of whom were young adults, making it one of the deadliest events in South Korean history.

Families of those who perished or were injured in the incident issued a statement yesterday condemning the inclusion of this footage, telling the BBC they were “appalled.”

“We cannot help but be appalled that the chaotic and desperate scene, in which many citizens at the site performed CPR to save one another and victims lay on the freezing street, was used to create a scene in a game,” they said.

“Using such an event as material for a game made for entertainment is in itself highly inappropriate and senseless,” they added.

Modern Warfare 4 is largely set in the Korean Peninsula during a “New Korean War.” Image via Activision

Activision has since responded to the backlash, promising to cut the scenes entirely in the game’s next update on Nov. 12. The company told the BBC it never “intended to reference or trivialize the Itaewon disaster or any other real-world tragedy in any way, shape or form,” but merely used real-world footage to frame the game’s fictional story.

The Modern Warfare franchise, both the original trilogy and these new games, is well known for its shocking and mortifying scenes. However, the difference here is that, while the most horrific moments in the games were all fictional, Infinity Ward used real footage of a tragedy. We can debate whether showing it here was distasteful or dehumanizing or reductive, but I can see where these families are coming from.

If we start drawing lines in the sand too much, though, art will have no way to push forward, and addressing reality will become impossible. Someone somewhere is going to be inevitably offended by your story, your characters, and what you’re saying, or rather, what they’re saying about the state of the world, but I think that is ultimately a fair price to pay.
