Three Latin Music Power Players on Being Managers in Today’s Industry: ‘It’s The Work That Goes Unseen’

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last week, during Billboard Latin Music Week, three Latin music Power Players took the stage to discuss the role of managers in today’s industry. They were Jorge “Pepo” Ferradas, president and CEO of FPM Entertainment; Rebeca León, CEO and co-founder of Lionfish Entertainment; and Walter Kolm, founder and CEO of WK Entertainment — all managers with decades of experience who have helped launch the careers of some of the biggest Latin stars.

During the conversation — titled “The Power Players: Managers Edition” and moderated by Pamela Bustios, Billboard Latin/Español’s senior charts and data analyst — the three executives reflected on the challenges they face in their careers and the selfless role of working to fulfill other people’s dreams.

“Being a manager is being a guide, having answers to many questions, and it’s super difficult. I don’t know if it’s for everyone,” said León, who has represented superstars like Juanes and Rosalía and whose current roster includes Anitta, Danny Ocean, Tokischa and Aria Vega.

Kolm, manager of Carlos Vives, Morat, Emilia and Xavi, among others, highlighted the importance of having a strategy to achieve specific goals. “I always tell artists: ‘You pay us to tell you what we think, not what you want to hear,'” he said, emphasizing the responsibility of always guiding with honesty.

Meanwhile, Ferradas, whose roster includes Camilo, Evaluna Montaner, Rauw Alejandro and Rels B, stressed the importance of reclaiming the manager’s work: “It’s the work that goes unseen. It’s clear that it’s not magic — the locomotive of the train is the music, the song and the artist. [But] behind that, there’s a team.”

Below are some of the best quotes from the panel, which took place on Oct. 21 at the Faena Forum in Miami Beach.

On being a manager:

León: For me, being a manager is having vision… Visualizing how the artist will grow, how that will develop. Always thinking months, years ahead; if you’re in the here and now, you are late. Being a manager is being a guide, having answers to many questions, and it’s super difficult. I don’t know if it’s for everyone, because you’re serving someone else’s dreams, so there’s a personal sacrifice — you have to set aside your own path to help this other person achieve their dreams.

Kolm: What Rebeca says is key. I would add that we’re also strategists; that’s fundamental in an artist’s career. Sometimes artists think the manager is the one who finds them dates or secures a show… No, the manager is the one who designs a strategy to achieve a goal, and that’s what I believe we are. We’re strategists with years of experience in the industry who understand from our own experiences, from studying and from observing the markets, which paths to take. We don’t always get it right, but at least we’re clear about the direction.

Ferradas: It’s the work that goes unseen. It’s clear that it’s not magic — the locomotive of the train is the music, the song and the artist. But everything that happens around it seems like magic — it seems like no one’s doing it. It seems like no one booked a good hotel, no one hired a quality vehicle, no one prepared the venue, no one ensured the sound and lights were ready. And it seems like no one brought the team of people who enter and sing the artist’s songs. Behind that, there’s a team. And leading that team is someone who plans the strategy and drives it forward. So I think the manager’s role is very important.

On the manager-artist relationship:

Ferradas: When we ask for discipline and work time, it’s because we know it works — it’s not about pressure. In the manager-artist relationship, there are always two questions: The artist will ask themselves why they hired this person, and we’ll ask ourselves why we’re spending all day pressuring you to do something that will benefit you more than it will benefit us. So I think it’s important for us today to leave a small mark by thanking the artists who work and have worked with us for their trust, but also by asking for some recognition of our work.

On the essential elements for building a sustainable career as a manager:

León: You have to have the ability to not take things personally. You have to be able to get rejected by somebody and have dinner with them the same day. You have to just have a very tunnel vision of like, I’m going after this goal, and I’m not going to let anybody distract me, and I’m not going to let any of the politics or the games or any of these things that can cloud your judgment. You have to be very laser-focused. And that requires a lot of growth within yourself… There’s never a relaxed moment. There’s no days off. I’m very grateful to have this beautiful — I think it’s a beautiful life, but it’s a very specific one.

On knowing when an artist is ready to conquer new markets:

Kolm: Everything starts with the music — let’s not forget that this is a business of music and songs. The most important thing is, when the artist works on their repertoire, to see if that repertoire is one we believe has potential. Because many artists think everything has to work everywhere, and that’s not the case. That’s why, going back to the manager’s work, we guide the artist. I always tell artists: “You pay us to tell you what we think, not what you want to hear.” And that’s what it’s about — helping them see how to create that repertoire with good writers, with good producers, to first have the product. And of course, attitude is important — whether the artist is committed… has discipline. For me, that’s the key word: discipline.

Inside Redeye’s 2026 Label Hang, Where Indie Execs Look to the Future

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As Redeye Worldwide, the global distribution and music services company, celebrated its 30th anniversary, distributed labels, retail store owners, digital music service providers and staff from both Redeye and its parent company, Exceleration Music, converged on the company’s home in Durham, N.C., for its annual Label Hang. 

In all, almost 250 people attended the three-day conference, which ran from Oct. 13-15. During the gathering, Exceleration and Redeye executives laid out their vision of what supplementary services they want to offer their labels while helping them achieve economies of scale. The conference served as a forum that allowed attendees to listen and exchange ideas on how to deal with industry issues, opportunities and problems.

Exceleration — which bought Redeye and its in-house labels Yep Roc and Sundazed in 2023 —was born in early 2020 amid the COVID-19 pandemic — a time when a lot of private equity money was floating into the industry looking for assets to buy. At the time, indie label owners were stressed about whether they should sell or not, while institutional investors were even willing to buy income streams, Glen Barros, managing partner of Exceleration, told Billboard on the last day of the conference 

“I was offended that suddenly music was an investment commodity,” Barros said. “To buy an artist’s work and throw it into a portfolio felt wrong. But anybody from the music industry knows soul and heart is where the music comes from. So those investors were missing that, at the heart of the music business, it is run by humans for humans.” 

Barros, who spent 25 years of his career helming and helping to build Concord into an indie powerhouse, decided after he left that company to start raising capital to purchase music companies and assets. 

He would take a different approach from most of the incoming institutional investors. Where it made sense, Exceleration would keep the original label entrepreneurs in place after the acquisition — if they wanted to become a part of the Exceleration team.

“The important part of the Redeye Label Hang is it brings people and teams together, and it celebrates the humanity of running businesses,” Barros said. In his approach to running a company that invests in music assets, he explained, “you have to take care of all your constituencies, your employees, artists, customers, and investors.”

The Redeye conference was scheduled with that approach in mind, seeking to enhance communications between the distributor and its labels. Throughout the conference’s programming, a slate of services provided by Redeye and Exceleration that the distributed labels can draw upon was presented. 

Conference Panel Discussions

A panel on the last day presented by Exceleration Partners entitled “Growing Your Toolkit: Marketing Service Opportunities” featured executives from third-party companies talking about what services they could provide to labels, or that labels should be aware of and can make their own arrangements to use if they so choose. Those companies included Ochre, which provides labels and artists with a direct-to-consumer infrastructure; DISCO, which provides a forum for labels and songwriters to present their music to film/TV and commercial supervisors for possibly lucrative synch licensing opportunities; and Third Chair, which scours social media on behalf of its client music rights holders to find unlicensed uses of their music, and then pursues compensation on behalf of their clients for those unauthorized uses.

Other issues touched upon during the conference throughout various panels and segmented group discussions included sharing ideas on the best PR firms for different music genres; how to come up with a list of influencers who can support each genre; how social media can be utilized to build fan engagement; and why the music press is still an important tool. As Exceleration’s head of global marketing Darin Soler put it, “Press is not dead; you don’t want to forget press but use other social media as well. Both pop up in [Google] searches.” 

The double-edged sword of AI was discussed throughout various panels. In a group discussion, one participant called upon one of the label executives in the audience to explain how he uses AI to improve his business. And the last panel of the conference — “AI Music, Navigating Fan Creations, Artist/Label Protections & Fraud” — was dedicated to the topic.

On the conference’s opening day, Redeye staff fielded a panel entitled, “DSP Download and Ask Us Anything: Trends, Tactics and Your Questions Answered.” That type of exercise is emblematic of Redeye’s overall approach. As Redeye co-founder Tor Hansen said at the convention, “It’s always been our goal to continue to grow and improve by listening to our partners and team to best understand their needs and how we best can help achieve their goals.” 

In looking back on the company’s anniversary, Hansen continued, “It’s hard to believe it’s been 30 years, but in many ways, we still feel like we’re just getting started… We’re looking forward to much more to come!” 

Company Roots

Redeye co-founder Glenn Dicker offered an observation reflecting both Hansen’s philosophy and the Excelertion doctrine. As he told Billboard, “After 30 years, one of the biggest things that I think about is that even though things have changed dramatically in that time, in many ways the things that were important in our early days remain our priority today. At Redeye, we value people and relationships first and foremost.  With a strong and growing foundation of some of the greatest music out there coupled with our passionate team, we intend to continue to provide an environment where our partners can remain independent and thrive doing so.” 

That same people-first axiom applies to how Exceleration came to be. Barros said he built the team even before he began putting together his plans for building the business. “The partnership is core,” said Barros, who added he began first conversing with former Epitaph Records GM Dave Hansen (no relation to Tor Hansen)  about all the changes going on in the industry and where the opportunities were. From there, it was time to build a team, including John Burk, who worked for Barros at Concord, heading up A&R before becoming president of the label. “We knew Charles [Caldas] was leaving Merlin [where he had been CEO) and we thought, ‘Who else?’ And that’s how former InGrooves Music Group executive vp Amy [Dietz] came up.”

The five Excleration partners had all known one another through participating as board or committee members of A2IM, Merlin and the Recording Academy.  “The way we divide up the responsibilities is I handle finance; Jon handles the creative side; Charles has global; Amy has distribution and Dave has marketing, but we collaborate and work across each of the areas,” Barros said. 

Barros also applies his “people first” philosophy to acquisition deals. Distinguishing itself from institutional investors, Exceleration usually doesn’t chase iconic music deals because there is often a lot of money chasing those types of deals, Barros said, “Whereas we tend to do deals where the structure is more creative. ”

When Exceleration looks at a deal, he explained, “besides asking, ‘Is it great music?’ we also ask, ‘Can we add value to it… and can we do a deal that serves every constituency?'”  

Exceleration’s Acquisitions and Investments

So far Exceleration has acquired, made investments in or formed strategic partnerships with more than a dozen labels and/or artists, including Alligator, Ace Fu, Azadi, Bloodshot, Candid, Cooking Vinyl, DangerBird Records, Down The Road, Heroic, Kill Rock Stars, Mack Avenue, Mom+Pop, Plus One, Reach Records, SideOneDummy, Sundazed, the Ray Charles Foundation/Tangerine Records, the Ryuichi Sakamoto estate and Yep Roc Records. In every deal done, “the mandate is to  figure out how to do the right things for those music rights,” Barros said. 

Barros declined to provide the terms of those deals or the source of funding, but financial sources tell Billboard that Exceleration has raised $300 million or more from family offices and other institutional investors and has so far deployed about $175 million to $200 million through the various deals it has struck.

Letting Indies ‘Do What They Were Made to Do’

While the company’s purpose for investing in music assets is to seek financial returns for Exceleration and its investors, another big part of the vision is to serve the needs of the indie sector and to help indie music companies do things that they may not do well. As Exceleration partner Dave Hansen puts it, “Our goal is to build services or strike partnerships with music service providers for the ‘indie-verse’ like trying to come up with things that can help indie labels scale advertising or better participate in the D2C [direct to consumer] business.” 

For those areas, Exceleration created a data management team and a high-level marketing team. “We can help indie labels with data management so that they can make good choices to run their businesses,” Barros said, adding that those types of services “let indies do what they were made to do, concentrate on A&R.”

But that’s not all Exceleration offers. “Along the way, the idea got bigger because as we grew we saw more possibilities. We said, let’s expand this and truly serve the indie sector’s infrastructure. And that’s where Redeye comes along. We didn’t do this to do distribution, but to expand our services, just like we bought Dave out to get his share of [Independent Record Pressing],” which provides Exceleration with a stake in vinyl manufacturing. 

The vinyl format remains a lucrative but fraught part of the indie music sector. It’s important to have the right amount of product available for distribution at the right time — when consumers want it — or else revenue will be missed out on; or, even worse, labels will be forced to deal with costly product returns. “We really believe in physical, but we know that a lot of labels struggle with financing physical, so we are looking for ways to mitigate that,” Barros said.  

“So now we have three buckets to serve the indie sector: financial servicing, where we can ask, ‘Do you need funding or do you want to sell?’, operational help or provide administrative services,” Barros said. “Also, we can help indie labels with data management so that they can make good choices to run their businesses.” 

Exceleration offers its help not only to the labels it has acquired and/or formed financial and/or operational partnerships with, but also to the 150 or so active labels distributed by Redeye, a global distributor servicing both physical and digital music formats; and many more distributed labels through its Scandinavian subsidiary Border Music. Depending on whether deals are sourced from its U.S., Scandinavian or Australian operations, Redeye handles all kinds of distribution deals, with some being global for both physical and digital, some being physical only, some being only for the U.S., and others covering only foreign territories.  

Consequently, it’s hard to easily describe how much of Redeye’s distribution business is U.S. versus global or physical versus digital. Most of the statistics Billboard heard on this topic during the conference had U.S. revenue at around 50% to 55% physical and 45% to 50% digital, while approximately 55% to 60% of total revenue is U.S. versus 40% to 45% international, though Exceleration executives say the latter is growing faster. 

Among the labels distributed by Redeye are: Beggars Banquet, Mom + Pop, Domino, Daptone, New West, Warp, Barsuk and Double Double Whammy; while some of the top acts Redeye distributes include Radiohead, Arctic Monkeys, Wet Leg, Thee Sacred Souls, Madvillain, Alex G, Title Fight, The Darkness, Alison Krauss, Ray Charles, Elliot Smith, Deacon Blue and Beach Bunny.  

An Indie Distribution Powerhouse

Headquartered in Hillsborough, N.C., a suburb of Durham, Redeye has an 80,000-square-foot distribution center packed to the rafters with physical product. While the distributor has a bigger presence in physical product than many of its competitors, the company arrived early in the digital distribution space as one of the first to embrace the long-defunct eMusic.

Besides its distribution center, Redeye also has offices in New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, San Francisco, London, Berlin, Sydney and Gothenburg, Sweden, where the company gained another distribution center after acquiring Border Music at the end of 2019. Exceleration has staff working remotely, which “allows us to hire the best people, so it doesn’t matter where they are,” Barros said. 

While company executives declined to provide financials, Billboard estimates that Redeye generates about $125 million in annual revenue from its distributed labels, which includes Exceleration-owned and -partnered imprints. 

One of the reasons Exceleration believes it can help indie labels better manage their businesses is because of the in-house labels it owns. “We work with our own labels in a lot of different ways so we understand the pressures that indie label entrepreneurs are under,” said Dietz. On the other hand, she added, “We are not here to tell anyone how to run their business. We are here to help them.” 

She added that the Label Hang and exchange of ideas that happen there will help Exceleration and Redeye focus on what services they should build to provide for their label partners next. “Exceleration is about the point of view of music and people, and everything follows from there,” Dietz said. 

As Exceleration looks forward, Barros added, “You will see us invest in music and record labels and publishing, but we will also invest in services to bring more to the table for artists and labels. We want to build something for the better that is here for the long haul for the indie world.”

The legendary actor, perhaps best known for his work on Star Trek as Captain Picard, has passed away, according to a statement posted to his Instagram, which was first reported by The Hollywood Reporter. The statement also disclosed that theYorkshire-born actor had been living with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.

Born in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, in 1940, Stewart joined the Royal Shakespeare Company at the age of 26 before becoming a household name as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation from 1987 to 1994. He later revisited the role in four feature films and the three-season Star Trek: Picard series. He also played Professor Charles Xavier in X-Men. He was knighted in 2010, and published his memoir, Making It So, in 2023.

“We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles,” the statement reads, noting that he was “in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny” after “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia.” It added that “He will be remembered for the dignity, compassion and humanity he brought to every role,” and asked for donations to Alzheimer’s research and caregiver charities in lieu of flowers.

Notorious B.I.G. Estate Lawsuit Ends With Faith Evans Sending $14M to Voletta Wallace’s Trust

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A judge has ruled that Faith Evans must immediately distribute nearly $14 million in profits from The Notorious B.I.G.’s estate to the trust set up by his late mother, Voletta Wallace.

The Tuesday (Oct. 27) decision ends a court dispute between Evans and Wayne Barrow, who managed Biggie (Christopher Wallace) and was named Voletta’s legal trustee upon her death in February 2025. Barrow is still involved in a separate lawsuit with Biggie’s son, CJ Wallace, who’s challenging the terms of his grandmother’s will.

Barrow initiated litigation last July against Evans, Biggie’s widow and the sole manager of his valuable intellectual property portfolio after Voletta’s death. Barrow said the trust was entitled to a 25% share of estate earnings, including profits from a landmark Primary Wave catalog deal. The R&B singer countered that Barrow did not have the right legal documentation that entitled him to this money.

A judge in Delaware’s Chancery Court sided with Barrow in August, ruling that the trust ultimately did ink the correct paperwork to take over Voletta’s share of estate earnings in December 2025. In Thursday’s follow-up ruling, however, Chancellor Kathaleen McCormick said Barrow’s financial stake kicked in earlier than that.

“All economic rights associated with Ms. Wallace’s member interest in the LLC transferred to her estate upon her death on February 21, 2025,” wrote the judge. “The Trust, as the ultimate beneficiary, is entitled to distributions accrued as of the date of Ms. Wallace’s death.”

Voletta’s share of estate profits running from February to December 2025 totals $12.5 million. Up until now, that money has been set aside and is sitting within the estate. Chancellor McCormick ordered Evans to immediately distribute the full amount, plus interest, to Barrow, who will then donate it to the Christopher Wallace Memorial Foundation, per the terms of Voletta’s will.

Barrow’s attorney Jay Freiberg told Billboard that the distributions with interest will total nearly $14 million. “We are pleased with the latest order from the Chancellor,” said Freiberg. “It continues the Chancellor’s decisions recognizing Faith Evans’ misbehavior regarding Notorious Big LLC after the death of Mama Wallace.”

“Hopefully she will [pay] and, equally important, stop fighting the inevitable result properly planned by Mama Wallace — that the trust is a co-equal member of the LLC with Ms. Evans, and entitled to all that comes with this status,” added Freiberg.

Evans’ attorney declined to comment on the ruling Thursday (Oct. 29).

Separately, Biggie’s son CJ is suing Barrow in Pennsylvania to change Voletta’s will. A previous version of his grandmother’s estate plan named CJ and Barrow co-trustees, but the will was changed a few weeks before her death to give Barrow sole control.

CJ is now alleging that Barrow manipulated Voletta into rewriting the will on her deathbed. Barrow denies this and says CJ’s lawsuit is being engineered by Evans, which Evans’ lawyers deny. The case remains ongoing.
