Legend is just one of the bold-faced names Acton will bring out on the road with her in the lead-up to the Nov. 3 midterm election in which she is facing off against tech mogul and former DOGE co-lead Vivek Ramaswamy. Legend’s support for Acton comes as Ramaswamy’s campaign has come under fierce bipartisan pushback for a campaign ad in which Acton’s long-estranged mother repeatedly calls her daughter a “liar” while insinuating that Acton’s claims that she was sexually abused by her stepdad, and that the family was homeless and lived in a tent for a period, are untrue.

Her former boss, Ohio’s Republican Gov, Mike DeWine, also weighed in, saying the commercial — which the Ramaswamy campaign has poured $4 million into airing in the final week of the race — “deeply, deeply troubling,” saying the campaign “needs to take it down.”

Trump and Vance, however, have given it their full-throated support, with Trump on Thursday repeatedly defending it, including in a Truth Social post, writing, “Vivek Ramaswamy put up a very powerful Ad about a mother saying that her daughter, Amy Acton, who is running for Governor of Ohio, is a liar, and much worse.” The President went on to call the backlash a “well-coordinated attack” from the press, even as he lambasted fellow Republican DeWine for his comments and encouraged Ramaswamy to keep the ad up.

During a visit to his native Cincinnati earlier this week, Vance said it was “not really my concern” if the ad was appropriate, adding, “I’m a little bit disgusted when I see Republicans across the country who are whining more about that advertisement than they are about what the congressional Democrats and other Democrats want to do to the United States of America.”

Trump has been hitting the campaign trail very hard over the past few weeks, including visiting deep-red states where Republicans typically feel safe during midterms, with polling showing him reaching historically low approval numbers as Americans struggle with high gas and goods prices amid the president’s unpopular war in Iran and costly tariff regime.

Fashion Is the Future of Technology: How V of BTS, Navy Blue, Kelela and Nothing Are Bridging Tech and Culture — ‘Find Joy in the Little Things’

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Technology and fashion have always been intertwined, but as more of the devices we use become things we wear, the line between the two is getting harder to distinguish.

Headphones are an obvious example. Once primarily functional accessories, they’ve become part of how people present themselves in public. Whether worn over the ears, resting around the neck, or sitting on a purse or a hat, they can complement an outfit, communicate personal taste and signal an affinity for a particular brand or community. Like sneakers, jewelry, socks or a favorite bag, they’re increasingly part of the visual language through which people express themselves and story tell.

And it makes sense. When technology becomes something you physically wear, its design becomes part of your appearance. You’re not just choosing a device for what it can do. You’re choosing something that will exist on your body, in public, alongside everything else you use to communicate who you are and what you’re about. And now you’re thinking, “Will these headphones match most of my looks? Will these headphones bring my fits together or take away from them?”

We’re at a point where so many headphones have amazing noise-canceling, transparency mode, great audio quality and mics. Some may be better than others in some categories but the differences — no matter if the brands like it or not — are becoming few and far between.

That raises a bigger question about the future of technology: As products become increasingly capable, will their cultural significance matter as much as their technical capabilities?

At Nothing’s New Noise event in New York, that question extended beyond the products on display. The event brought together technology, fashion and music, with conversations featuring Kelela and Navy Blue — two artists whose approaches to music and fashion offer different perspectives on where culture is heading.

The connection isn’t simply that musicians are wearing headphones or that technology companies are borrowing from fashion. It’s that technology, fashion and music are increasingly intertwined forms of self-expression. The products we carry, the clothes we wear and the music we listen to all contribute to how we construct our identities and how people construct identities for us. And as technology becomes more visible in our everyday lives, the way it looks, feels and fits into our personal worlds becomes harder to separate from its function.

For Nothing’s chief brand officer, Charlie Smith, that relationship is already apparent in the way people wear headphones.

“Headphones are like wearing a scarf or a necklace,” he says. “I think it says something about who you are as a person, in the same way that the brands of clothes you choose to wear do.”

Smith, who previously worked at fashion house Loewe, sees an opportunity for technology to develop the same kind of recognizable identity that fashion brands have cultivated for decades. He argues that many devices have become visually interchangeable, creating space for companies to distinguish themselves through more expressive design.

Philipp Von Lintel, who works on Nothing’s product design, makes the comparison even more directly: “The Headphone 1 is almost like a sneaker, but for your head.”

It’s a simple analogy with larger implications. Sneakers aren’t just designed around comfort and performance. Their silhouettes, materials and cultural associations can make them objects of desire, personal expression and community, as someone who collects with close to 250+ pairs, trust me I know. Headphones can occupy similar territory because they’re functional objects that people also wear as part of their everyday look.

But fashion’s influence on technology isn’t just about making devices look better. It’s also about the experience of interacting with them, the materials, the craftsmanship and the consideration behind their design.

Navy Blue, who describes himself as detail-oriented, says those considerations are what make products meaningful to him. “I think there are a lot of like-minded people who find joy in the little things, like how their T-shirt might have a single stitch, and so forth,” he says.

He believes consumers will increasingly want to understand how products are made, including where their materials come from. In that sense, technology’s relationship with fashion could involve more than aesthetics. It could also mean bringing the values associated with craftsmanship and intentional design into the products people use every day.

And this is where the conversation about technology starts to intersect with the future of music.

The connection between fashion, technology and music is also reflected in the people brands choose to represent them. At Nothing’s New Noise event, the company announced V of BTS as its new global brand ambassador alongside the launch of Headphone (1) Pro.

The partnership illustrates how technology companies are increasingly looking to music and culture to give their products an identity beyond their technical capabilities. V isn’t simply representing a pair of headphones; he’s bringing his own creative identity and global cultural influence into the way the product is presented to audiences.

Artists have long used their visual identities to complement their sound, turning clothing, photography, performance and album artwork into extensions of their creative worlds. But as Kelela points out, that relationship only works when the visual expression has something meaningful behind it.

“Sometimes it’s really great because the world-building you do around a certain type of sound helps guide you toward understanding what somebody means,” she says. “It helps you receive the sonic outcome even more.”

She also cautions that a strong visual identity can become an empty shell if there’s nothing substantive behind it musically.

That tension matters to both industries. Fashion can give technology a stronger identity, just as visual presentation can deepen the experience of music. But neither can rely on appearances alone. The design must mean something, and the music has to deliver something worth hearing.

For artists like Kelela and Navy Blue, that connection between appearance, identity and creative substance is part of a broader conversation about where culture is going. Their perspectives at New Noise offered a reminder that the future isn’t just about the next device or the next sound. It’s also about the people creating new ways for audiences to experience both.

Accordion Icons: Which Work Best?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

So what icon is the most reliable to show expansion and collapsing for accordions? Is there really any noticeable difference between carets, chevrons, arrows, and triangles?

Well, research shows that there is some difference indeed. But what’s more interesting is that split menus are harmful, why it's better to not have icons and text labels link to different actions, and why the caret is the safest icon choice. Let’s dive in!

No Icon = Opening The Page

The findings aren’t very surprising: without any icon in use, users expect that tapping on the menu item would take them directly to a new page. Users often struggle with custom icons as they don't clearly signal that they'll open an accordion. And people tend to tap fairly equally on the icon and on the label for most icons.

Many designs (like the one below) tend to slightly overcomplicate things by adding arrows and carets pointing everywhere, depending on the state of the accordion. But typically we don't need to use an arrow to indicate a link to the category.

The rule of thumb is very simple: if it’s a link, the easiest and most effective way is to simply underline it — without any arrow, caret, or other icon. Perhaps it’s not particularly exciting, but it works flawlessly every single time.

Confusing Behavior With Arrows ▶ and Split Buttons

Using a right-facing arrow icon ▶ isn’t associated with an expectation of going directly to a new page. And having the same icon pointing down ▼ doesn’t necessarily imply that an accordion is going to open on tap/click.

It’s a good idea to avoid split buttons for accordions — where the text label links directly to a page, but the icon opens the accordion. Instead, it’s much more reliable to allow users to tap on a main category and expose the menu; and to access the category page, we add a link to it inside the menu.

Plus +/− and Arrows ↓/↑ Are Understood, But Can Be Misunderstood

For accordions, we typically use +/− or arrows ↓/↑ to signal expanded and collapsed states, and typically it works well. However, both have many different interpretations. Plus + can mean anything from saving for later to adding to cart to zooming in, and can be particularly confusing for multi-level navigation, with many levels open at the same time.

Arrows ↓/↑ can mean scrolling or sliding or finding the next match, but also a jump to a particular portion of a page. Both plus and arrows are well-understood and work, but depending on the design, can be confusing — mostly because they are overloaded.

Wrapping Up

The safe choice is to use the caret/triangle pointing down ▼ by default, and change it to a caret pointing up or a caret pointing sideways ▶ on interaction. If you need an alternative, then +/- typically work well, too.

But we certainly don’t need any further icons or arrows to indicate where the links go. The same goes for split menus, which often produce more mistakes than help. Instead, we have one button or dropdown that does exactly one thing — shows all options and allows users to drill down.

And if you need a direct link, we link the title directly to the page and underline it. More often than not, simpler is better.

There’s a New Report on the Health of Global Nightlife Culture — How Did New York Rate?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amsterdam-based consulting and advocacy agency VibeLab has released a new report assessing the health of nightlife culture in six major global cities across six continents.

The report, the Nighttime Culture Index, was first presented Thursday (Oct. 22) at ADE, the dance industry gathering celebrating its 30-year anniversary this week in Amsterdam. The Index is a joint project by ADE and VibeLab, which sent representatives to Amsterdam, Lagos in Nigeria, New York, São Paulo, Seoul and Sydney to conduct research. See the Nighttime Culture Index here.

Major takeaways from the report include the disparity in the price of a night out across cities, with nightclub entry costing approximately an hour of minimum-wage work in Sydney, nine hours in São Paulo and 25 hours in Lagos. (These figures exclude transportation, drinks and other costs.)

Additionally, inequality persists, with female and gender-diverse artists making up just 27% to 32% of line-ups across the six cities. Experts across five cities also rated diversity in venue ownership and leadership as poor. Safety is also cited as an issue, with respondents reporting feeling less safe in the streets surrounding nightlife areas. The issue is particularly acute in São Paulo, where 73% of respondents feel safe inside venues, compared with just 11% in the streets. In Lagos, the figures are 74% and 20%, respectively.

In New York specifically, the Index cites the main challenge in nightlife as how expensive it is, saying that “as with many major cities, cost was regularly raised as a barrier to entry for both promoters — particularly venue hire and permits — and audiences. It was suggested that the DIY scene was thriving, but costs were also blamed for making careers within the industry difficult to sustain. Many called for more funding for marginalized groups to put on events, as well as for informal and DIY spaces. Gentrification threatening venues were also raised regularly, as well as a need for more sustainable wages for workers at night.”

In terms of what’s working well in New York, the report says the city’s legendarily vibrant nightlife culture has something for everyone, “with 94% agreeing that there is a variety and diversity of different scenes, styles, sounds and genres in New York. When asked for up-and-coming sounds and artists in New York, no artist, sound or style was named twice, reflecting the diversity [on] offer in the city. Our focus group felt strongly that New York was a place where new artists, sounds and styles can emerge and that New York’s nightlife community has the power to shape its scene.”

The Index also notes that in terms of New York venues, “survey respondents rated free or outdoor events in public space almost as highly as clubs when asked which were the most important nightlife spaces in the city.”

“But despite their popularity,” the Index continues, “promoters recognized the financial and legal risks of holding DIY parties — and one participant said she would be concerned inviting Black attendees to a DIY party for fear of ‘overpolicing’ if it were shut down. However, audiences were generally positive about nightlife inclusivity, with 84% of survey respondents agreeing that people from diverse communities, races, social classes and other parts of the city participate in nighttime culture.”

Additionally, New York received a 30/100 on festivals with sustainability commitments, 45/100 on public funding for nighttime culture and 74.5/100 on education pathways into nightlife careers. Two major trends found in the city are block party gatherings and the jazz improv genre.

In total, the Index analyzed more than 12,000 event line-ups, assessed more than 3,000 venues and researched nearly 30,000 non-music cultural events. It had more than 700 respondents to its public survey, 67 focus group participants and 14 nighttime culture experts. 

Working with local coordinators in each city, VibeLab also employed desk research, expert interviews and data collection to assess trends in each. The Index assesses each city based on creative ecology, space and infrastructure, diversity and representation, community and belonging, policy and civic health and sustainability and environment.

“Behind the numbers is a night culture that is thriving, even as the business around it is under immense pressure, VibeLab co-founder Mirik Milan said in a statement. “New ideas often come out of crisis. Let’s give nightlife culture the space to do what it does best: bring people together.”

“You can’t have a nighttime industry without a nighttime culture, and that culture depends on people being able to take part,” added ADE managing director Jan-Willem van de Ven. “When a club night costs a day’s wages or more in some cities, that is a question for all of us, including the industry.”
