An open approach to securing AI

AI spans many providers. Enterprise teams combine open and proprietary models, development tools, data platforms, and infrastructure. Shared security approaches can help organizations evaluate and protect those systems while preserving their ability to choose the right technology for the business.

Security has to cover the system around the model

A model assessment evaluates the model. But enterprise teams also need to understand the system around it: the packages it depends on, the tools it can reach, the data it can access, and the actions it can take.

That is why security needs to span development and production, with evidence that controls work as agents, models, and connections change.

A hidden ocean change near Antarctica could have global consequences

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The rapid decline of Antarctic sea ice since 2016 has triggered a dramatic shift in a crucial part of Earth’s climate system, scientists say.

In a paper published Wednesday, researchers documented a sharp salinity increase in a vast layer of the Southern Ocean, extending from 2,300 to 4,000 feet deep and encircling Antarctica. The shift reverses decades of freshening and echoes ocean changes usually associated with major global climate shifts over millennia but is now happening in just a few years.

The switch from a decades-long trend of freshening water to increasing salinity has implications that could extend beyond Antarctica. The layer, known as Antarctic Intermediate Water, spreads northward into the Indian, Pacific, and Atlantic oceans, transporting heat, carbon, oxygen, and nutrients toward the tropics, and even across the equator, said co-author Bishakhdatta Gayen, a researcher and professor at the University of Melbourne and at the Centre for Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences in Bengaluru, India.

We're in the Waning Gibbous phase of the lunar cycle, which means the Moon appears more than half illuminated but is gradually getting smaller as we move towards the Third Quarter Moon.

What is today's Moon phase?

As of Wednesday, Oct. 28, NASA's Daily Moon Guide tracker tells us the Moon is in the Waning Gibbous phase, with 94% of its surface visible.

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Tycho Crater, Copernicus Crater, and the Mare Imbrium. If you have binoculars you'll also see the Posidonius Crater, the Mare Humorum, and the Grimaldi Basin. Finally, if you have a telescope, you'll also see the Apollo 12 landing spot, the Rupes Altai, and the Caucasus Mountains.

When is the next Full Moon?

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Life ingredients probably didnt ride in on a comet, new study shows

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When comets hit Earth billions of years ago, they pelted the planet too fast and too steep for them to have delivered fragile organic molecules, according to a new study. 

Using advances in computer simulations, a University of Cambridge-led team of researchers ran scenarios of comet strikes, comet interactions with an atmosphere, and gravity's influence on comet movements through space to determine whether they could have brought the chemical ingredients for life to Earth. 

Rather than look at a bunch of important molecules, the researchers focused on one in particular: hydrogen cyanide, or HCN. Made of one hydrogen, one carbon, and one nitrogen atom, it's a simple molecule commonly carried on comets. They found that the immense heat would have literally vaporized most of those molecules on impact, challenging a theory on the origins of life that has existed for about a century.

Even in the rare case that HCN survived a crash to Earth, it would not have come in large enough quantities, said Richard Anslow, lead author of the paper, which appears in the journal Scientific Reports.

"It would really have to be a one-in-a-million occurrence," said Anslow, a Cambridge astronomer, in a statement. "We can't rule out for certain that it happened that way. But it seems to be a really rare occurrence — at best, a one-off fluke." 

SEE ALSO: Webb spotted a subtle difference in the interstellar comet's ice

Comets are chunks of ice, dust, and rock left over from when the solar system began about 4.6 billion years ago. Most range from a few hundred yards to dozens of miles wide, and they travel on long, stretched-out orbits. When a comet swings close to the sun, its heat converts some of the comet's ice into gas, which creates the glowing cloud and long tail. Scientists long suspected they helped start life on Earth.

In 1992, scientists Christopher Chyba and Carl Sagan studied the moon's craters and found that space dust, not comets, brought most of the carbon that reached Earth. But space dust holds its carbon in tough compounds that don't easily react, so it couldn't supply something like HCN. 

The Rosetta space mission took images of comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko in November 2014. Credit: ESA / Rosetta / NAVCAM

Because comets do carry HCN in a usable form, they have remained a contender as a source of life's building blocks. Lab experiments show that concentrated HCN can produce fats, sugars, and the nucleic acids that make up DNA and RNA.

The researchers selected HCN as the best case study because it's the only starter molecule that comets carry in useful amounts, and its triple bond makes it tougher than more complex molecules. In short, the scientists figured if HCN can't survive a comet crash, others chemicals probably won't either.

Recent computer models show that only comets wider than about 300 feet make it through the early Earth's atmosphere without breaking apart or burning up. Those models also show that earlier studies overestimated how much HCN can actually survive a high-speed collision. 

But if not comets, what could have brought life's ingredients? The researchers suggest asteroids, which slammed into Earth far more often. Their impacts may have triggered the critical chemical reactions to make prebiotic molecules. Or, an even simpler source: Maybe Earth made them on its own, without any extra help from space. 

The study showed comet delivery was slightly more probable for Mars. It's smaller than Earth with a thinner atmosphere, so comets had a better chance of reaching its surface intact. But the more likely answer is that it didn't happen on either planet, Anslow said. 

"Historically, scientists thought that comets were important for the origin of life on Earth because they carried carbon and nitrogen-bearing molecules, so these giant space snowballs were almost like a get-out-of-jail-free card that brought all the molecules a planet would need to seed life," Anslow said. "It’s kind of a romantic idea, but our results suggest it's highly unlikely."

Large language models (LLMs) now answer support tickets, review pull requests, draft marketing copy, and power the internal search bar employees use to find policy documents. They run as production software, and they carry production obligations.

Getting an LLM prototype working is easy. A developer with an API key can build a functional chatbot in an afternoon, which makes a project look further along than it is. Production adds failure modes that never appear in a demo: prompt and retrieval drift, inconsistent outputs, unmonitored latency, runaway token spending, and answers that are hard to trace back to the change that caused them.

McKinsey’s August 2026 State of AI survey measures how far organizations still have to go. Nearly 9 in 10 respondents use AI regularly in at least one business function, yet 44% say AI is scaling across their enterprise, and about 2 in 10 report scaling AI agents across the organization. Closing that gap takes operating discipline around the model: evaluation, release controls, and monitoring.

If you are responsible for taking LLM applications from pilot to production, this guide is for you. It covers what LLMOps includes, how it differs from machine learning operations (MLOps), the operational lifecycle, the challenges teams face, and how to choose between assembling a toolchain and adopting a platform.

What is LLMOps?

LLMOps, short for large language model operations, is the set of practices, processes, and tools that manage the full lifecycle of LLM-powered applications in production. It covers model selection, prompt and workflow management, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, safety controls, and continuous improvement.

In practice, LLMOps works as an operating model. It aligns engineering, data, security, and product teams around repeatable processes for output quality, cost, and risk. Teams adopt tools to support that model, the way a build server supports continuous integration.

LLMOps is the bridge between experimentation and production

Traditional machine learning (ML) required labeled data, training infrastructure, and specialist skills, so building a model took months, and the operational scaffolding took shape alongside it. Foundation model APIs remove that gate. You build a prototype first, and then build the scaffolding.

The missing layers are specific:

  • Repeatable evaluation that runs before and after each release rather than as ad hoc spot checks
  • Versioned prompts and workflows with change history and fast rollback
  • Controlled releases, so a prompt change reaches 5% of traffic before it reaches all of it
  • Observability across multistep chains, so a bad output can be traced to the retrieval step or tool call that produced it
  • Guardrails for safety, privacy, and compliance enforced in the request path

Build these layers before launch, starting with evaluation, because evaluation lets you measure whether any later change helped.

Security review can hold a launch for weeks. In Anaconda’s 2025 survey of more than 300 AI practitioners and decision-makers, 67% of respondents reported AI deployment delays tied to security issues. The governance section below covers how to produce review evidence as you build.

LLMOps vs. MLOps: key differences

LLMOps inherits the MLOps fundamentals. Versioning, continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD), automated testing, monitoring, and model registries all carry over. What changes is the surface area. LLMOps adds prompts, retrieval systems, nondeterministic generation, safety controls, and token-based economics, and each one breaks an assumption classical MLOps was built on. A 2025 peer-reviewed paper in the journal Information describes LLMOps as a subcategory of MLOps and names added risks, including jailbreak attacks, data poisoning, model drift, and high computational costs.

Treating LLMOps as MLOps with a different model type leads teams to apply evaluation, release, and cost practices that were built for deterministic models.

Foundation models vs. training from scratch

Model selection becomes an operational activity in LLMOps. MLOps assumes you own the training pipeline, which gives you control over the data, architecture, and hyperparameters. LLMOps starts with a commercial API or an open-weight model that you adapt through prompting, retrieval, or fine-tuning.

That shift means you must evaluate candidates on capability, latency, context window, licensing terms, cost per token at expected volume, and supply chain risk. Providers update and retire models during an application’s life, so reevaluate whenever the model changes. Document each decision in a form that security, legal, and procurement can review. Anaconda’s guide to open-source AI models covers the licensing and self-hosting trade-offs that belong in that record.

Prompt engineering as a core discipline

Prompts have no direct MLOps counterpart, and they need the controls application code already has. A prompt defines system behavior, encodes policy, and shapes the user experience, so a small edit can change outputs substantially once retrieval and tool calls are in the loop.

Teams that keep prompts in shared documents or scatter them across notebooks pay the cost the first time quality regresses, and tracing the cause means comparing nine recent edits by hand.

Nondeterministic outputs and evaluation challenges

Evaluation becomes a portfolio of methods in LLMOps, because generative output often lacks a single correct answer. Classical ML produces predictions you can score against a label with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. LLM output can be subjective, context-dependent, or only partially correct.

Automated scores cover part of the picture. BLEU (bilingual evaluation understudy) and ROUGE (recall-oriented understudy for gisting evaluation) measure word overlap with a reference text, and semantic similarity compares meaning through embeddings. LLM-as-judge evaluation, where one model scores another against a rubric, scales further but introduces its own bias and needs periodic calibration against human review. Human evaluation remains necessary when quality is subjective or the stakes are high.

Build task-specific benchmarks from your own traffic, since they reflect your actual tasks in a way public leaderboards cannot. A useful test asserts that the failure rate on a curated set stays below a threshold and that outputs follow a required format. It does not assert that an input returns a specific sentence.

Cost structures and resource management

Cost shifts from training to inference in LLMOps, and it grows with adoption. Each token, retrieval step, and tool call adds spending and latency. About 1 in 5 respondents in McKinsey’s 2026 survey say AI operating costs, including tokens, have constrained their organizations’ AI use. The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) includes unbounded consumption, which includes attacks that run up inference costs, in its Top 10 for LLM Applications.

Common levers include shortening prompts, caching repeated queries, tuning retrieval so fewer and better chunks reach the context window, and routing simpler requests to smaller models. Anaconda’s overview of small language models explains where a smaller model can match a larger one on a specialized task.

RAG and external knowledge integration

RAG adds a second system to operate alongside the model, with its own components, versions, and failure modes. It connects the model to knowledge it was not trained on.

Retrieval quality can degrade with no change to the model or the prompt. A corpus that doubles in size can reduce precision. An embedding model upgrade invalidates existing vectors. A document that should have been restricted can become retrievable by anyone who asks the right question.

Agents, tool use, and multistep workflows

In McKinsey’s survey, 40% of respondents from organizations with more than $1 billion in annual revenue report scaling AI agents in at least one business function, up from 27% a year earlier. As agents move into production, the operational bar rises, because agents add steps to trace, failure points to monitor, and actions with real consequences. An error in step two can produce a wrong result in step seven that reads as correct, and attributing it across the chain is hard. Evaluation has to score the path an agent took as well as its final answer.

The governance question changes with agents. It moves from which model you approved to the packages, data sources, tools, and deployment triggers the system can reach. Anaconda’s explanation of indirect prompt injection shows how hidden instructions in content an agent reads can steer its actions, and why prerelease red teaming and runtime guardrails belong in the plan. Start by scoring agent trajectories in your evaluation set and listing the tools and data each agent may use.

The LLMOps lifecycle

The LLMOps lifecycle is a loop: design, evaluate, deploy, observe, and improve, with governance running through each stage. Treating governance as a final approval gate invites a stall in security review, because the evidence reviewers need does not exist yet. The stages below cover each step of the loop.

Model selection and evaluation

Selection sets the constraints that downstream stages inherit, so complete it before any prompt is tuned. Benchmark candidates on representative work at expected concurrency and volume, using the criteria described above, because a model choice constrains cost structure, capability ceiling, and deployment options.

Provenance is where selection meets security. For open-weight models, find out where the weights came from, what the file format permits, whether the artifact has been scanned, and what documentation exists for audit. Anaconda’s six-step guide to choosing an open-source AI model walks through filtering by infrastructure, licensing, and provenance, and testing shortlisted models on your own data. A team that skips rigorous selection may revisit it after production deployment, when changing models means rewriting the prompts tuned to the old one.

Data preparation and fine-tuning

Three separate data assets support an LLM system, and each needs its own governance. Fine-tuning datasets shape model behavior and need the strictest provenance and approval controls. Evaluation datasets define what working means and must stay stable enough to detect regressions. Retrieval corpora change continuously and need freshness policies and access controls that mirror their source systems.

Fine-tuning approaches vary with how much adaptation the task requires. Full fine-tuning updates all weights and suits significant behavior change at significant cost. Parameter-efficient methods such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA) train a small number of added parameters, which makes customization affordable and makes it practical to maintain several task-specific variants. Instruction tuning improves adherence to a task format. For each dataset, record its version, lineage, approver, and approval date. This record is unglamorous work, and it is the evidence an auditor asks for.

Prompt engineering and management

Prompts and workflows belong in version control and release processes like any other production artifact. The lifecycle activities are developing and testing prompts, building templates and shared libraries, and reviewing prompts before they ship.

In practice, that means a prompt registry in place of a folder, shared components in place of copy and paste, tests that run on each change, and a named owner for each prompt. Ownership matters as much as tooling. Without a named owner and a release gate, prompt libraries accumulate contradictory instructions from different teams, and policy language that legal approved a year ago can disappear in an unrelated edit. Keep a rollback path measured in seconds, and scale the approval each change requires to the risk of the application.

RAG pipeline development

A RAG pipeline has five components that each need operational attention: document ingestion and chunking, embedding generation, vector storage and index management, retrieval strategy, and context assembly.

Version these components explicitly, because chunking strategy and embedding model are two changes that can break retrieval quality, and each is easy to change without seeing the blast radius. Monitor retrieval separately from generation. Retrieval quality degrades silently, since the model keeps producing fluent output grounded in the wrong documents. Govern the corpus by source, and decide which data sources the pipeline may include before ingestion begins. Carry source-system permissions through to retrieval so RAG does not become a path around existing access controls.

Deployment and serving

Data requirements should drive the deployment model more than engineering preference does. API-based services from providers such as OpenAI and Anthropic offer a fast path with less control over where data goes. Self-hosted inference on cloud infrastructure trades operational effort for control over data residency and model choice. On-premises deployment serves data sovereignty and regulated workloads. Hybrid approaches route by workload sensitivity.

Self-hosted options share infrastructure requirements: graphics processing unit (GPU) provisioning and scheduling, a serving framework such as vLLM or TensorRT-LLM, load balancing and autoscaling tuned for bursty traffic, and latency optimization through batching and quantization. Deployment consistency determines whether incidents can be debugged. If the Python environment serving production differs from the one where the system was evaluated, evaluation results do not transfer and incident investigation starts from zero. Declare environments as versioned recipes and rebuild them from approved package sources, an approach Anaconda describes in its post on Fast Bakery, which builds container images from a declared environment.

Monitoring and observability

Monitor in three groups, because each serves a different audience. System health covers latency, error rates, throughput, and token consumption, and indicates whether the service is up. Business outcomes cover task success, support deflection rate, resolution time, and customer satisfaction (CSAT), and indicate whether the service is useful. Model risk covers hallucination indicators, policy violations, prompt injection attempts, and output drift, and indicates whether the service is safe.

Observability for LLM systems means tracing the full request chain: prompt version, retrieved context, tool calls, intermediate steps, and final output, linked together. Without the chain, a bad output is hard to explain. Gartner’s May 2026 prediction is that 40% of organizations deploying AI will implement dedicated AI observability tools by 2028, and Gartner warns that skipping them exposes organizations to governance risk. Build logging with privacy controls from the start, because traces contain user inputs and retrieved documents. Retain what debugging and audit require, redact the rest, and set the retention policy before the first incident.

Governance and compliance

Governance is infrastructure, and it works best when the platform produces it automatically. The components are access controls and authentication, audit trails for interactions, lineage and provenance tracking across models and data, compliance documentation mapped to frameworks such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and SOC 2, and transparency artifacts such as model cards and bills of materials.

When provenance, lineage, and audit evidence come out of the workflow itself, security review becomes a request for records that already exist. Anaconda’s six-step guide to the Generative AI Profile from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST AI 600-1) covers the 12 risks the voluntary profile identifies as unique to or exacerbated by generative AI and how to manage them.

Continuous improvement and iteration

The improvement loop runs on evidence, and edits made directly in production do not belong in it. Collect user feedback and production traces, cluster failures by cause, refine prompts and retrieval against a curated regression set, and evaluate model updates as candidates rather than drop-in replacements. Fine-tune when prompting and retrieval have reached their limit.

Systems improve through controlled experiments with measurable evaluation and safe release. They drift when prompts are edited directly in production, which makes changes in quality unexplainable. Set the rule early that all changes go through the same release path.

Core LLMOps challenges

The lifecycle describes what to build. These five challenges are where teams meet resistance while building it, so each section names the problem and points to the practice that addresses it.

Evaluation and testing complexity

Test suites built on exact-match assertions do not work for generative output. A workable approach combines four layers. Automated metrics cover latency, token usage, format compliance, and refusal rates. LLM-as-judge evaluation scores subjective quality at scale, and human review covers high-stakes cases and calibrates the judge. Regression tests built from real production failures catch mistakes you have already made once. Start with the regression set, since it gains value with each incident.

Prompt management at scale

One use case does not create a prompt management problem, and 30 do. Different teams write conflicting instructions, and finding every prompt a compliance change affects means searching each team’s prompts by hand. The fix is organizational before it is technical: the named owners, registry, and release gates described in the lifecycle section.

Hallucination detection and mitigation

LLMs can produce fluent content that is wrong, and detection is difficult because hallucinations look like answers rather than errors. In production they create user distrust, compliance exposure, and reputational risk.

Mitigation is layered, and no single layer eliminates the problem. Retrieval grounding anchors responses in source documents. Citation requirements let users and reviewers verify claims. Constrained generation limits output format for high-risk tasks. Verification steps check factual claims against systems of record. Escalation paths route low-confidence responses to a person. Apply the layers in proportion to the cost of a wrong answer, and measure the residual rate on your regression set.

Cost management and optimization

Cost scales with usage, and nothing in the development experience signals it. A team that validates an approach at development volume can see the production bill two weeks after launch.

Put visibility in place before launch: token tracking attributed to application, feature, and environment, plus alerts and budget controls that fire before the invoice arrives. Then apply the levers described earlier, starting with the workloads that account for the most spending.

Security and data privacy

LLMs create attack surfaces that traditional application security was not designed to cover. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition) ranks prompt injection first, followed by sensitive information disclosure and supply chain risk. The controls are input validation, output filtering for sensitive data, access controls enforced at the prompt and retrieval layers, adversarial testing before launch, and explicit data handling policies for what is logged, where it is stored, and how long it is kept.

The Python layer beneath the stack needs the same controls. The orchestration framework, vector client, evaluation library, and serving runtime in an LLM stack are Python software assembled from public package registries. An LLMOps program with rigorous model governance and ungoverned dependencies has a governance gap. Close it by resolving packages from governed channels, scanning them for known vulnerabilities, and recording what entered each environment. Anaconda’s post on governed packages and models in flow steps shows one way to apply organizational policy before a package installs.

Building an LLMOps stack

Organizations face a structural decision: assemble specialized tools for each function, or adopt an integrated platform that covers several. No single answer fits all teams. The right choice depends on team maturity, security constraints, time-to-value pressure, and how many LLM use cases you expect to support over the next two years.

The toolchain approach

A toolchain pairs a best-in-class tool with each function: an orchestration framework such as LangChain or LlamaIndex, a vector database, an observability and tracing product, an evaluation framework, and a prompt management system. The benefits are a best-in-class option for each function, the ability to replace a component as the landscape shifts, and no concentration of dependence on one vendor.

The costs show up after selection. Integration work grows with each tool added, because each new tool must connect to the others. Governance fragments across systems, so answering an audit question means assembling evidence from five places. Total cost of ownership includes sustained integration and maintenance effort beyond license fees, and operational overhead grows with each tool the team must learn, patch, and monitor. Choose this path when your team has the platform engineering capacity to own the integration layer for the long term.

The platform approach

A platform provides several LLMOps capabilities with consistent interfaces, unified governance, and coordinated operations. The benefits are reduced integration overhead, governance that spans capabilities, one operational model for the team to learn, and a shorter path to production. Cloud providers offer integrated machine learning services of this kind, including Amazon SageMaker AI, Azure Machine Learning, and Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (formerly Vertex AI), and they work well within their own ecosystems.

The trade-offs are that no platform leads in all functions, and dependence on one vendor can grow. Deployment flexibility and open standards help keep that dependence manageable, especially when AI workloads cross clouds, on-premises infrastructure, and vendor boundaries. Anaconda’s post on multivendor AI stacks lists the signs that a single-vendor stack no longer fits, including data residency, provenance, model choice, and cost control. Choose this path when time to value and consistent governance matter more than best-in-class depth in each function.

Key evaluation criteria

Apply the same criteria to either approach.

  • Deployment flexibility: Can it run where your data must live, including on-premises environments? This constraint can eliminate options early.
  • Governance depth: Does it produce audit trails, lineage, and provenance documentation automatically rather than on request?
  • Integration with existing workflows: Does it work with the Python ecosystem your team already uses, your existing ML infrastructure, and your cloud provider, without forcing a new execution model or a migration?
  • Scalability path: Does the same system carry you from one pilot to 30 production use cases, or does a rewrite wait at some threshold?
  • Total cost of ownership: Count integration effort, ongoing operations, and the opportunity cost of engineers maintaining infrastructure instead of building applications.
  • Supply chain controls: Can you verify which packages and models enter your environment and produce a bill of materials on demand?

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between LLMOps and MLOps?

MLOps manages models you train, where outputs are deterministic, evaluation compares predictions to ground truth, and cost concentrates in training. LLMOps manages applications built on pretrained foundation models, where outputs vary between identical inputs, evaluation relies on rubrics and human judgment, and cost concentrates in per-token inference. LLMOps also introduces artifacts that MLOps has no direct counterpart for: prompts that function as product logic, and retrieval pipelines that operate as a second system alongside the model. The engineering fundamentals carry over, and the assumptions about determinism, evaluation, and cost change.

Do I need LLMOps if I am using API-based models like GPT or Claude?

Yes. A hosted API removes model hosting from your responsibilities, and the rest remains: prompts still need versioning and rollback, outputs still need evaluation, token spending still needs attribution, and retrieval still needs monitoring. Security review still asks what data leaves your environment. A provider can update a model on its own schedule, so regression testing matters more. For example, a support assistant that passed evaluation last month can fail a format check after a provider-side update, and a regression suite catches it.

What are the most important LLMOps capabilities to implement first?

Start with evaluation, then prompt versioning. An evaluation set built from real examples of correct and incorrect output is the baseline every later change gets measured against. Prompt versioning lets you identify and reverse a regression. Add cost tracing third, since it answers the question finance will ask. Add full-chain observability once more than one multistep workflow is in production, and plan governance from the beginning, because retrofitting audit trails is harder than emitting them from the start.

How do I measure whether my LLMOps practices are working?

Use operational metrics in place of model metrics: time from prompt change to production shows whether your release process works, and mean time to diagnose a bad output shows whether your observability works. Regression escape rate, meaning failures that reached production and should have been caught by your evaluation set, shows whether your tests reflect actual usage. Cost per successful task shows whether optimization improves efficiency or shifts spending without reducing it. At the enterprise level, track how many use cases reach production and how long each takes, since those use cases are where business results come from. McKinsey reports that 37% of respondents attribute at least some earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) impact to AI use.

How does LLMOps change when you add agents or tool use?

Agents change the unit of observation from a single request to a chain of decisions, and the unit of risk from a text output to an action. Tracing must capture each step and tool invocation, or failures become unattributable. Evaluation has to score trajectories, since an agent can reach a correct conclusion through an unacceptable path. Permissions become a first-class concern: define which tools, data sources, and deployment actions each agent can use. Cost also rises, and Gartner’s August 2026 forecast is that inference costs per agentic workflow will grow more than fivefold through 2028.

How does LLMOps change when using RAG versus fine-tuning?

RAG shifts operational weight toward data pipeline management. You run ingestion, chunking, embedding, indexing, and retrieval as a live system whose quality changes as the corpus changes, so it needs its own monitoring, versioning, and access controls. Fine-tuning shifts weight toward model lifecycle management: dataset governance, training runs, model versioning, and periodic retraining. RAG can update knowledge quickly and provides citable sources, while fine-tuning changes behavior, format adherence, and domain fluency in ways prompting cannot reach, at the cost of a training cycle per update. For example, a policy assistant that must cite current documents leans on RAG, and an assistant that must answer in a fixed report format leans on fine-tuning. Production systems often combine the two, which means managing two update cadences.

How do I secure the Python supply chain beneath an LLM application?

Treat each package and model as an input that needs approval before it enters an environment. Resolve packages from governed channels, apply policy based on known vulnerabilities and license status, and record what each run used. A concrete case is CVE-2025-32434, a critical Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) entry in the National Vulnerability Database: loading a model file in PyTorch 2.5.1 or earlier could allow remote command execution, and version 2.6.0 fixed it. Anaconda’s post on governed packages and models describes how policy can block affected versions before they are installed.
