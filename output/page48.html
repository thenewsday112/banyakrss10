As Cybersecurity Awareness Month continues, the GitHub Bug Bounty team is excited to spotlight another top-performing security researcher from the GitHub Security Bug Bounty Program: @r31n!

As home to the world’s developers, GitHub is dedicated to the security and reliability of the code behind millions of projects, and our Bug Bounty Program remains a cornerstone of that commitment. Collaboration with external researchers is one of the most effective ways to improve security, and every year, the community helps us identify and fix vulnerabilities that make the platform safer for everyone.

The security research landscape is changing rapidly. AI has lowered the barrier to entry and become a genuine force multiplier. We use it across our own internal security programs, and we welcome researchers who do the same. However, what separates great research has not changed: validation, a working proof of concept, and a clear demonstration of real security impact. The tools do not matter; the quality of the work does. The researchers we spotlight this month exemplify exactly that standard through findings that are verified, reproducible, and demonstrate that a security boundary has been crossed rather than simply theorizing that one exists.

Researchers who consistently work at that level can earn an invitation to our permanent VIP program, which offers higher payouts, faster response times, and early previews of beta products and features. Learn more about how to qualify on our public bounty site.

As we wrap up our Cybersecurity Awareness Month celebration, we interview @r31n, a researcher renowned for their work on GitHub Enterprise Server, where they have repeatedly uncovered high-impact issues. Their reports consistently include working proofs of concept and clear reproduction steps, helping our engineering teams move from triage to remediation more quickly.

How did you get started in security and bug bounty, and what keeps you coming back to GitHub’s program?

I got into bug bounty because I wanted a way to keep developing my security skills outside the environments I work with every day. It exposes me to different products, technologies, and architectures I probably wouldn’t run into otherwise. GitHub is one of those programs I keep coming back to because there’s just so much surface area. It’s large, complex, and constantly changing. New features ship all the time, so it never really feels like a finished target. They also work closely with researchers, the bounty structure is competitive, and the swag is a nice bonus. It’s simply a program I enjoy spending time on.

What are your favorite classes of bugs to research and why?

I don’t really have one favorite bug class. What interests me more are exploit chains, especially cases where a few things that seem relatively harmless on their own come together and create something much more serious. An open redirect is a simple example. By itself, the impact might be limited. But if you find an allowlisted-domain SSRF that follows redirects, suddenly that open redirect can become a useful part of a much bigger chain. That’s probably the part of vulnerability research I enjoy most: finding those connections, looking at two or three separate bugs and realizing they can be combined in a way that wasn’t intended. It takes some technical knowledge, but also quite a bit of creativity.

How do you keep your skills sharp and stay on top of new vulnerability trends?

It’s actually a mix of different things. Security blogs, vulnerability write-ups, conference talks, proof of concepts, research papers, courses. I try to learn from wherever useful work is being shared. But most of it only really sticks once I try it myself. I can read a vulnerability write-up, but working through it myself gives me a much deeper understanding. If I actually reproduce the technique, play around with it, break it, and figure out why it behaves the way it does, that’s when it really stays with me. And later, when I see a similar pattern on a real target, it’s much easier to recognize.

What tools or workflows have been gamechangers for your research? Any lesser-known utilities you’d recommend?

I tend to prefer manual investigation and small, purpose-built scripts over large automated toolchains. If I notice I’m repeating the same task during research, I’ll usually automate that specific part. AI has probably impacted my way of working more than any individual security tool.

Even then, I think mindset matters more than tooling. You can become extremely good with a particular tool and still miss interesting bugs because you’re only looking at the target through the lens that tool gives you. Sometimes relying too heavily on tooling actually narrows the way you think. Tools change. Techniques change. Curiosity and persistence tend to age much better. So, I don’t really have one tool I’d recommend to everyone. The most useful tool is usually the one that helps answer the specific question I’m investigating.

Walk us through your general approach. How do you pick a target area, and how do you go from “interesting feature” to a confirmed finding?

I usually start by mapping out the application and getting a feel for how its different parts fit together. I tend to spend more time on complex functionality, integrations, trust boundaries, and anything that behaves a little differently from the rest. Experience and intuition matter too. Some areas simply look more likely to expose interesting security behavior. From there, I often think in terms of sources and sinks rather than starting with a specific vulnerability class. A source is a point where attacker-controlled data enters the application.

Think of a request parameter, uploaded file, API input, or data coming from another service. A sink can be a security-sensitive operation that consumes this data, for example, a network request, file access, process execution, database query, or authorization decision. The interesting part is tracing the path between the two. I look at how the data is transformed, validated, or passed across trust boundaries, and whether those controls are sufficient before it reaches the sink.

I like this approach because it lets me reason about attack paths without forcing my investigation into a predefined bug class. Sometimes the vulnerability only becomes obvious once you understand how a particular source can influence a security-sensitive operation. I consider a finding confirmed once I understand the attack path, can produce a working PoC, and can explain the impact.

Do you use AI in your research? Where does it genuinely help and where does it fall short?

Yes. Quite a lot, actually, but mostly as an accelerator. I use it for navigating unfamiliar code, finding call sites, tracing data through the codebase, and understanding how a system is structured and how its components interact. I also find it helpful for writing PoC scripts once I’ve identified the vulnerability.

Where it works best is when the question is specific. “Is this application vulnerable?” usually isn’t a very useful prompt. Give it the right context, explain what you already know, and ask a narrow question, and the quality goes up dramatically. There’s a bit of a paradox there. AI becomes more useful as you understand the problem better yourself. And I never treat its conclusions as facts. Hallucinations, false positives, bad assumptions, and confidently wrong answers are all common. Everything should still be manually verified. AI makes my work faster. However, it doesn’t replace understanding the application, developing intuition, or proving that something actually works.

What’s your guidance on using AI responsibly in bug bounty?

Use it to improve your research, not to outsource responsibility for it. If AI tells you it found a vulnerability, that doesn’t mean you found a real vulnerability. You still need to reproduce it yourself. You should understand exactly what’s happening, which security boundary or control is being violated, and what the real impact is. Ideally, you should understand the issue well enough that you could write the report without AI at all. Build a clear PoC. Make sure the security team can follow it. Know why every step matters.

AI has lowered the barrier to doing security research, which is great in a lot of ways. But you can also see the downside in the number of unverified reports teams now have to deal with. AI should make research better. Not just make more reports.

As the industry ships more AI-powered features, does testing them require a different mindset than traditional web bugs?

Yes. Conventional vulnerabilities still matter, but attackers may also be able to influence a probabilistic decision-making component through the data, instructions, or context it receives. But getting a model to say or do something unexpected isn’t necessarily the interesting part. The more important question is what that influence allows an attacker to achieve. Can they access something they should not or cause the system to act with privileges the attacker does not have? That’s why I don’t think model-level guardrails should ever be treated as the only or primary security boundary. You still need deterministic controls around the model. This includes proper authorization, least-privilege access, isolation, and validation. The surrounding systems matter just as much as the model itself. And traditional application security doesn’t disappear just because AI is involved. Model output should still be treated as untrusted input. If that output ends up in a browser, database query, shell command, web request, or another downstream system without the right controls, you can still end up with very familiar vulnerabilities: XSS, SQL injection, command injection, SSRF, and so on. I think the model is new. A lot of the dangerous sinks aren’t.

What’s one thing you wish you’d known when you first started?

Learn systems, not checklists. When I started, I was tempted to collect huge lists of techniques, payloads, tools, and bypasses and then work through them one by one. Those lists can be useful.

I still use them. But understanding how a system works has been far more valuable. Its internal logic. The data flows. Trust relationships. Assumptions. Why one component is allowed to talk to another. Why a particular restriction exists in the first place. I also learned not to assume something is secure because it looks secure, or because plenty of researchers have already tested it.

A lot of interesting research begins with: “Why does this work this way?” rather than “Which payload should I try next?” There’s some luck involved too. Sometimes you spend days following something that goes absolutely nowhere. Then you notice one tiny detail almost by accident and it turns into a high-impact finding. You can’t control that part. You can control whether you keep questioning things long enough to notice it.

What advice or resources would you recommend to researchers trying to land their first valid report?

Get comfortable with the fundamentals, then pick one program and one target and stick with them for a while. Constantly jumping between targets makes it difficult to build a real mental model of any of them. Use the application. Explore weird flows. Test different roles and permission levels. Read the documentation. Watch the requests and responses. Look at the APIs. Labs, courses, public write-ups, disclosed bug bounty reports, and intentionally vulnerable applications are all useful too. They teach you where bugs tend to hide and, over time, help build the intuition to recognize similar situations elsewhere.

One mistake I see a lot is relying on tools too early. Another is giving up because the obvious tests didn’t find anything. Tools help. They don’t build the mental model for you. And when you finally do find something, spend the extra time validating it properly. Create a working PoC. Make reproduction easy for the security team. Understanding one target deeply will usually get you further than trying to find a bug as quickly as possible.

What do you enjoy doing when you aren’t hacking?

Endurance sports, mainly road cycling, especially in the mountains. I enjoy the physical challenge of a long climb and pushing myself over longer distances. It’s also a great way to get away from screens for a while and spend some time outside. There’s actually something about climbing that feels pretty similar to vulnerability research. You can put in a lot of effort without seeing much progress. Sometimes you have no idea whether what you’re doing is going to lead anywhere. You just keep going. And every now and then, it pays off.

Any social media profiles you’d like to share with our readers?

You can find me on HackerOne.

Thank you, @r31n, for participating in GitHub’s bug bounty researcher spotlight! Each submission to our bug bounty program is a chance to make GitHub, our products, and our customers more secure, and we continue to welcome and appreciate collaboration with the security research community. So, if this inspired you to go hunting for bugs, feel free to report your findings through HackerOne.

Cristina Perpiñá-Robert, director general of the Spanish Society of Authors and Publishers (SGAE), voiced this concern on Tuesday (Oct. 20) during a discussion on AI-generated music and the future of creativity at Billboard Latin Music Week. Since Anaconda acquired Outerbounds, I’ve been working with customers on what’s now AI Orchestration in Anaconda Platform, which runs Python workflows and apps at scale in your own cloud, built on open-source Metaflow. In recent conversations, customers asked:

  1. Does fanning out to over 1,000 tasks work? If so, how, and is it a good idea?
  2. How do I retry failed tasks?
  3. Can we create node pools with multiple instance types, so there’s a better chance of getting spot instances?

To test my understanding, I took the following path:

  • Part 1, getting it running: turning my old script into a flow, sending it to the cluster, the two walls I hit on the way (a 100-task cap and census.gov’s rate limit), and where retry and resume fit in.
  • Part 2, making it fast: batching to lower overhead, keeping GBs out of memory, reading only what I needed, and sorting the Parquet so searches skip most of it.
  • Part 3, the takeaways: short answers to those three questions (and a couple more), if you just want the TL;DR.

Part 1: Core platform usage

A foreach turns a loop over files into parallel tasks, one per file.

The search page: 2.3 million street names, one box.

One of my old apps made a good test, since it processes thousands of URLs in an embarrassingly parallel way. In MapnStreets, you type a street name, say “Main St”, and it tells you how many road segments carry that name (95,173 for Main St, the most common of 2.3 million names), which states have the most of them (Massachusetts, then Pennsylvania and New York), and paints every one of them on a heat map of the country. Add a * to match a prefix, like Roosevelt*, and see Roosevelt St, Roosevelt Ave, etc. I originally built it in 2023 with Fugue and GeoPandas on Ray, so porting it would also show what the platform can offer.

Every “Main St” in the US, densest in the Northeast and Midwest.

The processing script consists of three stages, basically extract, transform, and load (ETL):

  1. List: scrape the Census Bureau’s listing page for the ~3,200 .zip links.
  2. Download and process: for each link, download the zip and read the shapefile with GeoPandas.
  3. Combine: stack the results into one dataset the app can search.

The second stage is the one worth fanning out. Fanning out means running many copies of the same step in parallel, each on its own piece of the input, instead of looping over the input one item at a time. It works here because each file is independent: no download needs another one’s result. Metaflow calls this a foreach, and the step after it, join, is where the results come back together.

Each stage became a Metaflow step, plus the end step every flow needs. fetch_links and read_shapefile are the helpers for the first two stages:

class MapnStreetsFlow(FlowSpec): @step def start(self): self.urls = fetch_links(BASE_URL) self.next(self.download_and_process, foreach="urls") @step def download_and_process(self): self.gdf = read_shapefile(self.input) # this task's URL self.next(self.join) @step def join(self, inputs): self.gdf = pd.concat([inp.gdf for inp in inputs]) self.next(self.end) @step def end(self): pass

Instead of writing each result to disk, I assigned the GeoDataFrame to self.gdf. In Metaflow, the open-source framework underpinning the platform, anything you assign to self in a step becomes an artifact: it’s pickled, saved with the run (in S3 on the platform), and available to the next step as inp.gdf and after the run as run.data.gdf. That’s handy for smaller objects, but there are better ways for bigger objects. I tested it locally on a few URLs and it worked.

Picking where tasks run

Moving a step from laptop to cluster takes one decorator, @kubernetes, and that’s the main payoff: a step can run on a machine far bigger than a laptop. On the cluster the step can run in a container with fast-bakery rather than your local environment. Because each step is self-contained, you can wrap @pypi on each step with its unique Python version and packages.

Each step can also ask for its own resources. For example, the download tasks ask for two CPUs and 8 GB each, while the join, which sorts the whole dataset, gets more memory and 80 GB of scratch disk:

@kubernetes(cpu=2, memory=8000, disk=20000) @pypi(python="3.11", packages={"geopandas": "1.1.3", "requests": "2.32.3"}) @step def download_and_process(self): ... @kubernetes(cpu=4, memory=12000, disk=80000) @pypi(python="3.11", packages={"duckdb": "1.5.6", "pyarrow": "25.0.1"}) @step def join(self, inputs): ...

Before the decorator, the code ran on my laptop against a few URLs. After it, the full run goes to the cluster, where a single machine can have 96 cores or more and many tasks can run at once. But this isn’t bulletproof: there are other variables, such as upstream rate limits.

The tasks run on a compute pool, which you set up once in the platform UI: choose “Metaflow Tasks” under Usage, pick an instance type, and set the minimum and maximum number of instances and the disk size. Checking “Use spot (interruptible) instances” puts the pool on spot capacity: spare cloud machines, sold at a steep discount, that the provider can reclaim at any time. A pool is either spot or on-demand, so if you want both, create two pools. Spot GPUs are often scarce, so GPU pools are usually better left on-demand.

Creating a compute pool for Metaflow tasks on spot instances.

Each pool takes a single instance type, so spreading across types means one pool per type: for example, c5.2xlarge and c6a.2xlarge, both 8 cores and 16 GiB, so a task fits on either. Leave compute_pool out of @kubernetes and tasks go to whichever pool has room, which schedules about the same as one pool with several instance types.

Each pool takes one instance type from this list.

No Dockerfile or bucket

At this point the flow ran on the cluster, and I’d set up very little to get there. As a Python developer, I find infra setup particularly tedious (and dare I say, annoying?).

One thing I’ve always struggled with was Dockerfiles, and although LLMs can easily generate one now, the slow part is building the image, pushing it to a registry, keeping one image per set of dependencies, and rebuilding whenever a pin changes. The @pypi line alongside python flow.py --environment=fast-bakery run allows the platform to build and cache each step’s environments: the download step gets GeoPandas and the join step gets DuckDB.

There’s no bucket to set up either. Anything I assign to self is stored for me, and later, when I needed to write files myself, Metaflow’s S3 client was already configured with the location and credentials. I never created a bucket or copied a key.

Scaling up to all ~3,200 files is where the pitfalls show up (but can be easily addressed).

Pitfall #1: The 100-task limit

Fanning out past 100 tasks, like my 162 batches of 20 files (more on batching in Part 2), will result in this error message:

Foreach in step start yielded 162 child steps which is more than the current maximum of 100 children.

This limit is intentional: it stops a foreach from spawning more than 100 tasks by accident. If this is what you desire, raise the cap with --max-num-splits, and use --max-workers (16 by default) to limit how many tasks run at once.

python flow.py --environment=fast-bakery run --max-num-splits 162

Pitfall #2: Rate limits

Once a run fans out, the next thing that breaks might be from upstream. More parallelism doesn’t always pan out when the bottleneck is someone else’s server; in this case, census.gov started returning HTTP 429 (Too Many Requests) once lots of pods hit it at once. To recover from this, @retry can be added to the step, plus inline code that catches 429s specifically and waits for Retry-After:

for attempt in range(5): r = requests.get(url, timeout=60) if r.status_code == 429: time.sleep(int(r.headers.get("Retry-After", 5))) continue r.raise_for_status() break

The inline loop retries a single request, and @retry reruns the whole task if something else breaks. However, this is only part of the solution; we should be respectful of someone else’s server (and also not risk getting banned) so we should also add --max-workers to limit the number of tasks fetching from the server simultaneously. A random sleep inside each task can also help to prevent a retry storm.

Retry vs. resume

The second customer question has two answers, depending on when the failure happens.

@retry handles failures inside a run. If a task dies from something transient, like a network error or a spot instance getting reclaimed, Metaflow reruns just that task automatically, so you don’t have to watch the run and restart things by hand:

@retry(times=4, minutes_between_retries=1) @kubernetes(cpu=2, memory=8000, disk=20000) @step def download_and_process(self): ...

A retry reruns the same code, though, so it can’t fix a bug. When a run fails for real, fix the code (or add the missing file) and resume it:

python flow.py --environment=pypi resume join --origin-run-id 25 --max-num-splits 165

Resume reuses the steps that already succeeded in the original run (run 25 here) and reruns from the step you name, join in this case. If you don’t name a step, it picks up at the step that failed, and if a later step fails too, you can fix that and resume again. Without --origin-run-id, it resumes your latest run.

My original Fugue script handled this with .done marker files, so a rerun could skip downloads that had already finished:

# before (2023): skip any file that already has a .done marker for link in df["link"]: parquet_path = DATA_DIR / Path(link).with_suffix(".parquet").name done_path = parquet_path.with_suffix(".done") if done_path.exists(): continue ... # download, convert, write parquet done_path.touch()

Resume automatically handles this: successful tasks are copied over from the original run, and only failed ones run again.

Part 2: Optimization

With the flow running end to end, the rest was making it fast and light: fewer pod startups, less memory, and smaller reads.

Make each task worth its startup time

While watching the runs, I noticed a one-URL task ran for only about 4 seconds, but in my configuration a pod took much longer than that to start. That’s normal for container platforms: every pod has to be scheduled and started before your code runs. With one URL per task, startup was taking most of the task’s runtime. That reminded me of the Dask chunk-size tradeoff: more chunks means more parallelism but also more overhead, and the job is finding the sweet spot.

So I went back to 100 URLs and compared 1 URL per task against 20. Batching is a list of lists, and making the chunk size a Parameter means it can change without touching the code:

class MapnStreetsFlow(FlowSpec): chunk_size = Parameter("chunk-size", default=20, type=int) @step def start(self): ... self.url_batches = [ urls[i : i + self.chunk_size] for i in range(0, len(urls), self.chunk_size) ] self.next(self.download_and_process, foreach="url_batches")

The comparison was then two runs:

python flow.py --environment=pypi run --url-limit 100 --chunk-size 1 python flow.py --environment=pypi run --url-limit 100 --chunk-size 20

With 1 URL per task, the run took 6m 46s. The timeline shows why: 100 tasks of about 4 seconds each, spread across almost 5 minutes of the download step.

1 URL per task: 100 four-second tasks, and 4m 49s for the download step.

With 20 URLs per task, the same 100 URLs took 2m 32s. Each task ran for 10–16 seconds, and there were 20x fewer pods to start. The full run, all ~3,200 files in 162 batches of 20, took 19m 32s.

20 URLs per task: 5 tasks, and 1m 23s for the download step.

The rule of thumb: make each task’s work substantially longer than its startup time. Even at 20 URLs, a task’s work was probably still shorter than its pod startup, so there’s room to go bigger.

The cost is retry granularity: the unit of @retry and resume is now a batch, so if one URL fails for good, all 20 get re-downloaded. That’s fine as long as each batch is short.

Don’t hold everything in memory at once

With the downloads done, the join step combined everything into the final dataset. It took several minutes, but it finished. Everything was saved, so I pointed the MapnStreets app at the result… and the loading spinner kept spinning:

run = Flow("MapnStreetsFlow").latest_successful_run gdf = run.data.gdf # unpickles 5 GB into memory; app hangs

The slow join and the hanging app had the same cause. Each inp.gdf in the join downloads a pickled blob from S3, unpickles it, and keeps it in memory, and then pd.concat copies all of it again into one big DataFrame. The app had it worse: it had to load one 5 GB pickle before it could show anything. Holding all that in memory also means paying for far more RAM than the work needs.

Before: every task’s pickle goes through the join. After: parts go to S3, and only their keys reach the join.

The fix was to stop passing data through Metaflow artifacts. Each task writes its own Parquet part to S3 and passes along only its key, a short string that points at the file:

with S3(run=self) as s3: s3.put_files([(key, str(local))]) self.part_key = key # just a string self.next(self.join)

The join collects the keys, fetches the parts in parallel with s3.get_many, and hands them to DuckDB with a memory limit and a place to spill:

keys = [inp.part_key for inp in inputs if inp.part_key] with S3(run=self) as s3: for obj in s3.get_many(keys): # parallel downloads Path(obj.path).replace(parts / Path(obj.key).name) con = duckdb.connect() con.execute("SET memory_limit='8GB'") con.execute(f"SET temp_directory='{work / 'spill'}'") # spill to disk

DuckDB reads the parts from local disk and spills the sort to that temp directory once it passes the limit. The join needs disk space rather than RAM, and no single process ever holds the whole dataset, so it fits on a smaller, cheaper machine.

The app changed the same way. Instead of unpickling a DataFrame, it reads two strings off the latest successful run and queries the Parquet files directly:

run = Flow("MapnStreetsFlow").latest_successful_run edges_url = run.data.edges_url # s3://.../edges.parquet counts_url = run.data.counts_url # s3://.../name_counts.parquet

Because the app always reads latest_successful_run, every successful run updates the dashboard with no extra wiring.

The app queries the Parquet directly, and every match is a sortable row with its county, length, and coordinates.

Load only what you need

When the app first hung, my first suspect was all the columns I didn’t need. Topologically Integrated Geographic Encoding and Referencing (TIGER) edge files have about 30 columns, and the app uses three columns plus geometry. We can add it to the shapefile reader itself:

KEEP_COLUMNS = ["STATEFP", "COUNTYFP", "FULLNAME"] gdf = gpd.read_file(f"zip://{local_zip}", columns=KEEP_COLUMNS) geometry = shapely.to_wkb(gdf.geometry.to_numpy())

That pushes the selection into the reader, so the other columns are never parsed or carried anywhere. Storing geometry as well-known binary (WKB) instead of well-known text (WKT) makes the parts 2–3x smaller and skips a text parse on every row.

On the app side, because not everything in the app is a map, a separate, small counts table (name → count) can help answer record counts and rankings without touching geometry at all, making the results extra speedy.

total = con.execute( f"SELECT sum(n) FROM read_parquet('{counts_url}') WHERE FULLNAME = ?", [street], ).fetchone()[0]

The headline numbers, like Main St’s #1 rank out of 2.3 million names, come from the separate, small counts table.

Sort for how you query

The last fix was the search itself. At first, every query scanned the full 4 GB Parquet file. The data was unsorted, so every row group held names from A to Z and nothing could be skipped. The 2023 version got around this by spreading each lookup across CPUs with Fugue. But we can do better: sorting on FULLNAME in the join fixes it, and the row group size is set in the same statement:

COPY ( SELECT STATEFP, COUNTYFP, FULLNAME, geometry FROM read_parquet('parts/*.parquet') WHERE FULLNAME IS NOT NULL ORDER BY FULLNAME ) TO 'edges.parquet' ( FORMAT parquet, COMPRESSION zstd, ROW_GROUP_SIZE 100000 )

With the file sorted, DuckDB can skip any row group whose min/max stats can’t contain the name being searched. Row group size matters too, because a row group’s column chunk is the smallest piece a reader can fetch. At 1 million rows per group, looking up “Main St” pulled ~110 MB of geometry to use ~6 MB of it; at 100,000 rows, that dropped to ~11 MB per group.

Sorting turns “read every row group” into “read one.”

Smaller row groups mean less wasted geometry per lookup.

The big file only gets opened when the user asks to plot shapes on the map, and even then the app reads only the byte ranges it needs from S3.

Because names are sorted, a prefix like Roosevelt* is one contiguous range: 12,569 segments across 152 names, mostly avenues.

Part 3: What I’d tell customers now

Does fanning out to 1,000+ tasks work?

Yes. The 100-task cap is a guardrail: raise it with --max-num-splits and control concurrency with --max-workers. Whether it’s a good idea depends on how much work each task does. Pod startup isn’t free, and a task that does a few seconds of work is mostly overhead, so batch until each task’s work is substantially longer than its startup. On 100 URLs, batching 20 per task cut the run from 6m 46s to 2m 32s. And as concurrency goes up, watch for upstream rate limits.

How do I retry failed tasks?

Two ways. @retry reruns a failed task automatically inside the run, which covers transient problems like network errors or a reclaimed spot instance. resume is for after a run fails: fix the cause, resume with --origin-run-id, and it reuses whatever already succeeded. Resume can’t absorb every change to the graph (inserting a step inside a foreach didn’t work for me), so it’s worth getting the steps right up front.

Can node pools have multiple instance types?

Not within one pool: each pool takes one instance type. Create a spot pool per instance type instead and leave compute_pool out of @kubernetes. Tasks then go to whichever pool has room, which schedules about the same as one mixed pool.

How do I stay under an upstream rate limit?

Set --max-workers, because the number of tasks in flight is your real request rate. Then handle 429s inline by waiting for Retry-After, and add @retry as a backstop for anything else.

How should I pass large data between steps?

Through S3, not Metaflow artifacts. Write each task’s output to S3 with Metaflow’s S3 client and pass only the key. Metaflow artifacts are great for storing smaller objects, but unpickling gigabytes in a join or an app is slow and memory-hungry.

How do I make lookups on a big Parquet file fast?

Read only the columns you need, sort the file by the column you search on, and keep row groups small (100,000 rows worked well here). For counts and rankings, keep a small summary table so most queries never touch the big file.

Wrapping up

None of this is specific to street names. At its core it’s data wrangling and ETL, and the shape is reusable: list the inputs, fan out one task per batch, and combine the results through S3 keys instead of Metaflow artifacts. Swap fetch_links and read_shapefile for your own list and processing functions, and the rest of the flow (batching, retries, resume, the DuckDB join) carries over as is.

And through all of it, I never wrote a Dockerfile or created a bucket. The platform handled the setup I’d usually dread as a Python developer.
