SR 26-2, the 2026 replacement for SR 11-7, takes generative AI and AI agents out of US bank model risk guidance. The obligations around those systems still apply, so someone has to test them. Here’s how I think banks, and the insurers and asset managers facing the same questions, should close that gap.

In April, the Federal Reserve, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) rewrote the model risk management guidance that guides how banks manage the risk of their models. The old version, known as SR 11-7, had been in place since 2011, and model risk teams across the country built their programs around it. The new version, SR 26-2, keeps most of the same shape. One footnote stopped me: generative AI and AI agents are out of scope.

Think about what that means from inside a bank. The chatbots answering customer questions, the copilots drafting advice, the agents with access to core systems: the technology moving fastest inside financial institutions no longer sits under the framework designed to check it. The agencies say these systems are too new and changing too quickly to cover yet, and they plan to ask the industry for input.

I understand the reasoning. I also know how this plays out. When a system falls outside the process, nobody clearly owns testing it. Meanwhile, the rules that matter most to customers haven’t gone anywhere. Fair lending still applies. So do consumer protection, third-party risk, cybersecurity, and recordkeeping. A bank that deploys an AI assistant owns what that assistant says.

SR 26-2 is supervisory guidance, not a rule, and it says non-compliance won’t draw supervisory criticism. Supervisors can still act on violations of law and on unsafe or unsound practices, and the generative AI footnote tells banks to apply their own risk management and governance to these systems.

So here’s the question I’d put to risk and compliance leaders right now: which of your AI systems sit outside your model inventory today, and who is checking them?

When we test these systems, the failures rarely look like a movie hack. They look like ordinary business problems. A customer assistant quotes a fee that isn’t in the disclosure. An internal copilot shows one customer’s account details to a colleague who isn’t cleared to see them. A claims assistant tells a policyholder something is covered when it isn’t, or handles two nearly identical claims differently. An advisor tool recommends a product that doesn’t fit the client, or passes along information that was supposed to stay behind a wall. These are illustrations, not findings from our upcoming report, but they will sound familiar to people who have worked at a bank, an insurer, or an asset manager.

None of them needs an attacker. They happen when a system does exactly what it was built to do, in a situation nobody thought to test. Our Agent Incident Registry shows the pattern: as of September 21, 2026, 109 of the 529 agent incidents it documents involved no adversary at all. That’s about one in five.

Agent Incident Registry

That’s why I believe red teaming belongs at the center of how financial institutions govern AI. Red teaming means testing a system the way the real world will, before customers or examiners do. At Enkrypt AI, we built our approach around a few convictions. Test the whole experience the customer meets, including the tools the AI can use and the data it can reach, because nobody meets a model on its own. Start from the institution’s own obligations, because a claims assistant and an investment copilot fail in different ways. Keep the pressure on the way real people do, by rephrasing, pushing back, and switching languages. And leave behind evidence a risk team can file, map to frameworks like the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act, and hand to an auditor.

Who owns the testing

SR 26-2 doesn’t prescribe an org chart, and that flexibility helps here. It says the quality of validation depends on the rigor and effectiveness of the review rather than on how the risk function is organized, and that effective challenge needs expertise, independence, and the standing to get changes made. It also says internal audit’s role is to evaluate whether the program is rigorous and effective, not to duplicate the work, and that when a bank uses outside resources, it should keep proper oversight and fold that work into its own program.

Applied to AI outside the guidance, that suggests a simple split. The team that builds or runs the system owns the controls and the fixes. An independent group owns the challenge: model risk, security, or an outside red team. Internal audit checks that the whole program works. And I’d add one more: a named business owner who decides go or no-go with the findings in hand.

Three pillars for AI that sits outside SR 26-2

Red teaming is one leg of a larger program. The models SR 26-2 still covers come with an established playbook: they are inventoried, validated before first use, monitored as conditions change, documented, and owned by named people, with effective challenge running through all of it. The guidance says its principles apply to traditional statistical models and non-generative AI, and none of that stops mattering for generative AI and agents. But an agent adds two things a credit model never had: it can act, and it works alongside people. That changes what the playbook has to cover.

I’d build the rest of the program on three pillars. The first extends what SR 26-2 asks of models today. The second and third also draw on security and internal-control practice, because the guidance was written for systems that produce estimates. Agents take actions.

Traceability

For a credit model, a validator can rerun the inputs and see what it did. A generative system can answer the same question differently twice, and an agent’s “decision” is a chain of steps across tools. So the record has to capture the chain: what the system was asked, which instructions and documents it was working from, which tools it called, what it returned, what a human reviewed, and which versions of the model, prompts, and tools were live at the time. SR 26-2 asks for inventory and documentation so a bank can understand model risk, keep operations continuous, and track remediation. The same logic applies here, with a bigger job. It also asks for ongoing monitoring, to catch a model that stops performing as expected when products, clients, or data change. For agents, that means re-testing whenever a model, prompt, tool, or data source changes, and watching behavior in production. Red teaming tests whether the trail holds up. If a finding can be reproduced step by step, a failure in production can be reconstructed too.

Permissioning

A model produces an estimate. An agent can read records, update accounts, send messages, and start payments. So the question moves from “was the answer right?” to “should it have been able to do that at all?” SR 26-2 warns that using a model beyond its intended purpose adds risk, and for agents, permissions are how you enforce the intended purpose. That means least privilege scoped to the task, an identity of its own rather than borrowed human credentials or a shared service account, access granted when the agent is approved and removed when it’s retired, approval steps for high-impact actions, and segregation of duties, so the agent that prepares a payment doesn’t also release it. It also means a way to stop it: a documented switch-off, criteria for when to use it, a named person who can pull it, and a plan for who responds when an agent misbehaves. NIST’s Generative AI Profile asks for protocols to deactivate AI systems and criteria for when to do so.

It also means vetted tools and connectors. SR 26-2 says its principles still apply to vendor products when a bank can’t see the code, and it flags aggregate risk when many models share assumptions or data. The same logic applies when many agents rest on one foundation model (a large general-purpose model that many applications are built on) or call the same connectors. In our scans, 73% of 25,000 MCP servers, the connectors that let agents use outside tools, had vulnerabilities. NIST has started on this too: its AI Agent Standards Initiative includes a draft concept paper on agent identity and authorization. A red team tries to talk the agent past its limits, through privilege escalation, role bypass, and connector abuse.

Agents as colleagues

The third pillar is the one I think banks are least ready for. Agents are becoming the point where technology meets the workforce. They take requests from employees, work in the same systems, and hand work to people and receive it back. This pillar is about who answers for the agent and how people and agents work together. SR 26-2 asks for clear roles and well-defined accountability, and that carries over. Every agent needs a named owner who answers for its behavior, and supervisors with the information and the authority to override it. The people working beside agents need training too, because people tend to over-trust an assistant that sounds sure of itself.

The riskiest moments are the handoffs. Picture an agent that drafts account adjustments for an analyst to approve. If the analyst assumes the agent checked the source documents, and the agent assumes the analyst will, the error passes through both. The colleague comparison has limits. An agent can be copied overnight, and it can’t be held accountable the way a person can, so a flaw in its setup shows up everywhere at once. That makes the human owner more important, and accountability doesn’t move: the bank and named people own what the agent does. A red team probes the seams. Does the agent escalate when it should? Can it be pressured to skip an approval? What does it do when someone senior-sounding asks for something outside its role?

Scale the effort to the risk

You don’t need all three pillars at full strength for every system. SR 26-2 is risk-based: it scales oversight to a model’s exposure and purpose, and says low-materiality models can be limited to identification and monitoring, while higher-materiality models warrant more comprehensive oversight. I’d apply the same thinking to AI outside the guidance by asking three questions. What can it do: only read, or also act? Who does it touch: employees only, or customers? Does it inform regulated decisions or handle regulated data?

A read-only internal summarizer needs an inventory entry and basic monitoring. A customer-facing assistant needs the full program. An agent that can change records or move money needs the full program, plus approval gates on its high-impact actions.

FAQ

What is SR 26-2?

SR 26-2 is the revised model risk management guidance the Federal Reserve, the OCC, and the FDIC issued on April 17, 2026. It replaces SR 11-7 from 2011 and is most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets. It is supervisory guidance, not a rule. It says non-compliance won’t draw supervisory criticism, though supervisors can still act on violations of law and unsafe or unsound practices.

Does SR 26-2 cover generative AI or AI agents?

No. A footnote in the guidance places generative AI and agentic AI models (systems that can take actions, such as calling tools or updating records) outside its scope because they are new and changing quickly. For those systems, banks are pointed to their broader risk management and governance practices instead.

Is generative AI in banking unregulated now?

No. The exclusion applies only to the model risk management guidance. Fair lending, consumer protection, third-party risk management, cybersecurity, and recordkeeping obligations still apply to any AI system a bank deploys.

Does SR 26-2 apply to insurers and asset managers?

Not directly. SR 26-2 is guidance for banking organizations, so it reaches insurers and asset managers only when they’re part of one. Insurers answer to state insurance regulators, and as of October 8, 2026, 27 states, D.C., and Puerto Rico have adopted the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) model bulletin on insurers’ use of AI, which expects a written AI program with senior-management accountability, testing, and oversight of third-party AI. Broker-dealers answer to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), whose Regulatory Notice 24-09 says its rules apply to generative AI. Registered investment advisers answer to the SEC or to state securities regulators.

Who should own testing for AI that SR 26-2 doesn’t cover?

Split it four ways. The team that builds or runs the system owns the controls and fixes. An independent group (model risk, security, or an outside red team) owns the challenge. Internal audit checks that the program works. A named business owner decides go or no-go. SR 26-2 supports this flexibility: it says validation quality depends on the rigor of the review, not on how the risk function is organized.

How can banks test generative AI that falls outside SR 26-2?

Through independent red teaming: adversarial testing of the full workflow a customer or employee uses, built from the institution’s own obligations. Findings are documented and mapped to frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act, so they can go into a risk register or an audit file.

What does traceability mean for an AI agent?

It means being able to reconstruct what the agent did and why. A complete record covers the request, the instructions and documents the agent worked from, the tools it called, what it returned, any human review, and the versions of the model, prompts, and tools in use at the time. SR 26-2 asks for inventory, documentation, and ongoing monitoring for the models it covers, and the same discipline applies to AI outside its scope.

Should AI agents have their own permissions?

Yes. An agent should have an identity of its own, with least-privilege access scoped to its task, granted when it’s approved and removed when it’s retired, rather than borrowing a human’s credentials or sharing a service account. High-impact actions should need approval, duties should be split so the agent that prepares a payment doesn’t also release it, and the tools and connectors it can call should be vetted. There should also be a documented way to switch the agent off. NIST’s AI Agent Standards Initiative includes a draft concept paper on agent identity and authorization.

How many AI agent incidents involve no attacker?

About one in five. As of September 21, 2026, 109 of the 529 agent incidents in Enkrypt AI’s Agent Incident Registry involved no adversary. These failures happen when a system behaves as built in a situation nobody tested.

When do EU AI Act high-risk rules apply to credit scoring and insurance pricing?

From December 2, 2027. The Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on July 27, 2026 and moved the deadline for stand-alone high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act from August 2, 2026. Those systems include AI used to score individuals’ creditworthiness and AI used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance.

Over the past year, while Git traffic grew exponentially, 99.99999% of the Git repositories on GitHub began living in two places at once: our own data centers and Azure. The rest are among the busiest on the platform, and they’re held back by just one thing: a 25ms round trip.

In an earlier blog post, we laid out the two principles behind our next-generation storage architecture:

  • Minimize coordination
  • Decouple storage from compute

In this post, we’ll look at the immediate bridge between our old design and our new one. We’ll talk about how a change to the way our quorum serializes writes gets the busiest repositories into Azure, and how that same step opens the door to replicas that keep no persistent state of their own.

How we got here

The timing and shape of the move were driven by the agentic shift: rapidly growing traffic, a hard ceiling on the power capacity of our data centers and the surrounding geographical region, and physical supply chain constraints. At the beginning of our migration, the remaining capacity with all possible expansion gave us enough time to migrate safely, but not enough to do it slowly.

While we had prototyped new architectural approaches for scaling Git on cloud infrastructure, changing the storage architecture and the hosting platform at the same time, under that growth, carried too much risk. Given our scale and how critical the service is to the community, we needed to sequence the work carefully: move the data first, then evolve the architecture once it arrived.

Git operations are a Tier 0 service: they’re a core function of GitHub, and nearly everything in GitHub depends on the repository data. That meant the data, services, and every dependency had to move first. The first copy of a repository landed in Azure’s Central US region in August 2025. Today, most repositories have two of their five replicas in Azure, and Azure serves about half of Git traffic. For most of that time, Azure absorbed our growth while our own data centers held steady.

During the migration, traffic follows the data. Most repositories have five replicas to balance durability, availability, and scale. To minimize read latency, Spokes uses preferential routing to direct traffic to a replica in the same region and availability zone as the request. As replicas move to Azure, the share of the traffic that can be served from Azure grows with each one, until the last replica arrives and the old region stops receiving requests entirely. No repository left behind

So, what about the remaining 0.00001% of repositories?

In our current architecture, a repository hosted in more than one region has a lower write throughput ceiling. Spokes’ quorum only needs a majority to commit a write, but it waits for every healthy replica to respond (up to a timeout) before moving on, so slow replicas delay every write rather than falling behind. In practice, that means the slowest healthy replica sets the latency of every write, and because each write holds a repository-wide lock until it commits, it also sets the throughput ceiling. Once a replica lives in the target Azure region, it’s about 1,000 miles from our data centers, making each network round trip around 25ms, and a write requires multiple round trips. A repository already near its throughput ceiling can’t absorb that on every serialized write; it would fall further behind with every push.

This is where our first principle, minimizing coordination, comes into play. Specifically for this case: don’t serialize what doesn’t need to be. Git’s consistency requirements apply to each reference individually. Once the server accepts an update to a reference, no client should see an older value afterward, only that value or a newer one. That holds whether the update is a fast-forward, a force push, or a delete. But in most cases, updates to different references don’t depend on each other and can be applied independently.

So, we’re changing the scope of the quorum from the whole repository to individual references.

Holding a repository-wide lock for the entire reference update, round trips included, turns the quorum into a single, unordered request queue with one write in flight at a time. Each write in that queue takes as long as the slowest healthy replica. As the write rate grows, so does the queue, and each write waits longer than the one before it.

With per-reference serialization, only writes that contend on a shared reference must wait for each other. In practice, most writes to a repository touch different references—for example, agents and developers pushing to their own branches—so they no longer wait on each other. Each write still experiences the cross-region latency but writes to different references can be in flight at the same time. That removes the single bottleneck on the repository. These changes raise a repository’s write throughput ceiling roughly in proportion to how many references are being written at once, and get the last remaining repositories into Azure. In internal testing with realistic traffic, writes to different references consistently saw 3–4x higher throughput at lower latency.

Scaling reads on their own

Most repositories are heavily read-skewed, with somewhere between 100 and 1,000 reads for every write. Today, reads and writes hit the same replicas, so they compete for the same resources and can’t be scaled independently.

Decoupling storage from compute changes that. Once repository data is durably stored in Azure Blob Storage and updated with each incoming write, we can begin to decouple read traffic from the write quorum, and independently scale the compute needed for each. For example, reads would benefit from far more in-memory cache than we give them today, but that memory is also needed for compaction. Once they’re separated, each gets its own configuration.

The first step is making object storage a member of the quorum, but a special one that we call a virtual replica. The compute that represents it is our first fully ephemeral node. It serves as a bridge to object storage, streaming incoming writes and serving no reads. Because it’s a member of the quorum, a write isn’t acknowledged until it’s durable in object storage.

That adds a little latency to each write, but just like the cross-region round trip, per-reference serialization keeps it from becoming a throughput bottleneck. The virtual write replica also coalesces transactions on disjoint references into a single set of network I/Os to object storage, so a busy repository pays for one round trip across many concurrent writes instead of one per write. For now, the virtual write replica also handles compaction, and read replicas inherit the compacted state.

With the virtual write replica in place, we can scale out as many virtual read replicas as a repository needs, on demand. Each read replica warms its cache from Azure Blob Storage and starts taking read traffic without joining the quorum, using object storage as the source of truth and reconciling its local state against it in the background.

This step unlocks significant read scale. In internal testing, our distributed load testing harness routinely found ceilings in the current architecture. Against virtual read replicas, the harness hit its own limits first. It also comes with a few challenges that are solved in subsequent steps:

  1. Virtual read replicas start out reconciling with object storage in the background, which makes them a good fit for some read workloads but not all of them. Serving every kind of read requires moving that reconciliation in-line, fetching only the reference state and data that changed since the replica last caught up.
  2. While we’ve incrementally increased write throughput by shifting to a per-reference model, the quorum remains the bounding factor for increasing further.

How can we push write throughput further?

What comes next

The GitHub of today was built on the Spokes quorum, and over the years Spokes has become much more than a quorum coordinator. It’s also an intelligent routing and load balancing layer, a unified API that internal clients use to access and transact repository data at well over 100 billion operations per day, and a collection of background systems we call “sweepers” that handle repository placement, repair, and rebalancing between storage nodes.

The vast majority of what lives under the Spokes service umbrella applies just as well to the new architecture as it does to the existing one. But we also recognize that the commit protocol that has taken GitHub to a billion repositories won’t take it to the next billion and beyond.

We’re not done yet. With these steps in place, the next limit is write throughput. Every write still waits on replicas that each hold a full copy of the repository, yet very little of a write actually needs to be ordered. Git objects are immutable and named by their contents, so they can be stored without anyone agreeing on them. What’s left is small, and the hard part is ordering it as fast as possible without moving the bottleneck somewhere else. Designing for that on a whiteboard is one thing. Doing it underneath a billion repositories that developers and agents are writing to around the clock is another. Getting there forced us to rethink the second-order effects too, like where durable reference state lives and how compaction and garbage collection scale.

Agent traffic isn’t slowing down, and each of these steps gives repositories more headroom as it lands, without waiting for the end state.

AI coding tools can generate code quickly, but speed alone does not solve the hardest software engineering problems. Developers still need to provide context, validate results, protect maintainability, and remain accountable for what ships.

To learn how experienced developers strike that balance, we spoke with three Java Champions, all with over a decade of experience in using Java. They shared concrete examples of using GitHub Copilot in production and open source, along with the practices they use to turn AI-generated output into reliable software.

Here are the tips they shared with us.

Meet the Java Champions

  • Vlad Mihalcea, founder of Hypersistence and author of the book High-Performance Java Persistence
  • Patrick Baumgartner, software crafter at 42talents with 20+ years of Java experience
  • Frank Delporte, senior technical writer at Azul and maintainer of Foojay.io with 15+ years of Java experience

Vlad Mihalcea

GitHub profile: @vladmihalcea

How has GitHub Copilot helped you recently?

I’ve been using it to analyze, design, and implement various features in both Hypersistence Utils and Hypersistence Optimizer.

For example, I’ve been wanting for a long time to develop a web application that provides Optimizer event history so we can see how performance improvement efforts are materializing in application performance. Before AI, I kept postponing this task because it would have taken me weeks to develop. But using Copilot is like having a new team member, so I used it to create an app that will ship in the next version of Hypersistence Optimizer. Instead of weeks, this task took just two days to implement.

What’s your typical usage? Are you using GitHub Copilot as an IDE plugin or the GitHub Copilot app?

I use GitHub Copilot in IntelliJ IDEA because it’s convenient to run while reviewing source code or during a debug session.

It’s very useful as a code assistant, and it helps me speed up both investigations and building prototypes for features that need validation.

I still review the plans and the code that is generated by AI since their goal is to improve my productivity, not replace me entirely. While AI provides the right answer in many situations, there are still times when the response is not satisfactory and requires my intervention.

Can you share some tips and best practices? For example, which skills are you using?

I created my own skills so that whatever text Copilot generates looks like something I wrote by hand.

This skill captures my voice from 65 of my published articles, so a draft comes back sounding like my blog instead of like a language model. This is really useful when generating documentation for this project, since it lets the LLM learn from 12 years of blogging and provide examples that follow the same principles, such as expressiveness, testability, and simplicity.

Throughout my career as a software engineer, I’ve learned that we spend far more time on code maintenance than on creating it. Therefore, because maintenance and stability are critical for existing products, I developed skills that help AI mimic existing code practices in accordance with the principle of least astonishment.

One tip is to change the model type based on the type of operation. For thorough thinking, I use Opus with the 1 million context window and Extra High or Max reasoning. However, for simpler tasks, such as generating reports, release notes, or verifying documentation, I use lighter models that consume fewer tokens.

Patrick Baumgartner

GitHub profile: @patbaumgartner

Your work involves coaching teams. What is your approach?

A lot of my work happens while the normal business still has to keep running. You cannot stop everything for six months to “transform.” We usually start by solving immediate problems together, understand where the team currently stands, define where we want to go and how we will know that we are improving, and then move there step by step.

Depending on the situation, that can mean Software Craft practices such as TDD, refactoring, continuous delivery and XP; architecture and modularisation with Spring Boot, Spring Modulith; Spring Security; optimising Java and Spring Boot applications for containers; or increasingly, agentic engineering.

The important part for me is that I work with the team. We make decisions together. I don’t want a team to become dependent on me. Ideally, after some time they no longer need me except perhaps as a sparring partner.

And maybe that explains why I find this new generation of AI tools so interesting: I like working with people, learning, experimenting, and solving problems. AI gives us another very powerful tool for doing exactly that.

Can you share a good story about how GitHub Copilot has helped you recently?

There are actually three very different examples that changed how I think about working with GitHub Copilot.

Let’s start with the first one.

One was a migration of around 900 teaching slides across 15 presentations from Reveal.js to Marp. These were not simple slides. Many contain code, animations, and even interactive examples where I can change JavaScript live while teaching. More importantly, the presentations had already been battle-tested with students. The flow worked. Losing one small piece could break the story I use in the classroom.

Doing that migration manually would probably have taken me a week or more—and realistically, I might simply have avoided doing it because it was such tedious work.

With Copilot, I approached it differently. We first analysed the existing material, defined the target, created a migration plan, established constraints, provided examples, and defined what had to remain intact. Then we worked from the big structural changes down into the details.

The really important part was verification.

We generated screenshots of the Reveal.js and Marp versions and compared them visually and algorithmically. We built a small application where I could run both slide decks next to each other to verify animations manually. We even compared the generated PDFs.

That meant the agent did not just say, “Migration complete.” We had evidence.

The entire migration took me roughly eight to ten hours. I still owned the content, the teaching intent, and every meaningful change. Copilot could fix a typo automatically, but if content, versions, dates or meaning changed, that was something we discussed.

For me, that experience reinforced an important principle: delegate execution, not responsibility. I own the result.

Can you share the second example?

A second example came from building a terminal UI application with Tamboui. It initially worked perfectly in Linux under WSL, and I had completely forgotten that somebody might run it directly in Windows CMD or PowerShell.

And then a real user did.

They discovered that the Enter key was simply ignored in some forms. That is another principle I strongly believe in: ship software early, because you learn from how people actually use it.

We used Copilot to turn that feedback into reproducible UI tests. On Linux, Copilot was navigating the application with tmux. Through working with it, I learned about psmux and could execute similar flows in PowerShell. We reproduced the problem, created a local fix, traced the real problem into Tamboui, and I submitted the upstream fix, which was merged.

During this work, the test descriptions themselves became too large for the available context. So I asked Copilot to help me redesign the way the tests were represented. Instead of verbose tables containing identifiers, names and repeated descriptions, it proposed a much more compact arrow notation: step → step → input, with additional information only where it was critical.

That surprised me. We were not just using AI to execute information; we were working together on how information should be represented for execution. It did not have to be maximally human-friendly. It had to preserve the semantics while being efficient for the agent.

How about the third example?

The third example is completely different again.

Someone close to me needed to practise mathematics, and I thought: why don’t we build a small learning app?

It covers addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and division with remainder for grades one through four. But I did not want to build another simple quiz application. Together with Copilot, I researched learning approaches such as spaced repetition, immediate feedback, adaptive difficulty, motivation, cooperative learning and gamification without dark patterns.

Interestingly, during that research Copilot challenged one of my original assumptions. I initially wanted the application to be generic for children everywhere. The research led us toward looking at the actual school curriculum instead. I realised that the order in which topics are introduced, the operations and number ranges differ enough that making it “universal” immediately was unnecessary.

So we focused on Switzerland first and aligned the progression accordingly. Because Switzerland is multilingual, the application is available in German, French, Italian and English.

It also has cooperative and competitive two-player modes, and when someone gets an answer wrong, the application explains one way to solve it and brings similar questions back through spaced repetition.

I deliberately chose an architecture without a backend. There is no reason for me to collect children’s personal data on a server, so the data stays on the device and the application can simply be hosted on GitHub Pages.

What started as a small experiment is now being used and shared among friends. It accidentally became a little product.

That experience showed me another side of Copilot: don’t only use it to implement your solution. Use it as a sparring partner to expand and challenge the solution space before you start building.

How do you typically use Copilot?

I actually don’t use the classic Copilot autocomplete experience anymore.

My work has moved almost completely toward agentic workflows.

I use VS Code when I need a tight visual feedback loop—when I want to look closely at documents, code, presentations or intermediate results and adapt the process as I go.

For larger execution-oriented tasks, I often work with the coding agent from the terminal. There I am more comfortable delegating a piece of work, letting it execute, and then inspecting the complete result. The interface I choose depends mostly on the feedback loop I need.

I also had a fascinating experience using the coding-agent workflow through GitHub while travelling to JavaLand. I had started exploring an idea for a Maven plugin that optimises which Spring Boot auto-configurations are loaded. During roughly a four-hour journey, I was steering the agent from my phone: giving it a task, reviewing what it had done, thinking about the next step, and sending the next instruction.

By the time I arrived, I had a working Maven plugin, a CI pipeline, performance tests against a sample application, and reports that could demonstrate whether the optimisation actually worked.

That was the first time I had worked that way, and I was amazed that it actually worked. I wasn’t trying to write Java on my phone. I was reviewing, deciding and steering while the agent executed.

I also use MCP servers when they give me useful capabilities or context. For example, I have used Context7 for documentation, integrations with my Logseq knowledge base, and I have experimented with an MCP integration for Adobe Premiere Pro.

But I don’t use MCP just because I can. If a good CLI exists, I often prefer the CLI because it feels more direct and faster to me.

The agent is also not a “junior developer” in my mental model. I think of it much more as a sparring partner. But just like anyone joining a project, it first needs to be brought up to speed.

Context is king.

Can you share some tips and best practices?

The biggest thing I would recommend is: stop one-shot prompting.

For anything non-trivial, I want to understand the problem before execution starts. Quite often I explicitly ask the agent to interview me first. The purpose of that interview is to uncover assumptions and expectations that are still implicit.

A good agent can execute the wrong idea incredibly quickly. That does not make the detour cheaper.

I experienced this myself while working on my Spring Boot auto-configuration optimiser. Initially I wanted to support both Spring Boot 3.5 and 4.x. There were architectural differences, so we implemented both approaches. Later I realised I no longer needed the 3.5 path and removing it was painful.

The lesson was simple: detours are expensive. Get the requirements right before you accelerate.

Once the intent is clear, I want a plan. When I review that plan, four things matter especially to me: are assumptions visible, are verification gates defined, does the approach fit the architecture, and does it avoid unnecessary work?

The second principle is to define task-specific verification gates.

“Done” should not mean that the model says it is done.

For a refactoring, the gates might include passing tests, proving that all intended files were handled, updating documentation and leaving no TODOs behind. For the slide migration, the gates included screenshots, visual diffs, animations, PDF output and content completeness.

The gates depend on the task. The principle does not: the agent should be able to provide evidence that the work satisfies the definition of done.

I recently came across a skill called unlazy that follows a similar idea: create explicit gates, work through them deliberately, and prove that they have been completed rather than taking shortcuts.

The third principle is to turn your lessons into reusable skills.

I have started writing and maintaining my own Agent Skills. When I learn something from an execution—perhaps the agent misunderstood a convention, a useful check was missing, or I found a better way to approach a task—I update the skill.

The next time I perform that task, the lesson is already there.

One example is a skill I call maintainer-audit, inspired by work in the open agent skills community. I use it across my own repositories. It checks and, where appropriate, fixes the things I expect a maintained project to have: repository documentation, CI pipelines, Dependabot configuration, CodeQL, consistent cleanup and other project standards.

The goal is not to generate another audit report for me to read. It brings the repository back toward the baseline I want automatically. That makes my projects more consistent and easier to maintain.

I think of these skills as living engineering knowledge rather than static prompts.

The fourth principle is to put deterministic engineering tools around the probabilistic model.

I have my own quality harness that wraps tools such as Checkstyle, PMD and Spring Modulith architecture checks and produces a consolidated report that the agent can consume. I can run that from hooks and let the agent iterate:

change → execute checks → read findings → fix → execute again.

It continues until there are no findings left.

That does not mean I stop looking at the software. I still run the application, interact with it and decide whether it behaves and looks the way I expect. Sometimes I deliberately implement feature by feature because the feedback loop is much tighter.

I learned that asking for everything at once because “the AI can do it” is usually a bad idea. Large, generic specifications create large, generic implementations—and cleaning them up afterwards can be harder than building smaller increments correctly.

More broadly, I think we developers need to talk much more about how we use these tools. Share the experiments that worked. Share the ones that didn’t. Borrow ideas from each other and adapt them to your own context instead of looking for one universal AI development process.

That is also why I like the idea of AI as a sparring partner. On a Friday night, when I want to experiment with something and no human pair is available, I suddenly have somebody—or something—I can challenge an idea with, review an approach with, or use as a very sophisticated rubber duck.

But I still take those ideas back to real people. Human feedback, product understanding and real-world context remain essential.

How do you think AI is changing our profession?

I don’t think software engineering students should primarily aspire to become code-producing machines. They need to become strong problem solvers. They need to understand users and stakeholders, discover the real problem behind a requirement, challenge assumptions, understand products and markets, make architectural trade-offs, think critically, work in a structured way and transfer useful solutions from one context into another.

The model is extraordinarily good at generating possibilities and executing things. But as a human, I usually know much more of the actual context—including the things nobody ever documented.

So I want to remain in charge of shaping the solution and let the agent take over more of the execution.

In that sense, I think AI is putting the engineering back into software engineering.

And I like that.

Frank Delporte

GitHub profile: @FDelporte

How did you get started in tech?

I’ve been fascinated by computers since I got a Commodore 64 when I was 11. Took some side steps into video production and television work after school but turned back into coding as that video needed to be put on DVD and the very first websites… Yes, I’m that old ;-).

How has GitHub Copilot helped you recently?

It’s a perfect companion to evaluate pull requests and investigate bug reports.

One remarkable story is about the Pi4J library that lets you build Java applications to interact with electronic components on the Raspberry Pi. In February 2026, we released V4 which has a new plugin using the Foreign Function & Memory API. We tested it thoroughly before releasing, but still, shortly after the release someone reported that using 5 input buttons doesn’t work as expected. Pushing one of the first four configured buttons triggers a code execution as expected, while nothing happens with the fifth button.

This is the kind of report you don’t want to get. Nothing was visibly broken. No exception, no warning in the logs, the fifth button was simply silent. And it got worse: with the older gpiod plugin, the exact same hardware and the exact same application, worked fine. So the problem was in our new FFM code, which sits on top of the Linux kernel’s GPIO character device and talks to it through native calls. That is not the part of the codebase where you expect to find a quick answer.

So I assigned the issue to the Copilot coding agent and gave it two file paths as a starting point, the provider class and FFMDigitalInput. About half an hour later there was a pull request, with an explanation I really did not expect.

Each input runs an event watcher that blocks in a native poll() call, waiting for the kernel to report a change on its GPIO line. Those watchers ran in virtual threads. And a virtual thread that blocks inside a native call cannot be unmounted from its carrier thread. It stays pinned there for the whole duration of the call. A Raspberry Pi 4 has four cores, so the virtual thread scheduler has four carrier threads. Register four inputs, and all four carriers are parked in poll() forever. The fifth watcher is created, but is never scheduled. That is exactly the bug that was reported. Not “events are flaky”, but “the fifth one does nothing.”

What I liked most was not the diff, it was the conversation that followed in the review. I left a comment asking Copilot to explain this in more detail:

I thought the goal of virtual threads was exactly to step away from the platform thread limit? So how is this a problem that four virtual threads block the four threads on a four-core processor like the Raspberry Pi?

Check the pull request for the full conversation. Copilot was able to link the use of the Raspberry Pi which has four CPU cores with the reported problem. It was able to propose a simple fix for the problem, plus some extra improvements it found during its investigation.

Can you share some tips and best practices?

  • Tell it where to look: For the Pi4J issue #622, I added two file paths. That is a tiny bit of effort that removes a huge amount of searching in a large codebase. You usually have a hunch about the neighbourhood even when you have no idea about the bug. Give it to the tool so it doesn’t need to investigate the complete code.
  • Ask for proof and tests: “Add a unit test to validate the change” is something I ask a lot these days. Well, asked, because it has now become a rule in my instructions file. It also prevents cases where the diagnosis was wrong, because the test won’t fail on the old code.
  • Ask why: If you don’t understand a fix, say so and ask. The explanation is a deliverable too and should be added into your project documentation or inside the ticket or pull request. It teaches everyone who reads the info later. For example, this also triggered more questions within the Pi4J team about virtual thread pinning on native calls.
  • Stay in control: All of these pull requests in Pi4J were reviewed and approved by maintainers before they went in. That’s the model that works best in this project: the agent does the investigation, the humans stay responsible for what ships. But also for my other projects I always do a review of the code that was generated by the tool. I always want to keep code readable and maintainable. Too often, a tool creates duplicate methods or recreates something which is already available in another place and should be combined. That’s your responsibility. You can go very fast these days with AI-assisted coding, which also means you can very fast create a mess. Don’t do that, keep your code manageable, your colleagues and future-self will thank you if they have to investigate bugs or make improvements later.

If you run AI in production, you’re likely aware that AI regulations are regularly evolving. Some EU dates have already moved, US state laws keep getting rewritten, and in our customer reviews, buyers treat voluntary frameworks as mandatory. Here’s where each one stands this fall and what’s still coming before the end of 2027.

The EU AI Act hit its biggest date so far, and EU lawmakers pushed back the next one

August 2, 2026 was the general application date for the EU AI Act. From that date, the Article 50 transparency rules apply. In short:

  • AI interactions: providers must design systems like chatbots so people know they’re talking to an AI, unless that’s already obvious.
  • Synthetic content: providers of AI that generates audio, images, video, or text must mark the output in a machine-readable format so it can be detected as AI-generated.
  • Emotion recognition and biometric categorization: deployers must tell people when these systems are being used on them.
  • Deepfakes and public-interest text: deployers must disclose deepfakes, and label AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless a human has reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility.

Providers of generative AI systems already on the market before August 2, 2026 have until December 2, 2026 to meet the Article 50(2) marking requirement. As of August 2, the European AI Office, the European Commission body that oversees general-purpose AI models, has the power to enforce the rules for those models.

The high-risk obligations didn’t arrive on schedule. The EU’s “Digital Omnibus on AI” (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since July 27, 2026) delayed the heaviest requirements, including conformity assessments, risk management systems, and human oversight. They now apply from December 2, 2027 for Annex III high-risk systems and August 2, 2028 for Annex I systems embedded in products.

Annex III covers AI used for specific sensitive purposes, such as hiring, credit scoring, education, biometrics, and access to essential services. Annex I covers AI built into products already regulated under EU safety law, such as medical devices or machinery. To see where your systems fall, map each one’s purpose against those lists. For a step-by-step walkthrough, see How to Navigate the EU AI Act.

The obligations are delayed, not cancelled. The delay has drawn criticism from both industry and civil society. Industry association DIGITALEUROPE says the delay alone isn’t enough and the rules still need deeper fixes. In a joint statement, digital-rights groups EDRi, Access Now, and the European Center for Not-for-Profit Law (ECNL), with Amnesty International, say the final deal “delays key protections, weakens transparency and creates a dangerous precedent for the EU digital rulebook.” For compliance teams, the useful part is that the dates are now written into law, so you can plan against them.

NIST is becoming the common reference point

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework can’t be enforced on its own, but laws and frameworks keep pointing to it. Two developments to watch:

  • AI Agent Standards Initiative. On February 17, 2026, NIST’s Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) launched this initiative to close the gap left by governing agents the same way as regular generative AI. It rests on three pillars: industry-led standards, community-led open-source protocols, and technical research into agent security and identity.
  • Critical infrastructure profile. In April 2026, NIST published a concept note for an AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) Profile on Trustworthy AI in Critical Infrastructure. It’s meant to guide critical infrastructure operators on risk management practices when they use AI. It’s still under discussion with a NIST-convened community of interest, and no draft has been published.

Neither effort has produced finished guidance yet. What they show is where NIST is putting its attention: agents and high-stakes sectors.

State laws show NIST’s influence more directly. Colorado’s original AI Act (SB 24-205) gave organizations an affirmative defense if they found and fixed violations, for example through red teaming or internal review, and otherwise followed a recognized framework such as the NIST AI RMF or ISO/IEC 42001. The replacement law, SB 26-189 (signed May 14, 2026, effective January 1, 2027), swapped that defense for a 60-day cure period and dropped the risk management program and impact assessment requirements. Texas took a different approach. The Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act (TRAIGA), signed in June 2025 and in effect since January 1, 2026, gives companies a defense if they discover violations through feedback, adversarial or red-team testing, or state agency guidelines. A separate defense covers internal review, where a company substantially complies with NIST’s Generative AI Profile or another recognized AI risk framework.

OWASP and MITRE ATLAS: the technical baseline under the legal one

The Open Worldwide Application Security Project’s (OWASP) Top 10 for Large Language Model (LLM) Applications and MITRE ATLAS give security and compliance teams a shared technical vocabulary. OWASP lists vulnerability classes like prompt injection, improper output handling, and data and model poisoning. ATLAS lists the tactics and techniques attackers use against AI systems.

Neither one comes with a fine, but in our work with enterprise customers, both come up regularly in vendor security questionnaires and procurement reviews. If you can’t map your system’s risks to at least one of them, your testing probably hasn’t gone deep enough.

The US: no comprehensive federal law, a growing state patchwork

The US still has no comprehensive federal AI law. A December 2025 executive order pushes for a single national standard and challenges state AI laws, but Congress hasn’t passed preemption. For now, the states set the rules:

  • Texas’s TRAIGA has been in effect since January 1, 2026.
  • California’s Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53), in effect since January 1, 2026, requires large frontier model developers to publish safety frameworks and transparency reports and to report critical safety incidents to the state.
  • Illinois’s HB 3773, in effect since January 1, 2026, makes it a civil rights violation for employers to use AI that discriminates against protected groups, and requires them to tell workers and applicants when AI is used in employment decisions.
  • Colorado’s SB 26-189 takes effect January 1, 2027.
  • New York City’s Local Law 144 still requires bias audits for automated hiring tools.

These laws differ from one another. Many multinationals choose to build to the EU AI Act’s higher bar and adapt for US operations instead of running separate compliance programs.

What to watch heading into 2027

The deadlines still ahead, and who each one affects:

  • December 2, 2026: providers of generative AI systems already on the EU market before August 2, 2026 must meet the Article 50(2) content-marking requirement.
  • January 1, 2027: Colorado’s Automated Decision-Making Technology (ADMT) law takes effect.
  • December 2, 2027: EU high-risk obligations apply to Annex III systems, such as AI used in hiring, credit scoring, or education.
  • August 2, 2028: EU high-risk obligations apply to Annex I systems embedded in regulated products.

Building an evidence trail before enforcement starts is easier than doing it under a deadline. Waiting for enforcement to force the issue tends to compress a process that benefits from more runway.

FAQ

When do EU AI Act high-risk obligations take effect?

December 2, 2027 for Annex III (standalone) high-risk systems and August 2, 2028 for Annex I (product-embedded) systems. The Digital Omnibus on AI set both dates in 2026. The Article 50 transparency obligations have applied since August 2, 2026.

Is the NIST AI RMF legally required?

No, it’s voluntary. But laws increasingly reference it. Texas’s TRAIGA, for example, ties a liability defense to NIST’s Generative AI Profile or a comparable framework.

How is OWASP’s LLM Top 10 different from MITRE ATLAS?

OWASP lists vulnerability classes in LLM applications, such as prompt injection and improper output handling. MITRE ATLAS lists the tactics and techniques attackers use against AI systems. They complement each other.

Is there a US federal AI law?

Not a comprehensive one. Regulation is happening state by state, including Texas’s TRAIGA, Colorado’s SB 26-189 (effective January 1, 2027), and New York City’s Local Law 144. The result is a patchwork, with no single national standard. Try building a policy mapped to these frameworks yourself.

A customer is billed twice for a $340 hotel booking. Their card issuer refuses to reverse the second charge, it goes to a collections agency, and it now shows on their credit report. They file a complaint describing all of this in a few hundred words. A complaint like this needs three things:

  1. A resolution path: close it with an explanation, fix something such as the credit report, or pay the customer back.
  2. A yes or no on whether a person should look at it quickly.
  3. A severity grade.

Most organizations handle that with hand-written rules, a conventional machine learning model, and, as a fallback, a person reading whatever the first two missed. Increasingly there is a fourth option: give the whole complaint to a large language model and ask for JSON back so it can be plugged back into a standard programmatic workflow.

That works, but when you unpack it, it’s an odd way to get a one-word answer. The model writes its answer one token at a time, may reason through hundreds of words first, bills you for all of them, and needs a schema, a validator, and retry code to land in a fixed format (though often this last part is now handled by the model provider). Its confidence is weaker still: if it writes "confidence": 0.91, that is text it chose to write, not a measured probability. Yet plenty of organizations have started building agentic systems for fraud screening, moderation, and routing on top of these numbers.

Last month TypeSafe released Jev, a model that works differently. This post covers what it does, what is known about how it works, and how it compares on real complaints with a small open version we trained using AI orchestration in Anaconda Platform.

A language model writes its answer one piece at a time and returns text; a decision model reads the input once and returns a probability for every answer.

What a Decision Model Is

TypeSafe calls Jev a “System One model,” after Daniel Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow, where System 1 is fast, automatic judgment and System 2 is slow, deliberate thinking. The argument is that the language models people build software on are System 2 tools, while most decisions inside software are System 1: approve or reject, route to team A or team B, pick one of five labels.

The generic name that has stuck is “decision model.” It is a classifier: you give it some input (the “state”) and a set of questions, each with a fixed list of answers, and for each question it returns a probability for every answer. A decision model never writes text, so it cannot summarize a document, write code, or explain its choice. OpenAI has since announced a similar Decisions API, in limited preview, at DevDay on 29 September.

Kahneman’s System 1 is also the part of us that is fast and often wrong. TypeSafe sells Jev on the quality of its probabilities, and the second half of this post tests that on one task.

Why Developers Care

Sebastian Raschka, a longtime builder of text classifiers, went from assuming he could easily build Jev himself to finding it worked better than he expected. He calls it “the ChatGPT moment for classification”: one model that handles many kinds of classification cheaply, without training a classifier per task (Ahead of AI).

Three things make it useful as a building block.

The answer is always one of your options. A language model can return a wrong answer or an invalid one. A decision model only returns options you supplied, so the only thing left to check is whether the answer is right.

The probability is read off the model, not written by it. It comes from the model’s internal state, so it can be checked against outcomes, adjusted, and thresholded, though any model can still be over- or underconfident. One trap: the API’s confidence field is not a second accuracy estimate, but a measure of how far the top answer stands out (three options with a top probability of 0.8 report 0.7). Set thresholds on the probability, and check them on your own data.

It is cheap (and effective). Jev is named after the economist William Stanley Jevons, who observed that making a resource cheaper tends to increase how much of it gets used. It costs $0.042 per million input tokens, and nothing for output. Our complaints averaged about 630 tokens, so each cost around $0.00003, with a median of 88 milliseconds per answer. Raschka ran it over the 25,000 IMDb test reviews for about 65 cents and scored 96.5%, on par with a ModernBERT model he fine-tuned for the task. At that price you can decide on every row, event, or log line.

What We Know About Jev

TypeSafe has not published how Jev is built, and the model cannot be downloaded for separate inspection. The most thorough outside analysis is Archer Hume’s Jev’s Architecture Unmasked (17 September 2026), built on about ten thousand probing API calls. We follow its separation of what TypeSafe says, what can be observed, and what can only be inferred.

What TypeSafe says. All answers are produced at once. Questions can be multiple choice, yes/no, or a score on a scale. The model is an existing language model given extra training with an unpublished method TypeSafe calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), aimed at making the probabilities match reality; its closest public relative is Reinforcement Learning with Calibration Rewards (RLCR), which rewards accurate stated confidence. TypeSafe’s CEO has said all of the training data is synthetic.

What the API shows. Questions in the same request cannot see each other: a secret code hidden in one question is invisible to another (probability 0.00), but found when placed in the shared input (about 0.90). Extra questions cost very little time until there are around a hundred.

The list of options affects the answers. Adding an irrelevant fifth option to a four-option question shifted the balance between two of the original four, consistently across ten trials. That rules out scoring each option independently, where an extra option could not change the balance between two others. Order matters too: reversing it in an ordinary support-ticket question raised the top answer’s probability from around 0.84–0.89 to 0.93–0.96.

What is guessed. Since the model itself is proprietary, there are plenty of details that we can only infer. Hume’s investigation leads him to infer a large, general language model underneath that reuses the shared input across questions; Raschka guesses something small, similar to ModernBERT, which would explain the low latency. The rest of our analysis doesn’t depend on which is right.

Why It Can Answer All the Questions at Once

Two timelines. Both start with the same reading step. The language model then writes its answer one piece at a time; the decision model stops after reading and emits an answer for each question.

When a language model reads a prompt, it processes all the words together in one pass. The slow part is writing the reply, which it can only do one word after another. A decision model stops after reading and takes each question’s answer straight from that pass.

The limit is that a question cannot depend on another’s answer. Dealing with that would need a second round of questions, or one question covering both.

Where the Promise Falls Short

Good probabilities on a benchmark do not mean good probabilities on your data. On a public set of general-knowledge questions Jev’s probabilities look excellent, but most of those questions are easy and answered confidently. On a family of freshly written math problems it was right 56% of the time while giving itself about 35%, which is far too cautious. You have to measure them on examples from your own workflow where you know the answer, as we do below.

Reordering the options moves the probabilities. A probability that moves from 0.84 to 0.95 because the options were listed differently will cross a threshold like 0.9 and change what your system does. On 40 of our complaints, Jev picked the same answer after reordering in 79 of 80 cases, but its probabilities moved by up to 0.20 and crossed 0.9 in 7 of the 80. Some of that is noise from batched GPU computation on TypeSafe’s side: a question whose options we did not touch moved by up to 0.04 between identical requests. Our own model (discussed later) did much worse. Test option order before deploying any of these models.

Left: how often each model picked the same answer after the options were reordered. Jev kept its answer in 79 of 80 cases, our model in about 48 of 80. Right: how far Jev’s top probability moved for each complaint, with the seven cases that crossed 0.9 highlighted.

It cannot reason in steps or look things up. Those still need a language model. The sensible arrangement is TypeSafe’s own suggestion: the decision model decides, and the language model writes.

The Task

To test the capabilities of these models, we decided we’d use what is known about the architecture, combine it with a fine-tune, and apply it to a common business problem. The data we built our model on is the CFPB Consumer Complaint Database of complaints about US financial companies, each with the consumer’s account and the company’s reported response. The CFPB stopped publishing the accounts in August 2026, so we used an earlier copy.

We asked three questions of each complaint:

  • How was it resolved? Closed with an explanation only, with non-monetary relief (a fix such as correcting a credit report), or with monetary relief. The answer is what the company says it did.
  • Does it need quick human review? Yes or no.
  • How severe is it? Low, medium, or high.

Only the resolution has a real recorded answer. Payouts are rare (about 3%), so we oversampled them to about a quarter.

We made up the other two answers with a small set of rules. A complaint needs quick review if its issue category is on a list of serious ones (fraud, legal threats, misuse of credit reports and so on), if the consumer is a service member or older American with a moderately serious issue, or if it mentions at least two words from a list such as “attorney,” “lawsuit,” or “fraud.” Severity adds up points for the same signals.

The complaints are split by date: the oldest only for historical figures such as each company’s payout rate, then training, then a set for adjusting the probabilities, and the most recent 2,400 for testing. Every model reads the first 1,500 characters, which shortens about a quarter of complaints; the rules read the full text.

This is quite unlike IMDb sentiment. “Positive” means what everyone thinks it means; “needs quick review” means whatever our rules say, and “closed with an explanation” depends on how these companies behave. That difference matters more than anything else in the results.

Building Our Own Version

Our model follows the open jaredpalmer/kev implementation closely.

Each complaint and its three questions go into the model as one piece of text: the complaint first, then each question with its options. The trick is controlling what each part may look at. Every question sees the whole complaint, no question sees another, and each question is positioned as if it came straight after the complaint, so all three are treated as though they were asked on their own.

This reproduces both behaviors observed in Jev. Questions cannot read each other. Options within one question are read together, in order: each option is read in the light of the ones listed before it but not after, so adding an option changes how the others are read, and an option listed early is read without seeing its rivals.

At the end, the model compares its internal summary of each question with its summary of each option and turns each comparison into a probability. Because it compares rather than picking from a fixed list of slots, the options can change from one request to the next without retraining, and an option can be a whole phrase.

How the model scores options: it compares its summary of the question with its summary of each option, turns each comparison into a score, and converts the scores into probabilities. The same comparison works unchanged for three options or five.

Raschka sketches the main alternative: feed the model the input, the instructions, and one option at a time, score each, and turn the scores into probabilities. Scored alone, options cannot affect each other, so the design is immune to the order problem, at the cost of reading the complaint once per option. As shown above, this approach is not what Jev does.

We started from Qwen3 0.6B, a small open language model, and trained a small add-on rather than the whole model, which keeps training cheap enough to repeat often.

Running It

The pipeline runs on AI orchestration in Anaconda Platform, which runs on Metaflow. It is three Metaflow flows, for data, training, and evaluation, each starting when the previous one finishes and reading its results directly, so every number traces back to the data and model that produced it. Training runs on a cloud GPU even when started from a laptop. A Panel dashboard serves the model: try complaints, flip one fact to see which answers change, and inspect the probabilities.

The accompanying application allows us to visualize the generated probabilities and how perturbations (for example, adding an escalation sentence such as “I have already contacted an attorney about this matter”) affect the results.

The data preparation, training, and evaluation flows, each triggered when the previous one finishes.

Scaling up and down is a decorator. Training needs a GPU and nothing else does, so only the training step asks for one:

@kubernetes(gpu=1) @step def train(self): ...

Starting the flow from a laptop still sends that step to a cloud GPU, while data preparation and evaluation run on ordinary CPU machines. Going the other way is just as easy. For quick checks, we ran the whole pipeline locally on a small sample, with each step’s software environment supplied by the project’s lock file instead of being built in the cloud. Moving from Qwen3 0.6B to a larger base model changes the resource request on one step and nothing else.

Comparing many models is running them side by side. A flow can fan out into parallel branches, one per configuration, and join the results afterwards. That is how a comparison like ours grows cheaply: each XGBoost variant or Jev prompt is another branch, and adding one does not slow down the others.

Every result can be pulled up later. Anything a step stores, such as a trained adapter, a data split, a metric, or a reliability table, is saved and versioned as an artifact on S3 with the run that produced it. From a notebook, getting it back takes a few lines:

from metaflow import Flow run = Flow("ComplaintTriageCalibrationFlow").latest_successful_run run.data.metrics

The charts in this post were drawn from those stored results, and each flow also publishes a small report page that shows its numbers next to the run. When a result looks wrong, it can be traced back to the exact data and model version that produced it.

A language model can run the experiments. Because every experiment is code and every result can be queried, a coding agent can do much of the loop itself: change a flow, launch a run, read the stored results, compare them with the last run, and propose the next change. Results

We compared our model with XGBoost, a standard and fast machine learning method, given either the structured fields of each complaint or those fields plus word counts from its text, and with Jev, both zero-shot and with prompts that describe our rules. Besides the complaints themselves, our model trained on 2,666 “minimal pairs”: copies of complaints with one decisive fact changed, such as adding “I have already contacted an attorney about this matter.” Because “attorney” is on the rule’s word list, these pairs effectively taught our model the rules, and no comparison model saw them. All results are on 2,400 held-out complaints. The full method, every score, and the calibration analysis are in the technical report.

Accuracy per question for five models, with a dashed line where always guessing the most common answer would land. Our model leads on quick review and severity; XGBoost with word counts leads on the resolution question.

Asked cold, Jev does worse than always guessing the most common answer on all three questions. Nothing in the prompt says that half these complaints close with an explanation only, or where our rules draw the line. Jev says 77% deserve non-monetary relief, 74% need quick review, and 45% are high severity. This is the same model that matched a fine-tuned classifier on IMDb. Jev does well when the labels mean what most people would assume, and poorly when they depend on your own rules or on how your own organization has behaved in the past.

Shifting Jev’s answers toward this data’s base rates lifts resolution accuracy from 45% to 55%, so its reading does carry information about outcomes. On the made-up questions it carries almost none: after the same correction, Jev says no complaint needs quick review.

Writing out the rules gets Jev to 82% on quick review and 54% on severity, both below the XGBoost model that never reads the complaint. The rules are word matches and list lookups, which XGBoost learns almost perfectly and Jev has to follow from a paragraph of instructions. On resolution, Jev’s best is 55%, below every model that reads the text.

Jev’s probabilities on resolution are honest: after adjustment, they match how often it is right. Ours are not at the top end: of 237 answers given at about 95% confidence, only 47% were right. Raschka found that adding a Brier penalty to training barely improved calibration for his ModernBERT classifier, so that is probably not the fix here.

Our model’s lead on the made-up questions is smaller than it looks. Applying the rules to the issue category and tags alone, without reading the complaint, already gets 94% of quick-review and 89% of severity answers right, and on severity our model scores below that. Since the minimal pairs taught our model the rules, and rules rather than people produced the answers, these results show how well each model recovers our rules. On resolution, the one real outcome, XGBoost with word counts beats our model on both accuracy and probability quality.

Our model on a laptop GPU is about as fast as Jev’s servers, and most of Jev’s time is network; under load, one Jev request in twenty took about 7 seconds. The word-count model is one to two orders of magnitude faster than either and needs no GPU.

Making It Yours

The three questions, the labeling rules, and the minimal pairs were written for this demo. To use it on your own data, replace them with your own questions and your own definitions, ideally with labels written by people. A larger starting model (Qwen3 also comes in 1.7, 4, and 8 billion parameters) trades speed for accuracy, and the data can be your own tickets instead of Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) complaints.

What We Think This Changes

Until now, many teams have reached for a general-purpose large language model even for classification, with prompts and extra code to cover the gaps. A decision model handles classification as a separate step: it takes structured input, returns a fixed set of answers with probabilities, and is fast and cheap enough to run on every item.

Jev appears to be built from known techniques. Deploying it still takes more care than the launch suggested, because the main risks, such as sensitivity to how the options are worded and ordered, do not show up in an accuracy score.

Raschka concludes that Jev raises the bar for justifying a fine-tuned model, and for labels that mean what everyone assumes, we agree. When the labels depend on your own rules and history, it does not hold: here a small model trained on the questions beat the general one on all three, and a word-count model beat both on the real outcome while running many times faster. Jev did several things well: its probabilities could be trusted after one adjustment, its answers barely moved when options were reordered, and each decision cost a few thousandths of a cent. But a general decision model has to be told your base rates and definitions before it can match your labels, and a prompt can only supply part of that. Whether a larger model trained on your own data, or labels written by people, closes the gap is what the remaining experiments are for.

Two results in this post were easy to misread: our model’s best scores came from labels it had been taught, and a word-count model won on the only real outcome. We caught both because every number here is stored with the run, data split, and model version that produced it. For a decision model whose probabilities shift with option order, that record is how a team finds the problem before the model reaches production.

Embracing Playful Imperfection With The CSS `random()` Function

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In design, we love spending hours obsessing over consistency, grids, and alignment. But when I heard that the native CSS random() function landed in Safari and is rolling out in Chrome 156, I started wondering: where does randomness actually make sense on the web? Is it something we want in our design system? To find out, I did what I always do in doubt: I built a bunch of playful CodePen demos exploring organic typography, bouncing letters, splayed cards, and a punk fanzine.

As someone who loves building design systems and tinkering with CSS architecture, a big part of my day job revolves around control. We set up spacing scales, lock down typography, set up color tokens, and make sure everything aligns on a grid. Consistency is usually our bread and butter as design engineers and a proud badge of skill when creating design systems. If a card suddenly jumps 20 pixels to the left on a refresh, it’s not creative expression; it’s a bug that the project manager will log.

So when I first heard that the native random() function from the CSS Values and Units Level 5 spec was shipped in Safari, and is currently rolling out in Chrome 156 (with positive signals from Mozilla on Firefox as well), my first reaction was: that’s pretty cool!

And my second reaction was: wait... where would I actually use this without making a mess? How can I convince designers that random might be a good idea for a design system?

Because let’s be honest: why on earth would we want randomness in our stylesheets when we’ve spent the last decade trying to make the web more predictable? Even when using flexbox or fluid typography, we’re still trying to make things as aligned and uniform as possible.

The Charm Of Things Not Being 100% Straight

If you step away from the browser for a second and look at physical things around you, nothing is ever truly uniform.

Take an old letterpress print: the ink bleeds slightly into the paper grain, some letters have a microscopic tilt, and the baseline isn’t always that straight. Or think about a deck of Polaroid photos dropped on a coffee table; those photos aren’t perfect, probably not straight either. Or what about lights flickering in a street at night? That little bit of analog messiness is what makes things feel alive, organic, and human.

On the web, we’ve gotten so good at pixel perfection that sometimes our interfaces end up feeling a bit sterile. We notice this even more now with rapidly created designs by an unguided LLM; we usually end up with the same sort of design we had 10 years ago. Everything is squared off, aligned, neat. It works, but frankly, it does get a bit boring.

Randomness in CSS doesn’t have to mean throwing your design system out the window. It’s about little imperfections and playfulness. Maybe let’s even call it fun.

To see what this actually looks like in practice, I spent a few hours tinkering in CodePen. The demos might feel a bit isolated, but I’m sure that many designers could make the best of this controlled chaos. But before we look at those demos, let’s take a look at the syntax.

How The CSS random() Function Works

The random() function is specified in CSS Values and Units Module Level 5. It generates a pseudo-random value inside your CSS without needing JavaScript or a build-time Sass loop.

The formal specification looks like this:

random( <random-key>?, <min>, <max>, <step>? )

In practice, and across current browser implementations, you can just write comma-separated arguments:

/* Random angle between -3deg and 3deg */ rotate: random(-3deg, 3deg); /* Random width between 20px and 80px in steps of 10px */ inline-size: random(20px, 80px, 10px); /* Random animation duration */ animation-duration: random(1.5s, 3s, 0.2s);

Matching Units

It won’t be a shocker for most people, but I thought I should at least mention that your minimum and maximum bounds must share the exact same unit type.

The CSS parser needs to know what dimension it’s dealing with. You can use lengths, angles, time, percentages, or plain unitless numbers:

/* Good: both are angles */ rotate: random(-4deg, 4deg); /* Good: both are unitless numbers */ opacity: random(0.3, 1); /* Nope! You cannot mix units */ inline-size: random(2rem, 100px); /* Invalid */

If you ever need to combine units, you’ll have to reach for calc() inside the arguments so each side resolves properly.

Stepping And Rounding

The optional third argument is the step size. If you don’t add it, the browser picks from a continuous range. But if you provide a step, it rounds the random value to discrete increments:

/* Only produces 0px, 15px, 30px, 45px, or 60px */ top: random(0px, 60px, 15px);

This can be helpful in cases where you want things to jump around a bit, but you still want them to land on your pre-defined grid.

Caching Keys: Keeping Things In Sync

Normally, every time random() is evaluated on a property, it creates an independent random value. But there could be an example where you create a box that sets inline-size and block-size at random, but at the same time you need them to match so the element stays a perfect square.

That’s where the caching key comes in:

.card-thumbnail { /* Both properties share the key --square-size */ inline-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 60px, 120px); block-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 60px, 120px); }

In the latest spec, a bare dashed ident like --square-size shares its seed globally across every element in the document (which would make all cards identical). To keep width and height linked on a single element while giving each card its own independent size, the spec requires pairing it with the element-scoped keyword (e.g. --square-size element-scoped).

To see this in action, I created a little example. In the top row, the tiles have uncached dimensions, which distorts each tile into an unpredictable, mismatched rectangle. In the bottom row, both inline-size and block-size share --square-size, guaranteeing that each tile remains a pristine 1:1 square. Refresh the CodePen a couple of times and notice the bottom cards keeping the same randomness:

See the Pen Uncached vs cached random() [forked] by @utilitybend.

/* Uncached: width and height resolve independently, creating random rectangles */ .tile-uncached { inline-size: random(4.2rem, 8.5rem); block-size: random(4.2rem, 8.5rem); rotate: random(-3.5deg, 3.5deg); } /* Cached: shared key guarantees a clean 1:1 square */ .tile-cached { inline-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 4.2rem, 8.5rem); block-size: random(--square-size element-scoped, 4.2rem, 8.5rem); rotate: random(--tilt element-scoped, -3.5deg, 3.5deg); } Browser Support And Progressive Enhancement

Where can you test this today?

  • WebKit / Safari: Safari was the first to land support.
  • Chrome / Edge: Shipped and rolling out right now with Chrome 156.
  • Firefox: Positive signals on the standards track, so hopefully won’t be too far behind.

Because we’re still in the rollout phase, you’ll want to wrap your experiments in @supports:

/* Baseline for all browsers */ .badge { rotate: 0deg; } /* Enhanced for browsers with random() */ @supports (rotate: random(-2deg, 2deg)) { .badge { rotate: random(-2.5deg, 2.5deg); } }

The Future random-item Function

There’s also another function in the spec called random-item(). While random() handles numerical ranges, random-item() lets you pick from a list of predefined tokens or keywords:

/* Picking a random color from your palette */ background-color: random-item(--seed, oklch(75% 0.18 150), oklch(65% 0.22 350), oklch(85% 0.16 85));

This can be handy to randomise from a set of design tokens. This one does not have any browser support at the moment, but it certainly is something to look forward to.

And now for the practical part.

Demo 1: Let’s Just Randomize Letters

When I started playing with the feature, I was also doing some typography tricks with animations. So that’s where my first thought went: Typography! What if we just add a tiny bit of imperfection to a word?

Think of vintage book covers. The letters aren’t misaligned by miles, but they have small imperfections.

Please Don’t Break The Screen Reader

One quick accessibility warning here: If you break a word into separate <span> elements so you can style each letter, screen readers will often read it out as individual characters: “i - m - p - e - r - f - e - c - t - i - o - n”, or pause awkwardly between each one. That’s a terrible user experience.

Always hide the visual spans from assistive tech and provide the full word:

Screen reader users get the clean text, and sighted users get the fun visual quirks.

Demo 2: Animating The Letters: Pure Procedural Bounce

But if every letter gets its own jump height, its own duration, and its own landing squash, suddenly the word looks like a bunch of little characters jumping around on a trampoline.

See the Pen Bouncy imperfection with CSS random() [forked] by @utilitybend.

Here is how the bounce parameters are wired up:

@layer demo { .char { display: inline-block; transform-origin: center bottom; /* Procedural bounce variables */ --jump: random(element-scoped, -35px, -55px, 2px); --tilt: random(element-scoped, -8deg, 8deg, 0.5deg); --dur: random(element-scoped, 1s, 1.2s, 0.1s); --delay: random(element-scoped, -3s, 0s, 0.1s); --rebound-squash: random(element-scoped, 1.08, 1.18, 0.02); animation: letter-bounce var(--dur) linear var(--delay) infinite; } @keyframes letter-bounce { /* apex */ 0% { translate: 0 var(--jump); rotate: var(--tilt); scale: 1 1; animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.65, 0.05, 0.85, 0.35); } /* touchdown */ 44% { translate: 0 0; rotate: calc(var(--tilt) * 0.2); scale: 0.96 1.05; animation-timing-function: ease-out; } /* squash */ 52% { translate: 0 0; rotate: 0deg; scale: var(--rebound-squash) calc(2 - var(--rebound-squash)); animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1); } /* back up */ 62% { translate: 0 calc(var(--jump) * 0.2); rotate: calc(var(--tilt) * -0.4); scale: 0.98 1.03; animation-timing-function: cubic-bezier(0.15, 0.85, 0.35, 1); } 100% { translate: 0 var(--jump); rotate: var(--tilt); scale: 1 1; } } }

Notice the negative animation delay (--delay: random(-3s, 0s)).

If you set a positive delay, the letters sit frozen on page load, waiting for their timers to expire before jumping. By using a negative delay, the browser pretends the animation has already been running for a few seconds. The moment the page loads, every letter is already bouncing at its own rhythm.

Demo 4: The Splayed Deck With sibling-index And scroll

Here is where modern CSS features start getting really interesting. In the previous demo with tilting cards, we used pure randomness. But what if you have a stack of cards that needs to fan out across the screen as you scroll?

In this example, I decided to use math for the structure and randomness for the human imperfection.

See the Pen Scroll drivin animations with random() tilted cards [forked] by @utilitybend.

Here is the setup:

@layer demo { .deck-card { position: absolute; /* Mathematical fan base via sibling-index() (centered around middle card) */ --elevation: sibling-index(); --base-rot: calc((sibling-index() - (sibling-count() + 1) / 2) * 12deg); --base-x: calc((sibling-index() - (sibling-count() + 1) / 2) * 60px); --jitter-rot: random(-3.5deg, 3.5deg); --jitter-y: random(-12px, 12px); --jitter-x: random(-8px, 8px); transform-origin: center 130%; /* SDA */ animation: splay-fan linear both; animation-timeline: scroll(root); animation-range: 0% 80%; transition: scale 0.3s cubic-bezier(0.2, 0.8, 0.2, 1); &:hover { scale: 1.05; /* Hovered item on top */ z-index: calc(sibling-count() + 1); } } @keyframes splay-fan { from { rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 0; box-shadow: 0 1px 3px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.1), 0 2px 8px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.07); } to { rotate: calc(var(--base-rot) + var(--jitter-rot)); translate: calc(var(--base-x) + var(--jitter-x)) var(--jitter-y); /* Splayed: 1. Sharp inter-card contact edge shadow 2. Elevated atmospheric shadow scaled realistically by sibling-index() */ box-shadow: 0 calc(1px + var(--elevation) * 0.6px) 4px oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.1), calc(var(--base-x) * 0.15) calc(10px + var(--elevation) * 5px) calc(18px + var(--elevation) * 7px) oklch(20% 0.02 260 / 0.13); } } }

Look at how cleanly this works:

  • sibling-index() calculates the mathematical fan so cards spread out evenly around the center card, and scales --elevation so cards higher in the stack cast wider shadows.
  • calc((sibling-index() - (sibling-count() + 1) / 2) * 12deg); makes sure the amount of cards doesn’t matter; they each will rotate the same 12deg amount.
  • random() adds the slight wobble you'd get if a person dealt the cards by hand.
  • animation-timeline: scroll(root) ties the entire fan and shadow transition to the scrollbar.
Demo 5: Random Velocity

If you’ve ever looked at brutalist or high-end fashion websites, you’ve probably seen horizontal marquee text drifting across the screen as you scroll down the page.

Usually, you see developers creating classes like .drift-slow, .drift-fast, .drift-reverse, or using a hefty JavaScript scroll listener to compute transforms.

With random() and a tiny bit of math, we can get procedural drift speeds in just a few lines:

Here is the CSS:

@layer demo { .drift-line { white-space: nowrap; /* Alternate direction between rows using math parity */ --direction: calc(pow(-1, sibling-index())); /* Velocity distance */ --travel: random(15%, 44%); /* Tied to page scroll */ animation: tectonic-glide linear both; animation-timeline: scroll(root); animation-range: entry 0% cover 100%; } @keyframes tectonic-glide { from { translate: calc(var(--direction) * -1 * var(--travel)) 0; } to { translate: calc(var(--direction) * var(--travel)) 0; } } }

calc(pow(-1, sibling-index())) gives us alternating directions (1, -1, 1, -1), and random(15%, 44%) decides how fast each row travels. As you scroll, each typographic line moves at its own tempo.

Where Randomness Makes Sense (And Where It Doesn’t)

Now that we’ve played with the wild side, let’s talk about where this actually belongs in our daily work.

Good Use Cases

  • Subtle micro-imperfection on decorative UI: Tilting polaroids, slight angles on sticky notes, or typographic hero titles.
  • Desynchronizing ambient loops: Status beacons, gentle floating badges, or background particle grids where perfect unison looks robotic.
  • Generative backgrounds: Star fields, confetti celebrations, floating bokeh circles, or texture grain.
  • Editorial art direction: Magazine spreads, event flyers, brutalist layouts, and playful landing pages.
  • Layering on top of math: Using sibling-index() for the structural grid and adding small random offsets for human warmth.

Where You Should Avoid It

  • Core layouts and grids: Don’t randomize column gutters, form field widths, or container padding. People need predictable layout geometry to navigate.
  • Body copy: Don’t touch font sizes, line heights, or letter spacing on long-form paragraphs. Reading fatigue is real.
  • Interactive targets: Don’t leave small buttons or links tilted or shifted without resetting them on hover and focus. If a user has to chase a moving or crooked button, you’ve failed them.

But! I am happy to be proven wrong here. I encourage you to challenge the status quo and experiment with this.

Always Respect prefers-reduced-motion

Any time you use animations, you have to respect user preferences for reduced motion:

@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) { .char { animation: none; rotate: 0deg; translate: 0 0; } }

If someone prefers reduced motion, it could also be a good idea to flatten the angles, not just the animations. I can imagine that a lot of random angles can also upset people while scrolling. Design should be fun, but never at the cost of accessibility.

Wrapping My Head Around random()

Tinkering with random() over the last couple of days was a lot of fun. I’m still not sure if I’ll be able to convince my designer friends, but what I do know is that it brings a bit of joy.

Whenever we wanted a bit of organic randomness on the web, we had to make compromises: either pull in a JavaScript animation library, or write huge preprocessor loops that spit out hundreds of lines of static CSS. Having this built into the browser engine with zero JavaScript overhead is such a welcome addition.

We spent the last decade mastering consistency, tokens, and design systems. Now that we have that foundation in place, maybe it’s time to let a little bit of playful messiness back onto the web.

I really believe that the web is supposed to be fun.

Your developers love their AI coding tools. They’ve built workflows around them, learned which models work best for different tasks, and made AI-assisted development part of their daily routine.

The agent has finished the bulk of your change. Now you want to inspect one implementation detail, adjust a few lines by hand, and run the project locally before you ship it.

But the work is sitting in the GitHub Copilot app… and your editor is where you review diffs, debug code, and make final adjustments. Do you have to choose between staying in the session and starting over in Visual Studio Code?

You don’t. You can open the Copilot app session directly in VS Code and continue with its conversation, code changes, and working context intact. That means Copilot can handle the repetitive or time-consuming work, then hand control back to you for closer review, hands-on editing, and local testing.

In this tutorial, you’ll learn how to:

  • Open a Copilot app session in VS Code.
  • Review the agent’s changes line by line.
  • Continue the same conversation from your editor.
  • Modify and test the code locally.
  • Push your updates to the pull request associated with the session.

Prerequisites 

Before you begin, make sure you have: 

  • The GitHub Copilot app installed and signed in. 
  • Visual Studio Code installed and connected to the same GitHub account. 
  • A Copilot app session with code changes you want to review or continue. 
  • Permission to work in and push changes to the session’s repository. 

This tutorial assumes you are already comfortable navigating VS Code, running commands in its integrated terminal, and working with Git branches and pull requests.

1. Start with a session in the Copilot app

Open the session you want to continue. Before handing it off, take a moment to review the task and the work Copilot completed.

A session contains more than a collection of modified files. It also records the task, the plan, and the conversation that produced those changes. That context is important when you want to understand why Copilot chose a particular implementation or ask it to revise part of the work.

You do not need to summarize that history or copy it into a new chat before moving to VS Code.

2. Open the session in VS Code

From the session, select Open in VS Code. If the app presents more than one supported editor or VS Code installation, choose the one you want to use.

VS Code opens the project using the worktree associated with the session. It also displays the existing session in the Copilot sidebar.

That gives you three connected pieces of the original work:

  • The session context: The task, plan, and conversation remain available.
  • The diff: The files and lines changed by the agent are ready for review.
  • The worktree: VS Code opens the same isolated working tree the session used.

You are continuing the existing session, not reconstructing it in a new editor window.

3. Review the agent’s changes

Before editing, inspect the diff in VS Code. Open the Source Control view to see the changed files, then select a file to review its changes line by line.

Review the implementation as you would any other proposed change. In particular, check that:

  • The code solves the requested problem.
  • The implementation follows the repository’s conventions.
  • Error states and edge cases are handled appropriately.
  • Tests cover the behavior that changed.
  • No unrelated files or behaviors were modified.

The preserved conversation can help explain how Copilot arrived at the implementation. If something is unclear, continue prompting Copilot in the sidebar. For example:

Explain why you changed this function instead of adding a new helper. Identify any behavior this could affect.

You can also ask Copilot to make another change. Because the session and worktree remain connected, you do not have to describe the task from the beginning.

4. Make a hands-on edit

Once you understand the proposed changes, edit any file directly in VS Code.

This is useful for adjustments that are faster or easier to make by hand, such as:

  • Refining a label or error message.
  • Renaming a variable for clarity.
  • Adjusting layout or styling.
  • Adding a missing test case.
  • Applying a repository-specific convention.

You are editing the session’s existing worktree, so your manual changes build directly on the agent’s work. You do not need to copy files between directories or manually re-create the branch.

5. Run and verify the project locally

Use the VS Code integrated terminal to run the project’s normal validation workflow. The exact commands depend on the repository, but some web app examples may include:

  • npm install
  • npm test
  • npm run lint
  • npm run dev

Treat agent-generated changes the same way you would treat code written by a person: run the relevant tests, inspect failures, and verify the behavior yourself.

If the development server prints a localhost URL, open it to test the change in a browser. When supported by your VS Code setup, you can open the URL in VS Code’s integrated browser and inspect the result without leaving the editor.

For a user-interface change, verify both the requested behavior and nearby states. For example, check the modified component at different viewport sizes and confirm that loading, empty, and error states still work.

6. Continue with Copilot or keep editing yourself

At any point, you can choose the interaction that best fits the next step:

  • Prompt Copilot when the change requires repository-wide analysis or repetitive edits.
  • Edit directly when you know the exact change you want.
  • Use both approaches iteratively as you test and refine the implementation.

For example, after making a manual UI adjustment, you might ask Copilot:

Add a test for the updated label and run the relevant test file. Do not modify unrelated components.

Because you are still in the original session, Copilot retains the context needed to interpret “the updated label.”

7. Commit and push the finished changes

When the implementation is ready, review the final diff one more time. Then commit and push the changes using the Source Control view or the integrated terminal:

  • git status
  • git add
  • git commit -m "Refine session changes"
  • git push

Use your repository’s normal branch, commit, review, and approval practices. Because VS Code opened the worktree and branch associated with the session, pushing the branch updates the pull request already tracking that work.

There is no need to copy the finished files into another checkout or reconnect the work to the pull request manually.

Opening a Copilot app session in VS Code gives you a clean division of labor without splitting the work itself. Copilot can take on a larger or repetitive implementation, while you can move into the editor for detailed review, targeted changes, local testing, and final polish.
