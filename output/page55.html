YOSHIKI Reflects on Music, Life, Legacy & His New Collaboration ‘Forever Love (Live)’ With Josh Groban: Interview

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

YOSHIKI and Josh Groban‘s collaborative recording of “Forever Love (Live)” was released for streaming worldwide on Aug. 28. The ballad, originally released in 1996 as X JAPAN’s 14th single, gets a new rendition, one born from a live performance which the two artists gave together at YOSHIKI CLASSICAL 2026 IN LOS ANGELES AT WALT DISNEY CONCERT HALL “VIOLET NIGHT” on July 17, 2026. 

Billboard JAPAN sat down with YOSHIKI to talk about how the collaboration came together, the raw emotions behind the original song, his ongoing commitment to philanthropy and what’s next.

Could you explain what led to Josh Groban singing “Forever Love” with you, and then to releasing the song?

I first met Josh at an event during the Sundance Film Festival in Park City in 2016, when the documentary “WE ARE X” premiered there. I’ve spent over half my life living in LA, so when I put on YOSHIKI CLASSICAL 2026 IN LOS ANGELES, I thought it would be good for the show to include friends that I had a personal connection with. This year is the 30th anniversary of the release of “Forever Love,” and I had this intuitive feeling that if Josh sang it, it would turn out amazingly. I added English lyrics to the verse, making kind of a “bilingual version.” I wanted to create a perfect balance for listeners everywhere, easy to understand for both English listeners and Japanese listeners.

When Josh came to Japan and performed at Bunkamura Orchard Hall earlier this year, on February 21, you appeared as a special guest, right?

Right. I just happened to be in Japan myself at the time, and the decision for me to perform was last minute, the day before the show. We played “ENDLESS RAIN,” and Josh sang some of the lyrics in Japanese. That’s when I realized that he was good at singing in Japanese, too.

In the new release, the listener can feel the power of his singing voice and your delicate piano playing like they’re right there at the show. Was there anything you paid special attention to in the concert to create that kind of dynamic sound for the listener?

First off, I freely change up the key of my music to match different vocalists. “Forever Love” was originally written for X JAPAN, so it was in a very high key. I lowered it by a seventh for Josh. I listened to dozens of his songs to find the right range, and I thought that this would be around the right range for him, so I suggested it to him, and he sang it in that range.

What kind of emotions were you feeling when you first wrote the song, three decades ago back in 1996?

I’d been living a really chaotic life. Honestly, at the time, I didn’t expect I’d be living for very long. I had a very pessimistic view on life, partly because of the fact that my father took his own life. I figured that one day I’d just vanish somewhere. But, my music might just live on forever. That’s why I named the song “Forever Love.” I remember, when writing the song, thinking that while everyone will eventually disappear from this Earth, perhaps love was eternal. I never imagined that I’d still be here, alive, thirty years later.

It seems clear that you poured a lot of emotion into the song, because it’s still touching people’s hearts even now, three decades later.

I find the whole thing profoundly moving. On the one hand, I’ll wonder to myself, “How have I managed to stay alive so long?” But I feel like I’ve lived because of the support everyone has given me. I feel so thankful. I think that’s why the song could cross the language barriers and national borders through this performance with Josh.

In addition to your music, you’ve been heavily involved in philanthropic activities. You’ve donated generously and you’ve taken part in support activities to help people around the world. What are your feelings on the world today? Is there anything you’d like people to do?

I think this connects to what I was talking about just now, wondering why I’m still alive, but when I think “Why am I still here? Why am I alive?” I’ll often slip into thinking “Should I even be alive?” This applies to creative work, too. But then I’ll think “Maybe this song will help someone out there,” and it really motivates me to create. I think that’s why I’ll have these runs of releases. I write a lot of music, and the same kind of concept applies (to my philanthropy). I feel like if what I do through my charity work helps someone, then my life has meaning. That, in turn, saves me. When I think that my existence helps others, then I feel like it’s okay for me to live. Doing charity work for others saves me, in turn.

In a video interview before the concert, you said that “life was a series of challenges.” So, are you constantly taking on challenges in order to live, and in order to keep making your music?

I think my fans and my friends have given me a second or a third lease on life. I feel like if you’re going to live anyway, you’ve got no choice but to really live your life. For me, that covers a lot, but if I can, I want to live my life giving it full gas and really take all that life has to offer. I think the very fact that we’re granted a life means that we’ve got a mission to accomplish. I’m not sure if life consists of finding that mission, but I want people to know that it is for everyone too, not just me. If you’re going to live, you have no choice but to live your life to the fullest. I think that philosophy is part of “Forever Love,” too.

We’re all looking forward to what you do next.

I’ve been writing music for years, and even now, I’m writing at breakneck speeds. I’ve collaborated with all kinds of artists over the past few years, crossing over genres three or four times, including pop and rock. If I try to release something to represent a culmination of all of that, I’ll never end out putting it out. Instead, the approach I’m trying to take is suddenly putting out a piece of music, like I did this time. And eventually making all of those individual releases come together. For example, I’ve been an artist for decades, and I still find the concept of an album really important. But I’ve trapped myself by that idea, and for this reason or the other I haven’t been able to put out an album. It’s been like that for a few years, and it’s very frustrating. So I’ve started feeling, quite strongly, that I need to free myself from this fixation on the concept of albums, and instead just keep putting out individual songs. I’m writing all these new songs, but then I’m also putting out a song that I wrote 30 years ago. But I think that’s totally fine, too. For me, “Forever Love (Live)” feels like the first breakthrough in a process of freeing myself from these preconceptions and fixations.

—This interview by Takayuki Okamoto first appeared on Billboard Japan

Kroi Talks New Song ‘SPIN’ & the Creative Journey Intersecting With the Chaos of ‘JoJo’

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Kroi’s new song “SPIN” was written as the opener for the anime Steel Ball Run: JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure. A rousing melody and beat that evoke the American Old West, where the story is set, blend with Kroi’s signature chaotic funkiness, resulting in a highly original and exhilarating track. The song took off on streaming right after release, including the U.S. and debuted at No. 44 on Billboard’s Hot Rock & Alternative Songs chart (dated Oct. 10, 2026), the band’s first-ever entry on a Billboard U.S. chart.

Talking with the band revealed the relentless spirit of inquiry and creative urge behind it — an attempt to depict a new world no one has ever seen by digging into cultures closely tied to the band, from the anime song, a uniquely Japanese form of pop, to The Ventures, part of vocalist Leo Uchida’s roots. Drummer Hidetomo Masuda was unfortunately absent due to illness, but the remaining four members shared the story behind the making of “SPIN.”

Your new song “SPIN” is the opening theme for the anime Steel Ball Run: JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure. What does Steel Ball Run, and by extension the JoJo’s Bizarre Adventure series, mean to the members of Kroi?

Yuki Hasebe (Gt.): Of all the members, I’m the one who especially loves JoJo, and I’ve read quite a lot of it. Steel Ball Run was my favorite of them all. I’ve always been drawn to the Old West aesthetic, so pairing that with JoJo is, for me, a collaboration between two things I love. When we got the offer to do the opening theme this time, I was really happy and surprised.

Uchida: I hadn’t experienced JoJo yet, but it’s a series that people around me are deeply passionate about, so I’d been wanting to check it out someday. This was a good opportunity in that sense, too.

What did you feel after experiencing the world of JoJo?

Uchida: It really is amazing. It pulls you in. The works I love make me feel that there’s an atmosphere and a sensation you can’t experience unless you come back to them. JoJo is exactly that. There’s a feeling you can’t get unless you come back here. I was moved by how incredible that world-building is.

“SPIN” has elements reminiscent of Ennio Morricone’s film scores along with Kroi’s signature funkiness, making it a strikingly original song. What concept were you working from?

Uchida: The concept was something like, having no choice but to keep moving through a wilderness where you can’t see what’s ahead, still believing there’s a goal. There’s a goal in your heart that you want to move toward, but don’t know if it’s really the right direction. Still, you have no choice but to keep going. I think that feeling exists in Steel Ball Run, and it exists in the journey of making things, too.

You spoke of “a goal in your heart,” and the line “The frontier that still remains is within” really does give the sense of locating the goal you should aim for inside yourself.

Uchida: Right. When I write lyrics, for example, I can’t write with things brought in from the outside. I can only write with what’s inside me. So in the end, all I can do is dive down into myself.

Images of “wilderness” and “sand” have appeared in Kroi’s songs before, haven’t they?

Uchida: Yeah, it’s strange. We made a song called “Funky GUNSLINGER” a few years ago, and when we were studying up on things like that, thinking, “Let’s try writing a song with sandy terrain as its field” with no tie-in involved, we got an offer from a work with an Arabian flavor, then an offer from a work set in the desert, and this time an offer from a work set in the Old West. It feels like something is descending on us.

As a result, “SPIN” ended up with an extremely original sound, even within today’s Japanese music scene.

Uchida: There’s no one like this in Japan’s major-label music scene right now, with this kind of Morricone-esque feel. I also had an image of The Ventures in mind. The first band I was ever in was a Ventures cover band that an older guy I knew made me join when I was a kid. [Laughs] I played drums in it, so The Ventures are part of my foundation. A lot of people may associate The Ventures with surf music, but ever since I was a kid, I’d listen to songs like “Apache” and think, “This sounds like a Western.” I wanted Hasebe to take on a song with that kind of feel, and besides, a lot of people who play instruments in Japan have The Ventures in their foundation. The first thing I ever played on guitar was “Pipeline,” too. But there isn’t much music now that puts that front and center. So I also wanted to deliver that kind of thing to everyone once again.

Hasebe, Seki and Chiba, how did you approach the making of “SPIN”?

Hasebe: Within the ensemble, the guitar was in charge of evoking a deserted landscape, so I tried putting in a sound like a horse’s whinny and quoting memorable phrases from Morricone. There was a lot of room to mix in things I love, and it was a lot of fun.

You’ve sometimes performed live in Western-inspired outfits, and you’re quite fond of this kind of Western flavor, aren’t you?

Hasebe: Yeah. It’s a bit like Leo’s The Ventures anecdote just now, but sometimes you realize later on what you loved when you were little. When we made “Funky GUNSLINGER,” which came up a moment ago, I watched Western flicks to study them, but long before that, back in junior high, I loved Stevie Ray Vaughan. So it’s always been a good fit for me.

Seki, how about the bass?

Masanori Seki (Ba.): This time Yuki took on the role of expressing the Western feel and the world of Steel Ball Run, so I decided to keep a hi-fi quality in the sound and carry the power and momentum of an anime opener. Within that, though, I tried adding nuances like the sound of a horse’s hooves, so there were also parts where I was conscious of that sense of getting it right.

Chiba, what about the keyboards and the mix?

Daiki Chiba (Key.): What I prioritized on the keyboards was momentum. I remember it being hard to figure out the rhythmic phrasing, what kind of note placement would fit the beat and create momentum. As for the mix, the version that plays in the anime is my mix, but the one being released was mixed by someone else. We asked Nathan Boddy to do it. Our label boss suggested, “Do you want to try asking someone else to mix it?”

I see. Listening to “SPIN,” there’s a sense of scale, like a vast landscape opening up, and at the same time a claustrophobic chaos, like peering into an inner world. That chaotic feel really leaves an impression.

Uchida: This time I also wanted to face the peculiarity of the anime song, something that’s been built up over time within Japanese culture. Layers stacked on top of layers like this, fast-moving changes, and on top of that, it’s a vocal song. I don’t think you find music like this much outside Japan. I think it became the most chaotic song we’ve made recently, one that also carries that essence of the Japanese anime song. There were a lot of tracks, too. We brought back the horns at the very end, like, “Let’s put them in after all,” after cutting them at the demo stage, and the arpeggiator Masuda added is a nice touch as well. I think having that mechanical sound conveys that this song isn’t simply a recreation of a Western, but a story of a new world based on one.

“Not a recreation of a Western, but a story of a new world” is exactly what comes across when listening.

Uchida: We crammed in all kinds of things, each one of them has meaning, and the sum of them became a song that connects Kroi and Steel Ball Run. That’s how it feels. Part of what I came to love about JoJo is that “chaos.” The premise is fantasy, but because that chaos is there, people become entangled with one another, the story twists and winds, and the work as a whole becomes realistic. That strikes me as being very much like life itself. I hope we were able to express that in this song, too.

—This interview by Fumiaki Amano first appeared on Billboard Japan

The Amazing Digital Circus made a surprise cameo in a recent episode of Law & Order Toronto: Criminal Intent. Unfortunately, the writers behind this iteration of the long-running police-procedural drama don’t seem to think much of the hit indie animated series.

In the episode, titled “The Business of Boys,” the Toronto Major Crimes Unit investigates a video depicting the gruesome murder of a local school teacher. As the detectives interrogate the student they believe filmed the video, they note his interest in cosplaying The Amazing Digital Circus‘ resident rascally rabbit, Jax, and suggest he wore the costume to hide his face during the crime.

In fairness, the detectives’ conclusion rests on sound evidence (the kid accidentally recorded a reflection of his mask while filming the video), but the scene frames the boy’s interest in The Amazing Digital Circus as all the proof they need of his murderous potential. As the detective swipes through the many in-costume selfies the student took, his voice and body language suggest he sees the kid’s interest in the animated series as a big red flag all by itself.

As wild as it is to find out that The Amazing Digital Circus is canon in the Law & Order universe, the fact that the show paints one of its fans as a sadistic assistant to murder takes all the wind out of the reveal’s sails. And this isn’t the first time Law and Order has done something like this.

Throughout its decades-long run, Law & Order has never been very kind to nerd culture. If a film, book, or video game plays a big role in an episode, it’s usually what caused whatever crime the detectives are solving. Law & Order: Special Victims Unit, in particular, really loves to pick on video games, with entire episodes centered on fictionalized versions of Grand Theft Auto and Slender Man that drove their fans to murderous madness.

Even episodes not centered on a piece of pop culture don’t miss a chance to pick on “over-enthusiastic” fans of whatever’s popular right now. Whether they’re the perp behind the week’s crime, a red herring who leads the detectives astray, or a naive victim, Law & Order‘s geeks are always pitifully awkward at best and “How do you function?” incompetent at worst.

In an age when geek culture is more popular and accepted than ever, it’s depressing to see a show as popular as Law & Order so often sink to the classic “Aw, aren’t nerds weird?” mentality. By now, it’s pretty low-hanging fruit, and you’d think a series about the complexity of the human condition would have found a better way to explore our relationship with pop culture by now.

Taylor Swift’s The Life of a Showgirl spends a 14th nonconsecutive week atop the Billboard 200 chart (dated Oct. 17), earning 93,000 equivalent album units in the United States in the week ending Oct. 8 (down 46%), according to Luminate. The set jumped back to the top of the list a week ago, vaulting 21-1, after it was reissued in a deluxe edition on Sept. 25 with four previously unreleased songs, including the Billboard Hot 100-topper “Patient Zero.”

With a 14th week in the lead for The Life of a Showgirl, Swift adds a milestone 100th career week at No. 1 on the chart across her 15 No. 1s, extending her record among soloists. Elvis Presley has the second-most weeks at No. 1 among soloists, with 67. The total encompasses her 15 No. 1 albums; she is tied with Drake for the most among soloists. Only The Beatles have more weeks at No. 1 (132) and more No. 1 albums (19) among all artists. The Billboard 200 began publishing on a regular, weekly basis in March 1956.

Also in the top 10 of the latest Billboard 200, Drake’s HABIBTI soars 116-2 (returning to its peak) after it was reissued with four additional songs. The Beatles’ chart-topping Rubber Soul is back on the chart for the first time in more than a decade after an expansive deluxe reissue, as it re-enters at No. 4. The Billboard 200 chart ranks the most popular albums of the week in the U.S. based on multi-metric consumption as measured in equivalent album units, compiled by Luminate. Units comprise album sales, track equivalent albums (TEA) and streaming equivalent albums (SEA). Each unit equals one album sale, or 10 individual tracks sold from an album, or 2,500 ad-supported or 1,000 paid/subscription on-demand official audio and video streams generated by songs from an album. Of The Life of a Showgirl’s 93,000 equivalent album units earned in the latest tracking week, SEA units comprise 65,000 (down 52%, equaling 66.64 million on-demand official streams of the set’s tracks; it falls 1-2 on Top Streaming Albums), album sales comprise 27,000 (up 28%; it moves 5-2 on Top Album Sales) and TEA units comprise the remaining sum (down 90%).

Drake’s HABIBTI returns to its peak position, as it flies 116-2 after it was reissued with four additional songs on Oct. 2. The set earned 71,000 equivalent album units in the latest tracking week (up 506%), with nearly 70,000 of that sum driven by SEA activity (up 497%). The expanded edition of the album, dubbed HABIBTI (FOMO), includes the added tracks “Solar Eclipse” (with Don Toliver), “Quebec,” “Cold Shoulder” (with Toliver and Yebba) and “Classic PT2.” All versions of the album are combined for tracking and charting purposes.

Olivia Rodrigo’s chart-topping you seem pretty sad for a girl so in love falls a spot to No. 3 on the latest Billboard 200 with 66,000 equivalent album units earned (down 2%).

The Beatles’ former No. 1 Rubber Soul returns to the Billboard 200, re-entering at No. 4 after its expansive reissue on Oct. 2 across an array of formats and editions. The album, which topped the chart for six weeks in 1966, has been absent from the ranking for more than a decade. It was last on the chart dated Jan. 16, 2016, and last in the top 10 on the April 9, 1966-dated chart. The album includes familiar favorites like “Norwegian Wood (This Bird Has Flown),” “Michelle” and “In My Life.” In the latest tracking week, Rubber Soul earned 58,000 equivalent album units (up 1,626%). The Rainbow Six franchise has a wealth of installments, and when it comes to this amount of volume, you can’t always get them right. That’s the case for Rainbow Six Mobile, which is shutting down in January 2027, Ubisoft announced today.

The game will stay up until Jan. 15, 2027, the developer said, and its upcoming season “will be released as planned next week.” The announcement teases more updates to prepare for the transition, though it’s unclear what this means in practice.

Rainbow Six Mobile has had an unusual run since its reveal in 2022. At the time, Ubisoft said it was a version of Rainbow Six Siege built from the ground up to “adapt the Siege experience to mobile devices.”

Rainbow Six Extraction, the other forgotten R6 spinoff. Image via Ubisoft

Despite a few closed tests and soft launches after 2023 and a litany of changes in 2024, the game only released globally in February 2026. It has had less than eight months in the hands of players all over the world before being sunset—which, as we all know by now, is the “your dog went to live on a farm” of the gaming industry.

At first glance, Rainbow Six Mobile may almost seem doomed to fail. Siege‘s identity is built on its tactical, unforgiving gameplay—the kind that may get you to hold an angle with an ACOG on your gun, so tensely you can barely breathe. It’s a hard element to bring to a mobile game.

“Our goal is not to have you stop playing on PC or console and switch to mobile,” the team said on the announcement blog post. “We know there is nothing like the adrenaline rush of a close Siege match on the big screen or the precision of a mouse and keyboard.” Instead, the game aimed to let fans “get the R6 experience” in “short, accessible bursts,” the developers said.

It’s unclear what will happen to Rainbow Six Mobile until its shutdown date, but Ubisoft will announce more of its plans “in the coming weeks,” according to its announcement.
