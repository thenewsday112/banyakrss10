Overview

There are 2 basic categories of these stocks you can invest in — renewable and nonrenewable energy stocks. 

Companies that produce and distribute energy generated from resources of limited availability such as coal, natural gas and oil are categorized as nonrenewable energy stocks. Businesses that generate revenue from the production and distribution of energy from unlimited resources such as solar, wind and hydropower plants are considered clean or renewable energy stocks. 

Since there is tremendous demand for energy from countries all over the world, stocks from both of these energy sectors can become promising investments for you. Moreover, the recent developments in climate change and global warming may lead to a resurgence of clean energy stocks in the near future. 

Remember that energy stocks are some of the most unique on the marketplace. These businesses can grow quickly through acquisitions and mergers, or they could be acquired while you’re holding the asset. Don’t be afraid to research these assets and determine which companies are going to perform the best in the long-term and which are worth holding so that they can be cashed in during a merger or sale. At other times, you may want to exit these positions to protect yourself, depending on market conditions.

Overview: Stocks Under $5

Stocks that trade under $5 are considered cheap stocks. But unlike stocks under $10 or stocks under $20, stocks under $5 are in a separate category. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) considers stocks that trade under $5 to be penny stocks. Yes, these are often the best cheap stock to buy even if they’re called “penny stocks”. When you do your research, a term like penny stock shouldn’t scare you. Market insights often indicate that these stocks can gain and are even good for swing trading.

True, an investment advisor might not zero in on these stocks first, but you might consider adding these assets to your portfolio so that you have some room for growth. However, room for growth requires patience. Impatient investors may want to look into swing trading or day trading options, which allows them to maximize their profits, enhance their portfolios and plan for the future.

Column: Raucous Milwaukee Brewers celebration reminds Chicago Cubs fans of their fleeting moment in the sun

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Some Chicago Cubs fans will be celebrating the 10-year anniversary of the 2016 World Series title Monday.

Ten is a nice, round number, and the Cubs spent much of the 2026 season reminding everyone of the 2016 championship, building a ceremonial archway at Gallagher Way and inviting back players and staff from that team.

But one of the best parts of that playoff run is likely to be forgotten.

Large crowds of fans showed up at Wrigley Field to add to the chalk wall and take photos on Nov. 6, 2016, days after the Cubs won their first World Series title since 1908. (Nancy Stone/ Chicago Tribune)

It came late during the World Series, when fans flocked to Sheffield and Waveland avenues with colored chalk and began writing their names, the names of loved ones and messages on the bleacher walls outside Wrigley Field. It grew from a couple of fans into an army, and the weekend after the parade brought hundreds out to leave their marks on The Wall.

It was an organic happening that showed Chicago at its best.

Naturally, the Cubs couldn’t leave well enough alone and sent out workers to powerwash The Wall a few days later, erasing all the thoughtful messages. They claimed the ongoing Wrigley renovations were the reason for the move, but it was a lame excuse from executives who saw the chalk-writing fans as a nuisance rather than a blessing.

It never happened again, of course, and we have only photos and our memories to remind us of that beautiful time in November when all was right with the world.

What if … ?

Watching Milwaukee Brewers fans celebrate their championship Friday might have been painful for some Cubs fans, but it also should remind them of how good it felt in 2016 and how you can never take success for granted.

Everyone said the 2016 Cubs were a young team and would do it again.

Everyone was wrong.

Team President Theo Epstein and the World Series trophy roll pass on a double-decker bus toward Wrigley Field during a parade honoring the World Series champion Cubs on Nov. 4, 2016. (Antonio Perez/ Chicago Tribune)

The Cubs couldn’t replicate that season for many reasons, and the architect of it, Theo Epstein, resigned as team president after the 2020 season.

Would Epstein still be with the Cubs if they hadn’t won a World Series 10 years ago?

“Well, I wouldn’t have left voluntarily,” Epstein said when I asked him at the 10-year celebration in July. “We were all here to do one thing. We got it accomplished. Everyone was in it together. Everyone can die happy. We had more work to do, but that’s when it sunk in for me, knowing that I could choose my path going forward. (But) I wasn’t going to leave without getting the job done.”

Can everyone die happy? Would not winning a World Series have left a blot on the lives of Cubs fans?

Epstein left without getting back to the World Series, resigning with a year left on his deal and handing the job off to his friend and longtime wingman, Jed Hoyer, who was the general manager during the rebuild.

The Cubs haven’t won a division title in a full season since 2017 and have gone to the postseason only twice in Hoyer’s six-year reign, losing to the Brewers in the NL Division Series last year and in the wild-card round to the San Diego Padres last month.

Hoyer will be under the gun again to make the Cubs a realistic championship contender, but apparently only from fans since Chairman Tom Ricketts appears satisfied with the status quo. The Brewers’ stellar bullpen performance in the regular season and postseason was in stark contrast to Hoyer’s bullpen construction. Remember, the Cubs were prohibitive favorites to win the division but finished 14 games behind the Brewers.

The pen has been Hoyer’s Achilles heel since he took over from Epstein, and a complete makeover looks inevitable … again.

Reign delay

David Ross is carried off the field by Anthony Rizzo and Jason Heyward (22) after the Cubs defeated the Indians in Game 7 to win the World Series at Progressive Field on Nov. 3, 2016, in Cleveland. (Brian Cassella/Chicago Tribune)

During White Sox playoff games in Cleveland in October, I went looking for the visiting weight room where the Cubs players gathered during the Game 7 rain delay and outfielder Jason Heyward gave his inspirational speech that’s credited with the clinching win. First baseman Anthony Rizzo called it “the best rain delay of all time” after the Cubs won.

Alas, the Guardians renovated Progressive Field recently to add a high-priced seating area for fans, and the visitors clubhouse was moved from behind first base to well down the right-field line. The old weight room was history, and the area was being used as a space for pre- and postgame news conferences.

There was no sign marking the epic moment in Cubs history, or any carving on the wall that read: “Heyward was here.”

Epstein told Rizzo and David Ross on the “Lovable Reunion” podcast that Fox honchos originally wanted to stop the game during the delay and restart it the next day to get better ratings.

“They were asking about the rain, and they said we can suspend this game right now and pick it up tomorrow night in prime time,” Epstein said. “It’s going to be worth like 25 million more viewers. And he was like, ‘As tempting as that is, we can’t f−−−−−− do that. … This is World Series, Game 7.'”

Would the Cubs have still won if Game 7 was stopped and Heyward never had to give his rain-delay speech?

Fortunately it lasted only 17 minutes, and Fox didn’t get a chance to mess with fate.

Feel-good story

The midterm elections on Tuesday remind me of a conversation with Cubs co-owner Laura Ricketts during the 2016 postgame celebration in Cleveland. She said the Cubs win helped take her mind off the ugliness of the presidential election pitting Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton.

“That’s what was great about this,” she said. “It was a great distraction. What a great story. This election has been hurtful for the whole country and sad to watch. This is a good, feel-good story.”

The 2026 World Series was another feel-good story, just without the Cubs.

Final words

On Nov. 6, 2016, I ended a column about the Cubs’ celebration with these words:

“Sooner or later Chicago will come down from this high and get back to life as we know it, namely scraping ice off our windshields with credit cards. But for now, enjoy this organic moment as long as you can. It may never be this fun again.”

Hopefully I’ll be proved wrong about that someday.

And on paper, it might seem as if this young team is answering that call. The Bulls entered Saturday ranked 10th in the league in total rebounds at 45.8 per game. They’re averaging 3.8 more offensive boards through six games than they did last season.

But in reality, the Bulls are still battling to keep bigger and better teams off their backs when the ball goes up. Their high volume of rebounds mostly reflects the fast pace of their playing style, which creates more possessions. In reality, the Bulls sit in the bottom third of the NBA in rebounding percentages — and most concerningly only pull in 65.6% of their defensive rebounds.

Those numbers account only six games, which is far too small of a sample size to make any definitive conclusion. But the Bulls aren’t trending toward the expectations Splitter set.

“We’re a young team,” Splitter said. “We’re coaching them, but there’s a lot of things that we got to get better. But we try to focus on the most important things, the things that we believe we can take an advantage (in). We are better at it, but there’s still room for improvement.”

Splitter also has been encouraged by the efforts of wings Leonard Miller and Wilson, who can use their size and positioning to leverage a higher percentage of rebounds.

The main players who still are struggling, it appears, are the guards. For instance, Josh Giddey’s rebounding numbers have taken a noticeable dip. The 6-foot-10 guard has been a prolific rebounder for most of his career, which supplements his high volume of triple-doubles. But he isn’t getting to the boards as often this season, averaging three fewer rebounds per game than last year.

This is partially due to Giddey playing 5.8 fewer minutes per game in Splitter’s system, which utilizes a deep rotation to support a breakneck pace. But even adjusted to rebounds per 36 minutes, Giddey is averaging two fewer rebounds.

Bulls guard Josh Giddey (3) grabs a rebound against the Wizards on Oct. 24, 2026, at the United Center. (Arthur Maiorella/Chicago Tribune)

We’ve got to do a better job, everyone committing to crashing. … It just kills a team’s spirit when you consistently offensive rebound and we’ve got to look at it that way and try to get better at it.”

Players and coaches emphasize the simplicity of rebounding as a concept. But one wrinkle that has challenged Giddey is the expectation of boxing out that Splitter has placed upon backcourt players.

In many systems, offensive rebounds are predicated on an expectation that the center — or comparable big man — will box out while the rest of the wings crash and the point guard leaks back. But Splitter wants every Bulls player to find a body every time a shot goes up. That’s not how Giddey is used to pursuing rebounds — and it has thrown him off his rhythm.

“I’ve always been a guy that has tried to just hunt the ball down off of our misses, so I’m just adjusting to a different rebounding scheme,” Giddey said. “Before I’ve always been a guy to just go and hunt it.”

There’s a mighty big difference in the price Chicago Bears general manager Ryan Poles paid for Jeffery Simmons and what he gave up three years ago for Montez Sweat.

The premium the Bears met to get Simmons, a first-team All-Pro last season, out of Tennessee erases projection when it comes to the impact he can make for his new team.

That was forecasting sunny days ahead as much as it was leaning into Sweat’s resume.

Simmons, on the other hand, is a guy who changes the math immediately for defensive coordinator Dennis Allen. Nobody at Halas Hall needs to extrapolate what Simmons will mean for the Bears (5-2), who reached the divisional round of the playoffs last season and entered Week 8 behind only the Minnesota Vikings (6-0) and San Francisco 49ers (6-1) in wins in the NFC.

Make no mistake, Poles paid an enormous price to add Simmons, who is 29 and had a career-high 11 sacks last season. One reason Simmons is praised so thoroughly is the character matches the performance. The Titans went to the playoffs in his first three seasons, and it has been all downhill in Nashville since. But who ever heard Simmons grumble?

The Bears traded two first-round picks to acquire edge rusher Khalil Mack a week before the 2018 opener. It looked like a masterstroke that season as the Bears dominated defensively. Mack was really good the next two seasons — maybe a tick shy of great — and then dealt with injuries in 2021.

If the measuring stick for success with the Simmons move is Super Bowl or bust, that’s not unfair. Poles and the Bears appear well-positioned to take multiple swings at it with coach Ben Johnson, quarterback Caleb Williams and a strong offensive cast.

That’s why Poles could feel comfortable with this move. He has Simmons for 10 games this season, beginning Monday night in Seattle, and Simmons is under contract for four more seasons through 2030. Bears offensive coordinator Press Taylor was in his office Thursday morning, deep in preparation for a talented Seahawks defense, when his brother, Bengals coach Zac Taylor, FaceTimed him.

“Thank you,” Zac Taylor said with a grin spread across his face.

Press Taylor wasn’t positive what was up and then found out the Bears indeed had landed Simmons.

The Bears have held up pretty well in some basic defensive metrics. They’re eighth in total defense (313.4 yards per game), third versus the run (87.6) and 18th against the pass (225.9). But all of that is partially a product of the offense’s ability to play keep-away with a league-high average time of possession of 35 minutes, 30 seconds. The next-closest offense is three minutes behind the Bears.

The Bears defense ranks 32nd in yards per play (6.25) and yards per pass (8.15) and 23rd in yards per rush (4.44) — evidence that the more it’s on the field, the worse things will be.

Adding Simmons to the middle of the defensive line affects everything. It will create one-on-one opportunities for teammates, including Sweat, with whom Simmons played at Mississippi State. Allen won’t have to lean on the blitz so much to create pressure. And Simmons is probably a better run defender than he is a pass rusher — and he’s elite at that compared with other interior players.

Poles’ biggest move since trading the No. 1 pick in 2023 to the Carolina Panthers — a deal that led to the pick used to select Williams — comes at the right time. That’s because rivals in the rugged NFC North have help arriving too.

Green Bay Packers edge rusher Micah Parsons reportedly will return to practice next week, meaning his comeback from a torn ACL is nearly complete. The Detroit Lions were hurting in the secondary, but versatile safety Brian Branch is back and fellow safety Kerby Joseph is practicing.

The Vikings, meanwhile, have the No. 1 defense in the NFL, so the Bears had to do something to improve their chances of playing true complementary football as the playoff race heats up.

The Bears believe with Simmons they have a chance to make this deal a winner for them multiple times over, meaning this year and in seasons to come. They didn’t win a playoff game in four seasons with Mack, but they have a better quarterback now and Johnson has proved to be elite as a play designer and caller.

Simmons will be a legitimate multiplier for the defense. The Bears just need those equations to extend deep into the postseason.

By the way, the last time Simmons was in the playoffs after the 2021 season, he had three sacks in a loss to the eventual AFC champion Bengals.

The last time the NFL had a matchup of teams giving up 13 points or fewer and scoring 30-plus points per game in Week 8 or later was in 2019, when the Baltimore Ravens’ high-scoring offense beat the New England Patriots’ stout defense and handed the Pats their first loss that season.

Vikings quarterback Kyler Murray said his confidence in the the team’s offense will never waver, knowing the players around him, and believes a breakout performance is coming.

“We’ve just got to end the drive with touchdowns instead of kicking field goals,” Murray said.
