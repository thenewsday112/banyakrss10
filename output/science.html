Lucky Lindy survived 2 crashes in Illinois

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Less than a year before he became the most famous person in the world, airplane pilot Charles A. Lindbergh appeared on the front page of the Tribune. The story, dated Nov. 4, 1926, was a paragraph long and misspelled his name:

“For the second time within a month Charles Lindberg, pilot of the St. Louis-Chicago air mail plane, yesterday encountered bad flying weather and, rather than risk a hazardous landing, made a parachute jump to safety, leaving his plane uncontrolled. By telephone Lindberg said he landed safely near Stafford, Ill., but at a late hour had not been able to find his plane.”

Two days later, the newspaper ran a photo of the smashed-up airplane, this time getting the aviator’s middle initial wrong in the caption. According to the paper, Lindbergh “jumped 13,000 feet in parachute when caught in a snowstorm over McLean County. The plane fell within 300 feet of the home of Charles Thompson, farmer, near Bloomington, Ill.”

That parachute jump and the earlier one are described in greater detail in Lindbergh’s memoir, “WE.”

Lindbergh liked to refer to himself and The Spirit of St. Louis — his airplane or “ship” as he preferred to call it — as “we.” In May 1927, man and machine would achieve together what had seemed impossible, a nonstop solo journey by air from New York to Paris across the vast Atlantic Ocean.

At the time of the parachute jumps, Lindbergh was working for the Robertson Aircraft Corp., which had a contract with the Postal Service to deliver mail by air from St. Louis to Chicago — specifically, Checkerboard Field in Maywood — with intermediary stops in Springfield and Peoria.

The Tribune published a photo showing Charles Lindbergh's wrecked airmail plane near Bloomington in 1926. (Chicago Tribune)

In the book, he noted that there were “only two conditions which delay the air mail: frost and sleet.” When these conditions prevailed, “the mail is entrained and usually reaches its destination at least as soon as it would have if sent by train in the first place.”

“With all of our difficulties, however, the mail went through with surprising regularity,” he wrote. “During the first five months of operation we made connections over ninety-eight per cent of our trips.”

With the stage set, Lindbergh then shared accounts of his jumps, taken directly from “official reports.”

The result is a matter-of-fact retelling of harrowing experiences where survival wasn’t guaranteed. “Being caught in a fog at night,” he wrote simply, “was the cause of two of my forced jumps.”

In the first, he almost collided with his crashing plane:

“When I had jumped it had practically stopped dead and I had neglected to cut the switches. Apparently when the ship nosed down an additional supply of gasoline drained to the carburetor. Soon she came into sight, about a quarter mile away and headed in the general direction of my parachute.”

After avoiding being cut to pieces by his own plane, Lindbergh touched down in a cornfield outside Ottawa. After bundling up his chute and hailing a passing motorist, he proceeded to telephone his employer to report the crash and to locate the felled plane. “The mail was laid open and one sack of mail was on the ground,” he noted of the crash site. “The mail, however, was uninjured.”

Almost a month later, he was forced to jump again. “The Irving chute functioned perfectly,” he wrote with satisfaction, but the “ground appeared so suddenly that I landed directly on top of a barbed wire fence without seeing it.”

Charles Lindbergh sits in the cockpit of a small plane as the airmail pilot for the route between Chicago and St. Louis on April 16, 1926. (Chicago Herald and Examiner)

Uninjured thanks to his thick flight suit, he responded as before, contacting his employer and locating the plane, which had “gone through two fences and the wall of the house.”

The mail didn’t fare as well this time, with a “full bag from St. Louis … split open and some of the mail oil-soaked but legible.” The other two bags were undamaged.

“It was just about at this time, or shortly after,” he wrote, “that I first began to think about a New York-Paris flight.”

Lindbergh’s dry tone is something Tribune literary critic Fanny Butcher remarked upon in her 1927 review of “WE,” the “best-selling book of nonfiction in town.” The book “is modest almost to the point of being less than the newspaper stories about him,” she observed. “It tells less than reporters found out.”

In what could serve as a summation of his description of the two parachute jumps, she wrote: “There isn’t a word about what he thought at any moment of his life, except what he thought about his ship or the science of aeronautics.”

Butcher’s review was published a week before Lindbergh arrived in Chicago by air on Aug. 13, 1927. As the newspaper noted, pilot and plane circled “three times around the loop and as far north as Chicago avenue” while people below stopped what they were doing to look skyward.

“He comes a little late to the second city of the continent, so far as the first explosion of enthusiasm is concerned,” the Tribune observed, “but he can never be too late to find the admiration and regard due him.”

Touching down at the city’s Municipal Airport, today’s Midway Airport on the Southwest Side, Lindbergh found himself thronged by admirers.

The crowds only grew as he headed by car first to Comiskey Park and then to Soldier Field. The Tribune estimated that “260,000 persons grouped at three principal points and packed along a twelve mile route … saw Lindbergh himself.”

At Soldier Field, he told the people gathered there that “Chicago has been in a position to observe the development of aviation as probably no other city in the world,” before describing the history of airmail delivery to the city and “the necessity of developing commercial aviation.”

Chicago seems to have taken Lindbergh’s advice. In 1929, the Tribune’s Robert Wood proclaimed: “Chicago is the junction point for the longest of all air lines in the world, the transcontinental mail route.”

This volume of air travel would lead to the installation of an enormous “beacon light” to guide planes to Midway Airport, an idea first proposed when Lindbergh visited the city.

On Aug. 27, 1930, the “mighty Lindbergh air beacon shot its blue-gray beam into the skies” for the first time, the Tribune reported, “and began its official service to night flyers.”

Brief ceremonies were held on the roof of the Palmolive Building, 919 N. Michigan Ave., but the newspaper noted that “Charles A. Lindbergh, the ex-airmail pilot whose name is perpetuated by the light,” was not in attendance to see the beacon turned on.

And although the beacon was a memorable sight, its practical benefit for pilots appeared limited on that first night.

“David L. Behneke of the Boeing Air transport, flying the United States mail in from Omaha, arrived at the Municipal field at 9:40 pm,” the Tribune reported. “He was about 100 miles out when the light was turned on, but he did not pick it up until he had arrived directly over the field, which is 13 miles southwest of the loop.”

Tonight, without the use of visual aids you should be able to spot the Tycho Crater, Kepler Crater, and the Mare Imbrium. When is the next Full Moon?

The next Full Moon will take place on November 24.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

Computer Simulations Suggest Building Blocks of Life Didn't Arrive on Prehistoric Comets

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
CNN reports: The popular theory that comets were crucial in kick-starting life on Earth billions of years ago may not hold up, according to new computer simulations that counter decades of scientific thinking. The finding suggests the fragile organic molecules supposedly carried by the icy objects would have been destroyed on impact, upending an established origin of life theory that dates back to at least 1961 [with earlier versions postulated in 1908]... Early in its history — over 4 billion years ago — Earth may have experienced a period of heavy bombardment from more than 100,000 comets larger than 100 meters (328 feet), according to the new study, published Wednesday in the journal Scientific Reports. Those impacts, however, were mostly too heavy for life-starting molecules to withstand. The team used the latest models of comet formation and evolution to simulate this early planetary period 100,000 times. "Given what we know about surface environments on the early Earth, on average, our simulations found that there were no successful impacts," said lead study author Richard Anslow, who conducted the research while completing his doctorate at the Institute of Astronomy of the University of Cambridge in the United Kingdom... "The typical velocities of these comets when they impact the Earth meant that actually very few, if any, would have been able to deliver intact organic molecules, so comets actually don't seem to be as useful for prebiotic chemistry as was previously thought," Anslow said. More precisely, 54% of the simulations showed no impact at all, while the remaining 46% had at least one and a "tiny minority" returned up to three collisions, Anslow said. "We can't rule out confidently that there was never a successful impact — there could have been one of these fluke impacts." However, such flukes had to occur in a "perfect location" that already met several important conditions, including the presence of liquid water and other chemicals needed to support life. That combination makes the overall process unlikely, he added. The article notes his argument that even if a successful impact occurred, it's unlikely an important chemical like hydrogen cyanide (HCN) could survive the impact, and would've dissolved in an ocean impact. Plus, "many other precursor molecules are also needed, such as compounds that contain phosphorus and sulfur, but these are not routinely found in comets, Anslow said." An alternative cosmic source of organic material could have been asteroids, which typically struck Earth more slowly and in greater numbers. Anslow pointed to recent findings on samples from asteroids Bennu and Ryugu, which contain the majority of protein-building amino acids. The recent study also raises another intriguing possibility: While Earth may not have had the ideal conditions to foster HCN from comet impacts, ancient Mars might have been better suited. "Because Mars is much smaller than Earth and further away from the sun, the typical impact velocity of comets is much lower, so while these impacts on Earth were destroying most of the HCN, this wasn't the case so much for Mars," Anslow said. This idea could add another dimension to the long-standing question of whether the red planet once supported life. While the authors closely analyzed how HCN could have survived comet impact, the chemical may have undergone reactions before the icy bodies entered Earth's atmosphere, said Queenie Chan, an associate professor of Earth sciences at Royal Holloway University of London, who was not involved with the study. This process could have created biologically important compounds, such as those protein building blocks, that are less volatile and more likely to survive.

Why Your Hex Converter Lies to You: Two's Complement, the 53-Bit Wall, and Radix Magic

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Every programmer eventually encounters that moment of quiet confusion during a late-night debugging session: you cast a signed 8-bit -1 to hexadecimal, expecting something clean and human-readable, only for the debugger to spit back 0xFF. Widen it to a 32-bit register, and it suddenly balloons into 0xFFFFFFFF.


Where did the minus sign go? Why didn't it output -0x01? And why does running 0x20000000000001 through your standard JavaScript parser silently yield the wrong integer altogether?


Number base conversion is often treated as a trivial Computer Science 101 exercise—something solved with a simple parseInt(val, 16) or a textbook division-by-radix loop. But beneath high-level abstractions, radix shifts are inextricably linked to hardware registers, two’s complement mathematics, IEEE-754 rounding traps, and data-encoding strategies.


Here is what is actually happening under the hood when bits change their radix, and why understanding these low-level mechanics prevents catastrophic bugs in production.

1. The Illusion of the Minus Sign: How Two's Complement Works

Hardware processors don't have dedicated silicon cells reserved for storing an aesthetic - glyph. In CPU registers, negative numbers are encoded via Two’s Complement.


In an NNN-bit register, the most significant bit (MSB) acts as a negative weight:

Value=−bN−12N−1+∑i=0N−2bi2i\text{Value} = -b_{N-1} 2^{N-1} + \sum_{i=0}^{N-2} b_i 2^iValue=−bN−1​2N−1+i=0∑N−2​bi​2i


When you assign -1 to an 8-bit signed integer (int8_t):

  1. Invert all bits of positive 1 (0000 0001 →\rightarrow→ 1111 1110).
  2. Add 1 (1111 1110 + 0000 0001 = 1111 1111).


In hexadecimal, each character represents an exact 4-bit nibble. Thus, 1111 1111 maps directly to 0xFF.

c #include <stdio.h> #include <stdint.h> int main() { int8_t small_neg = -1; int32_t wide_neg = small_neg; // Sign extension occurs here printf("8-bit hex: 0x%02X\n", (uint8_t)small_neg); // 0xFF printf("32-bit hex: 0x%08X\n", (uint32_t)wide_neg); // 0xFFFFFFFF return 0; }

The Sign Extension Trap

Notice what happened when widening int8_t to int32_t: the single byte 0xFF became 0xFFFFFFFF.


If the hardware merely padded the higher 24 bits with zeros (0x000000FF), the value would evaluate to +255, silently mutating your application state. To preserve the mathematical value of -1, the CPU performs Sign Extension: it duplicates the MSB (which is 1) across all newly allocated upper bits.


Pro-Tip for Packet Inspection:

When inspecting raw network packets or memory dumps, you can instantly determine if a hexadecimal value represents a negative signed integer without converting to decimal: look at the leading character.


Because 8 through F have their highest bit set to 1:

  • 0x8... through 0xF... →\rightarrow→ Negative (in signed contexts)
  • 0x0... through 0x7... →\rightarrow→ Zero or Positive

2. Arithmetic Shift (>>) vs. Logical Shift (>>>): A Subtle Production Hazard

Understanding two’s complement makes the difference between bitwise shift operators crystal clear:

  • Arithmetic Right Shift (>>): Preserves the sign bit. Vacated positions on the left are backfilled with whatever the MSB was.
  • Logical Right Shift (>>>): Agnostic to sign. It unconditionally backfills vacated positions with zeros.
typescript const value = -42; // Binary: 11111111 11111111 11111111 11010110 (in 32-bit) console.log(value >> 1); // Output: -21 (MSB remains 1; arithmetic integrity preserved) console.log(value >>> 1); // Output: 2147483627 (MSB replaced with 0; immediately converts to large unsigned integer)

In telemetry, hashing routines, and protocol deserialization, accidentally using >>> instead of >> (or vice versa) produces bugs that unit tests with small positive integers never catch.

3. The 53-Bit Wall: Why JavaScript Silently Corrupts Large Radixes

Consider this seemingly benign conversion snippet:

javascript const highSeqId = "9007199254740993"; // 2^53 + 1 console.log(Number(highSeqId).toString(16)); // Expected: "20000000000001" // Actual: "20000000000000" (Precision collapsed!)

Standard JavaScript Number types conform to the IEEE-754 double-precision floating-point specification. Double floats allocate:

  • 1 sign bit
  • 11 exponent bits
  • 52 explicit fraction bits (providing 53 bits of precision with the implicit leading 1)


Any integer larger than Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER (253−12^{53} - 1253−1, or 9,007,199,254,740,991) cannot be uniquely represented. When you convert an arbitrary-large hexadecimal hash or a 64-bit Snowflake ID via standard floating-point numbers, the least significant bits get silently rounded to the nearest representable floating-point neighbor.

The Fix: BigInt and Limbs

Arbitrary-precision arithmetic operates outside the Floating-Point Unit (FPU). Instead of relying on a single hardware register, runtimes allocate heap arrays of 64-bit integer chunks (often called machine limbs):

javascript // Lossless 64-bit / 128-bit Radix Transformations const safeVal = BigInt("0x20000000000001"); console.log(safeVal.toString(10)); // "9007199254740993" (Exact) console.log(safeVal.toString(36)); // "2000000000001"

4. Radix 36: The Workhorse of URL Slugs and Distributed IDs

While base-2 (binary), base-8 (octal), and base-16 (hexadecimal) map directly into CPU word architectures because they are powers of two (21,23,242^1, 2^3, 2^421,23,24), higher non-power-of-two radices serve specialized architectural purposes.


Chief among them is Radix 36 (0–9, a–z).

While Base-64 yields the highest raw density, its reliance on uppercase versus lowercase characters makes it vulnerable to collisions on case-insensitive filesystems and legacy databases. Furthermore, standard Base-64 requires URL-safe variations (RFC 4648) to avoid collision with URI reserved characters.


Radix 36 hits the sweet spot: it is totally case-insensitive, yields compact strings, and requires zero URL encoding.

python def to_base36(num: int) -> str: alphabet = "0123456789abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz" if num == 0: return "0" arr = [] while num: num, rem = divmod(num, 36) arr.append(alphabet[rem]) return "".join(reversed(arr)) print(to_base36(1685894123512)) # "hicw5f3g" (Clean slug ready for routing)

5. Visualizing & Validating Complex Edge Cases

When designing custom bitmasks, verifying register overflows, or calculating variable-width two's complement strings, hand-calculating bit shifts invites human error.


If you are debugging packet headers or writing firmware-level parsers, you can cross-reference your math against this interactive Number Base Converter sandbox, which calculates signed/unsigned representations, arbitrary-width sign extensions, and custom radices from 2 to 36 without floating-point precision drops.

Summary Checklist for Systems Developers

  1. Check your signedness: 0xFF is 255 in an unsigned 8-bit byte, but -1 in a signed two's complement register.
  2. Be cautious with bitwise shifts in JavaScript: JS converts operands into 32-bit signed integers before running bitwise operators. >> sign-extends; >>> zero-extends.
  3. Beware the 53-bit boundary: Never parse 64-bit IDs or large hashes with parseInt() or floating-point primitives—always enforce BigInt.
  4. Use Base-36 for safe slugification: When you need human-readable, URL-safe, compact sequential keys without case-sensitivity bugs, reach for Radix 36 before Base-64.

Rare meteorite from Mars gives us a view into its ancient crust

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Crater counts tell us that most of the Martian surface is more than 3 billion years old, but pieces of Mars we have on Earth tell a different story. The three main classes of Martian meteorites—shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites—are all 2.4 billion years old or younger. Until now, only two genuinely ancient Martian meteorites were known: Allan Hills 84001, found in Antarctica, and a jumbled rock from northwest Africa nicknamed Black Beauty. Now we have a third one.

A team led by Yang Liu, a planetary scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, has shown that a Martian meteorite called Teghaza 001, found in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022, formed at least 4.1 billion years ago. “It's very old and similar to some of the oldest rocks on Earth,” Liu says.

NASAs Voyager 1 gives mission control another moment of panic

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA's farthest spacecraft gave mission control a scare when it inexplicably went silent and missed two days of planned check-ins. 

The nail-biting incident happened just two weeks ago, when the Deep Space Network didn't receive a call from Voyager 1 on either Oct. 18 or 19. But before the agency could send repair commands, the spacecraft, now outside the solar system, signaled back on Oct. 20. No communication outages have happened since. 

Engineers still don't know what caused the problem. So far, the team has discovered that one set of thrusters shut down, leaving a backup set to take over. The thrusters are small engines that point and steady the spacecraft. Voyager 1's computers do this automatically whenever they detect an issue with the active thrusters to ensure the spacecraft is in a safe mode. 

"This is the first time we've seen the spacecraft do something like this, but we are also at a point in the mission where we have to expect the unexpected," said Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, in a statement. "We're relieved to be back in contact with Voyager 1 and are eager to understand what happened."

Voyager 1's trouble adds to a growing list of signs that NASA's longest-running mission is winding down. Its plutonium power source loses about four watts every year, forcing engineers to turn off two science instruments to conserve power. On Nov. 18, the spacecraft is expected to reach a historic milestone of one "light-day" from Earth. That distance, roughly 16.09 billion miles away, measures how far light travels over 24 hours.

SEE ALSO: Scientists think they've just discovered a new dwarf planet like Pluto

Voyager 1 has traveled farther than any other human-made object and is racing through space at 38,000 mph.

Launched in 1977 to survey the outer planets, the spacecraft and its twin Voyager 2 have explored four planets — Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune, and Uranus — 48 moons, and a host of planetary magnetic fields and rings.

In August 2012, Voyager 1 was the first to enter interstellar space. The two remain the only spacecraft ever to leave the solar system, though New Horizons is trying to make its escape as well.  

Both Voyagers run on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, which are power units that turn heat from decaying plutonium into electricity. In September, NASA engineers stretched their shrinking power supplies by turning off three instruments on each and replacing them with three lower-powered alternatives — a plan they nicknamed "the Big Bang." The devices involved in the swap also help keep the spacecraft warm enough to work in the freezing cold of space, so the swaps had to happen at the same moment to spread heat evenly.

That strategy should keep their Cosmic Ray Subsystems, Magnetometers, and Plasma Wave Subsystems running for at least one more year, according to NASA. The devices measure high‑energy particles, the local magnetic field, and ripples in charged gas around the spacecraft.

Once Voyager 1 passes one light day, a radio message from Earth will take 24 hours to reach it, and its reply will take another 24 hours to come back.

"If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response," said Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager.

What are Moon phases?

The Moon takes approximately 29.5 days to complete an orbit around Earth, going through a series of phases along the way. Depending on its position relative to Earth and the Sun, the amount of its illuminated surface we can see changes, creating the familiar sequence of crescents, quarter moons and the Full Moon. Once the cycle is complete, the pattern repeats, as explained by NASA.

The eight phases are:

New Moon - The Moon is between Earth and the sun, so the side we see is dark (in other words, it's invisible to the eye).

Waxing Crescent - A small sliver of light appears on the right side (Northern Hemisphere).

First Quarter - Half of the Moon is lit on the right side. It looks like a half-Moon.

Waxing Gibbous - More than half is lit up, but it's not quite full yet.

Full Moon - The whole face of the Moon is illuminated and fully visible.

Waning Gibbous - The Moon starts losing light on the right side. (Northern Hemisphere)

Third Quarter (or Last Quarter) - Another half-Moon, but now the left side is lit.

Waning Crescent - A thin sliver of light remains on the left side before going dark again.

The rapid decline of Antarctic sea ice since 2016 has triggered a dramatic shift in a crucial part of Earth’s climate system, scientists say.

In a paper published Wednesday, researchers documented a sharp salinity increase in a vast layer of the Southern Ocean, extending from 2,300 to 4,000 feet deep and encircling Antarctica. The shift reverses decades of freshening and echoes ocean changes usually associated with major global climate shifts over millennia but is now happening in just a few years.

Sculpture re-creates odd optical effects of famed Lycurgus Cup

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Dating back to around 400 CE and made of dichroic glass, the Lycurgus Cup is notable for exhibiting different colors depending on the light. (It gets its name from the scene depicted on its surface, of King Lycurgus of Thrace.) Light it from the front, and the cup looks green; light it from behind, and the color changes to a deep red. University of Sheffield scientists have re-created the original recipe to produce a contemporary glass sculpture with the same optical effect.

The cup is considered one of the seven glass wonders of the world, one of just 40 glass cage cups that survived. Exactly why the unusual color effects of the Lycurgus Cup occurred was a mystery until 2007, when a team of scientists discovered the color shifts were due to the presence of nanoparticles in the glass—the result of Roman artisans adding finely ground gold and silver into the mix. Those nanoparticles change how the electrons in the cup vibrate in response to light so that the color one sees shifts with the observer’s vantage point.

As we reported in 2019, it’s a similar phenomenon to the one responsible for the shifting colors of 19th-century daguerreotypes due to the scattering properties of nanoparticles. The Romans added salt when they melted the silicon (sand) to make the glass, and those salts contained gold atoms. In the melting process, those atoms aggregate and produce nanoparticles, which in turn produce the colors. It’s how any stained glass is made. It's also similar to how the precise lattice-like structure of photonic crystals produces iridescent colors in nature, like the wings of butterflies, or opals.
