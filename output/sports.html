Column: Raucous Milwaukee Brewers celebration reminds Chicago Cubs fans of their fleeting moment in the sun

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Some Chicago Cubs fans will be celebrating the 10-year anniversary of the 2016 World Series title Monday.

Ten is a nice, round number, and the Cubs spent much of the 2026 season reminding everyone of the 2016 championship, building a ceremonial archway at Gallagher Way and inviting back players and staff from that team.

But one of the best parts of that playoff run is likely to be forgotten.

Large crowds of fans showed up at Wrigley Field to add to the chalk wall and take photos on Nov. 6, 2016, days after the Cubs won their first World Series title since 1908. (Nancy Stone/ Chicago Tribune)

It came late during the World Series, when fans flocked to Sheffield and Waveland avenues with colored chalk and began writing their names, the names of loved ones and messages on the bleacher walls outside Wrigley Field. It grew from a couple of fans into an army, and the weekend after the parade brought hundreds out to leave their marks on The Wall.

It was an organic happening that showed Chicago at its best.

Naturally, the Cubs couldn’t leave well enough alone and sent out workers to powerwash The Wall a few days later, erasing all the thoughtful messages. They claimed the ongoing Wrigley renovations were the reason for the move, but it was a lame excuse from executives who saw the chalk-writing fans as a nuisance rather than a blessing.

It never happened again, of course, and we have only photos and our memories to remind us of that beautiful time in November when all was right with the world.

What if … ?

Watching Milwaukee Brewers fans celebrate their championship Friday might have been painful for some Cubs fans, but it also should remind them of how good it felt in 2016 and how you can never take success for granted.

Everyone said the 2016 Cubs were a young team and would do it again.

Everyone was wrong.

Team President Theo Epstein and the World Series trophy roll pass on a double-decker bus toward Wrigley Field during a parade honoring the World Series champion Cubs on Nov. 4, 2016. (Antonio Perez/ Chicago Tribune)

The Cubs couldn’t replicate that season for many reasons, and the architect of it, Theo Epstein, resigned as team president after the 2020 season.

Would Epstein still be with the Cubs if they hadn’t won a World Series 10 years ago?

“Well, I wouldn’t have left voluntarily,” Epstein said when I asked him at the 10-year celebration in July. “We were all here to do one thing. We got it accomplished. Everyone was in it together. Everyone can die happy. We had more work to do, but that’s when it sunk in for me, knowing that I could choose my path going forward. (But) I wasn’t going to leave without getting the job done.”

Can everyone die happy? Would not winning a World Series have left a blot on the lives of Cubs fans?

Epstein left without getting back to the World Series, resigning with a year left on his deal and handing the job off to his friend and longtime wingman, Jed Hoyer, who was the general manager during the rebuild.

The Cubs haven’t won a division title in a full season since 2017 and have gone to the postseason only twice in Hoyer’s six-year reign, losing to the Brewers in the NL Division Series last year and in the wild-card round to the San Diego Padres last month.

Hoyer will be under the gun again to make the Cubs a realistic championship contender, but apparently only from fans since Chairman Tom Ricketts appears satisfied with the status quo. The Brewers’ stellar bullpen performance in the regular season and postseason was in stark contrast to Hoyer’s bullpen construction. Remember, the Cubs were prohibitive favorites to win the division but finished 14 games behind the Brewers.

The pen has been Hoyer’s Achilles heel since he took over from Epstein, and a complete makeover looks inevitable … again.

Reign delay

David Ross is carried off the field by Anthony Rizzo and Jason Heyward (22) after the Cubs defeated the Indians in Game 7 to win the World Series at Progressive Field on Nov. 3, 2016, in Cleveland. (Brian Cassella/Chicago Tribune)

During White Sox playoff games in Cleveland in October, I went looking for the visiting weight room where the Cubs players gathered during the Game 7 rain delay and outfielder Jason Heyward gave his inspirational speech that’s credited with the clinching win. First baseman Anthony Rizzo called it “the best rain delay of all time” after the Cubs won.

Alas, the Guardians renovated Progressive Field recently to add a high-priced seating area for fans, and the visitors clubhouse was moved from behind first base to well down the right-field line. The old weight room was history, and the area was being used as a space for pre- and postgame news conferences.

There was no sign marking the epic moment in Cubs history, or any carving on the wall that read: “Heyward was here.”

Epstein told Rizzo and David Ross on the “Lovable Reunion” podcast that Fox honchos originally wanted to stop the game during the delay and restart it the next day to get better ratings.

“They were asking about the rain, and they said we can suspend this game right now and pick it up tomorrow night in prime time,” Epstein said. “It’s going to be worth like 25 million more viewers. And he was like, ‘As tempting as that is, we can’t f−−−−−− do that. … This is World Series, Game 7.'”

Would the Cubs have still won if Game 7 was stopped and Heyward never had to give his rain-delay speech?

Fortunately it lasted only 17 minutes, and Fox didn’t get a chance to mess with fate.

Feel-good story

The midterm elections on Tuesday remind me of a conversation with Cubs co-owner Laura Ricketts during the 2016 postgame celebration in Cleveland. She said the Cubs win helped take her mind off the ugliness of the presidential election pitting Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton.

“That’s what was great about this,” she said. “It was a great distraction. What a great story. This election has been hurtful for the whole country and sad to watch. This is a good, feel-good story.”

The 2026 World Series was another feel-good story, just without the Cubs.

Final words

On Nov. 6, 2016, I ended a column about the Cubs’ celebration with these words:

“Sooner or later Chicago will come down from this high and get back to life as we know it, namely scraping ice off our windshields with credit cards. But for now, enjoy this organic moment as long as you can. It may never be this fun again.”

Hopefully I’ll be proved wrong about that someday.

Chicago Bulls are trying to improve their rebounding. It&#8217;s still not enough for coach Tiago Splitter.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

BOSTON — Caleb Wilson bounded into the paint with the lack of abandon every rookie possesses, stretching his arms toward the rim in a desperate reach for the ball.

Less than seven minutes into Friday’s 125-111 loss to the Boston Celtics, the Bulls already were flirting with a double-digit deficit. Wilson knew the Bulls needed a spark. An offensive rebound — and an ensuing whistle in response to a reach-in by Payton Pritchard — answered that call.

Friday’s loss illustrated the power of fervent, constant rebounding to sway the momentum of a game. The Bulls dug too deep of a deficit in the first quarter to overcome, but their insistence on pursuing second-chance opportunities buoyed an outmatched roster to win the second half, accounting for 25 of the team’s point total.

Bulls coach Tiago Splitter talks with forward Leonard Miller in the first quarter of a preseason game against the Grizzlies on Oct. 9, 2026, at the United Center. (John J. Kim/Chicago Tribune)

Rebounding is supposed to be a key strand of this team’s DNA. Coach Tiago Splitter set an expectation in preseason for players to shoulder a hefty responsibility for their individual rebounding. And on paper, it might seem as if this young team is answering that call. The Bulls entered Saturday ranked 10th in the league in total rebounds at 45.8 per game. They’re averaging 3.8 more offensive boards through six games than they did last season.

But in reality, the Bulls are still battling to keep bigger and better teams off their backs when the ball goes up. Their high volume of rebounds mostly reflects the fast pace of their playing style, which creates more possessions. In reality, the Bulls sit in the bottom third of the NBA in rebounding percentages — and most concerningly only pull in 65.6% of their defensive rebounds.

Those numbers account only six games, which is far too small of a sample size to make any definitive conclusion. But the Bulls aren’t trending toward the expectations Splitter set.

“We’re a young team,” Splitter said. “We’re coaching them, but there’s a lot of things that we got to get better. But we try to focus on the most important things, the things that we believe we can take an advantage (in). We are better at it, but there’s still room for improvement.”

Tobe Awaka is the only player who has earned Splitter’s complete approval, earning every minute he’s given on the court through tireless effort to claim any and all rebounds during his rotations. Splitter also has been encouraged by the efforts of wings Leonard Miller and Wilson, who can use their size and positioning to leverage a higher percentage of rebounds.

The main players who still are struggling, it appears, are the guards. For instance, Josh Giddey’s rebounding numbers have taken a noticeable dip. The 6-foot-10 guard has been a prolific rebounder for most of his career, which supplements his high volume of triple-doubles. But he isn’t getting to the boards as often this season, averaging three fewer rebounds per game than last year.

This is partially due to Giddey playing 5.8 fewer minutes per game in Splitter’s system, which utilizes a deep rotation to support a breakneck pace. But even adjusted to rebounds per 36 minutes, Giddey is averaging two fewer rebounds.

Bulls guard Josh Giddey (3) grabs a rebound against the Wizards on Oct. 24, 2026, at the United Center. (Arthur Maiorella/Chicago Tribune)

“It sounds so simple but it’s not as simple as it looks,” Giddey said. “If we look at the analytics of it, we’re just not going as much as we should be — and myself included in that. We’ve got to do a better job, everyone committing to crashing. … It just kills a team’s spirit when you consistently offensive rebound and we’ve got to look at it that way and try to get better at it.”

Players and coaches emphasize the simplicity of rebounding as a concept. But one wrinkle that has challenged Giddey is the expectation of boxing out that Splitter has placed upon backcourt players.

In many systems, offensive rebounds are predicated on an expectation that the center — or comparable big man — will box out while the rest of the wings crash and the point guard leaks back. But Splitter wants every Bulls player to find a body every time a shot goes up. That’s not how Giddey is used to pursuing rebounds — and it has thrown him off his rhythm.

“I’ve always been a guy that has tried to just hunt the ball down off of our misses, so I’m just adjusting to a different rebounding scheme,” Giddey said. “Before I’ve always been a guy to just go and hunt it.”

These box-out assignments present a new habit for the Bulls to learn — and a notable change for guards, who are often asked to box out at a significantly lower rate than frontcourt players. But veteran guards such as Norman Powell don’t want to couch their inefficiency on the boards in any excuses about a new scheme.

The Bulls have learned the hard way how a ceded offensive rebound can suck the life out of an outmatched team.

“We just got to sight-check,” Powell said. “A lot of it is ball-watching to see if the ball is going to go in or not. And as you’re doing that, those guys are crashing out the corner. The first thing is sight-check, see where your guy’s at. Is he crashing? Now you get a body. Now you track the ball down. What all of us are doing is a lot of ball-watching when a shot is going up rather than finding a body, hitting it and then going to pursue the ball.”

This is also not a problem that will be quickly fixed by the return of starting center Nic Claxton, who has not played this season after suffering a hamstring injury in the preseason. Claxton is a notable rim protector, but he is not a high-efficiency rebounder and averaged only 6.9 boards for the Brooklyn Nets last season. The center does not pose a massive presence in the paint and did not box out consistently for the Nets, which means he likely won’t translate to the Bulls system immediately.

That could be a source of frustration for the Bulls, who want to fix their rebounding as soon as possible. They understand the need for patience in many other areas — shooting, playmaking, defense by rookies — as they work through a rebuild. But that patience does not extend to effort-based habits like crashing the boards.

“The timeline needs to be next game,” Powell said. “It’s not something like, ‘Oh, we’ve got to figure out how to rebound.’ That’s just effort. Effort and attention to detail and competitiveness. The little things that we can control, that is right now. The X’s and O’s of execution offensively, that takes time. But effort, that’s something that happens immediately.”

Column: Jeffery Simmons&#8217; arrival changes the math for Chicago Bears defense, which finally has &#8216;a true alpha&#8217;

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There’s a mighty big difference in the price Chicago Bears general manager Ryan Poles paid for Jeffery Simmons and what he gave up three years ago for Montez Sweat.

The premium the Bears met to get Simmons, a first-team All-Pro last season, out of Tennessee erases projection when it comes to the impact he can make for his new team.

The Bears talked about Sweat being a “multiplier” — a player whose presence alone elevates the level of those around him — when Poles acquired him from the Washington Commanders for a second-round pick on Oct. 31, 2023. That was forecasting sunny days ahead as much as it was leaning into Sweat’s resume.

Simmons, on the other hand, is a guy who changes the math immediately for defensive coordinator Dennis Allen. Nobody at Halas Hall needs to extrapolate what Simmons will mean for the Bears (5-2), who reached the divisional round of the playoffs last season and entered Week 8 behind only the Minnesota Vikings (6-0) and San Francisco 49ers (6-1) in wins in the NFC.

Make no mistake, Poles paid an enormous price to add Simmons, who is 29 and had a career-high 11 sacks last season. He had four sacks, six tackles for a loss and four deflections through seven games this season for a lousy Titans team that will take the draft capital and try to reboot in Year 2 under coach Robert Saleh.

One reason Simmons is praised so thoroughly is the character matches the performance. The Titans went to the playoffs in his first three seasons, and it has been all downhill in Nashville since. Tennessee is 7-34 since the beginning of the 2024 season and has seen constant upheaval throughout the organization.

But who ever heard Simmons grumble?

“The Bears are getting a true alpha and a leader,” one veteran scout texted Thursday, unprompted. “Jeff empties the tank on Sundays. Dominant run defender and a really good pass rusher. A must-account-for guy at all times. Game wrecker who loves the game.”

Sounds like the kind of guys the Bears have been trying to collect in their locker room, and that’s not just a footnote because if you’re going to part with massive draft capital to add a player who will be one of the highest-paid on the roster, he better match your standards.

The only other defensive players acquired via an in-season trade for two first-round picks are cornerbacks Jalen Ramsey (2019 Los Angeles Rams) and Sauce Gardner (2025 Indianapolis Colts). Ramsey helped the Rams win a Super Bowl in his third year. The Gardner trade is trending toward being a loss for the Colts.

The Bears traded two first-round picks to acquire edge rusher Khalil Mack a week before the 2018 opener. It looked like a masterstroke that season as the Bears dominated defensively. Mack was really good the next two seasons — maybe a tick shy of great — and then dealt with injuries in 2021.

If the measuring stick for success with the Simmons move is Super Bowl or bust, that’s not unfair. Poles and the Bears appear well-positioned to take multiple swings at it with coach Ben Johnson, quarterback Caleb Williams and a strong offensive cast.

That’s why Poles could feel comfortable with this move. He has Simmons for 10 games this season, beginning Monday night in Seattle, and Simmons is under contract for four more seasons through 2030. Defensive tackles tend to age well, so it’s reasonable to believe he can deliver elite play in 2027, when he’ll be 30, and again in 2028.

Anything beyond that is gravy. The Bears are very much focused on the present.

Unlike Mack, whom the Bears had to pay after acquiring him from the Oakland Raiders, the Titans already did the contract for Simmons, signing him to an extension in June that included a $20 million signing bonus. That contributed to the high draft-pick compensation the Bears had to pay.

Bears defensive coordinator Dennis Allen works on the sideline in the second quarter against the Patriots on Oct. 22, 2026, at Soldier Field. (John J. Kim/Chicago Tribune)

Allen’s defense now has a legitimate difference maker, and you have to give him credit for making it work to this point. Whether it’s a case in which the whole is greater than the sum of the parts or it’s smoke and mirrors — or maybe a little bit of both — Simmons now changes the calculus. Opposing offenses will highlight him every week.

“Well, two hours of anxiety last night is gone,” center Ted Karras told Cincinnati media Thursday, three days before the Bengals’ game versus the Titans.

Bears offensive coordinator Press Taylor was in his office Thursday morning, deep in preparation for a talented Seahawks defense, when his brother, Bengals coach Zac Taylor, FaceTimed him.

“Thank you,” Zac Taylor said with a grin spread across his face.

Press Taylor wasn’t positive what was up and then found out the Bears indeed had landed Simmons.

The Bears have held up pretty well in some basic defensive metrics. They’re eighth in total defense (313.4 yards per game), third versus the run (87.6) and 18th against the pass (225.9). But all of that is partially a product of the offense’s ability to play keep-away with a league-high average time of possession of 35 minutes, 30 seconds. The next-closest offense is three minutes behind the Bears.

The Bears defense ranks 32nd in yards per play (6.25) and yards per pass (8.15) and 23rd in yards per rush (4.44) — evidence that the more it’s on the field, the worse things will be.

Adding Simmons to the middle of the defensive line affects everything. It will create one-on-one opportunities for teammates, including Sweat, with whom Simmons played at Mississippi State. Allen won’t have to lean on the blitz so much to create pressure. And Simmons is probably a better run defender than he is a pass rusher — and he’s elite at that compared with other interior players.

Poles’ biggest move since trading the No. 1 pick in 2023 to the Carolina Panthers — a deal that led to the pick used to select Williams — comes at the right time. That’s because rivals in the rugged NFC North have help arriving too.

Green Bay Packers edge rusher Micah Parsons reportedly will return to practice next week, meaning his comeback from a torn ACL is nearly complete. The Detroit Lions were hurting in the secondary, but versatile safety Brian Branch is back and fellow safety Kerby Joseph is practicing.

The Vikings, meanwhile, have the No. 1 defense in the NFL, so the Bears had to do something to improve their chances of playing true complementary football as the playoff race heats up.

The Bears believe with Simmons they have a chance to make this deal a winner for them multiple times over, meaning this year and in seasons to come. They didn’t win a playoff game in four seasons with Mack, but they have a better quarterback now and Johnson has proved to be elite as a play designer and caller.

Simmons will be a legitimate multiplier for the defense. The Bears just need those equations to extend deep into the postseason.

By the way, the last time Simmons was in the playoffs after the 2021 season, he had three sacks in a loss to the eventual AFC champion Bengals.

NASHVILLE — Matt Robertson just became $1.25 million richer. But don't expect the Kentucky bass fishing pro to pack up and move into a mansion anytime soon.

The Bassmaster Elite pro backed up a week's worth of trash talk by bringing 20 pounds, 2 ounces of bass to the scales on Championship Saturday, claiming the largest first-place prize in professional bass fishing history.

And despite all that confidence, even Robertson seemed a little overwhelmed by the moment.

"Dude, it's freaking insane. I don't think it's really hit me yet," he said.

Robertson, of Kuttawa, Kentucky, dominated the first two days of competition, then delivered another monster bag Saturday after the weights reset to zero. Pake South finished second with 13 pounds, 7 ounces, taking home $500,000, while Wesley Strader earned $300,000 for third place.

The historic tournament brought together top anglers from the Bassmaster Elite Series and Major League Fishing's Bass Pro Tour, along with two amateur qualifiers, to compete for a record $3.25 million purse.

But for Robertson, the payday is about much more than the money.

A millionaire who's staying in his trailer

Robertson might not be upgrading his own home, but he already knows where some of that $1.25 million is going.

His wife is getting a new vehicle. And he's planning to buy a couple more trailers.

Before the final weigh-in, Robertson told OutKick OutDoors that he'd been eyeing two trailers for sale near his home, one priced around $60,000 and the other around $80,000.

"I'm for sure buying a couple more trailers," he said. "They're bought, and my wife, she gets her new ride."

MATT ROBERTSON HAULS IN 20-POUND BAG TO LEAD THE CHAMPIONS AFTER DAY 1 ON OLD HICKORY LAKE

And after securing the championship, he doubled down on those plans.

"I hate to tell anybody, but them things are sold," he joked.

Behind Robertson's larger-than-life personality, however, is a man who hasn't forgotten where he came from.

"I didn't grow up with much," he explained during his post-victory press conference. "I worked so much paycheck to paycheck ... I went through real struggles in life."

Those experiences, he said, helped prepare him for the pressure of competing for life-changing money.

Robertson admitted that the money will drastically change his life, but he insisted he wasn't particularly nervous about the stakes.

In fact, when OutKick OutDoors asked him what winning would mean before the results were officially announced, he had something else on his mind.

"I want that damn trophy. It's what I want," he said. "Everybody talking about the 1.25. Like, I want the money too, but I want to get my hands on that trophy."

A honey hole, a navigation lock and an unlikely friendship

While Robertson's victory might have looked easy from the leaderboard, it took some unconventional strategy to get there.

Rather than fishing the main lake, he traveled through the navigation lock at Old Hickory Dam to target smallmouth bass in the tailwater below the dam.

Robertson discovered his productive fishing area during practice and made the gamble that the bigger fish would be there when it mattered.

"If I hadn't found that spot, I wouldn't have won this thing," Robertson said.

The strategy certainly paid off. Robertson caught the tournament's only 20-pound bag on Thursday, then added another strong day Friday to secure the top qualifying position for Saturday's championship.

But his honey hole wasn't exactly a secret.

MLF pro Ron Nelson had also discovered productive water below the dam, and the two anglers spent much of the tournament fishing in close proximity.

The two were among just a handful of competitors who ventured below the dam. In fact, Nelson deliberately avoided casting into the area where Robertson had been catching his fish, despite the enormous money at stake.

And Robertson noticed.

"Never knew Ron before this week. Got to know him real well, and dude, there is not another person in the world with as much integrity as him," Robertson told OutKick OutDoors during Saturday's weigh-in.

"Anybody else fishing for this kind of money, he saw where I was catching a lot of fish, and he never came in on me."

As water levels and currents shifted, the two eventually ended up sharing the productive area.

But tournament rules prohibited them from discussing how many fish they had caught, creating an unusual dilemma.

Robertson said he had no idea Nelson was struggling to fill his limit and felt terrible afterward.

"If I'd knew Ron only had two ... I wouldn't even have pulled up down there," he said. "I feel awful about it."

Robertson had been hoping they could both walk away with enormous paydays.

"I was wanting us to win $1.75 million from down there," he said.

Nelson, who ultimately finished sixth and earned $75,000, had equally kind words for his new friend.

"Matt's a heck of a dude," Nelson told OutKick OutDoors. "I don't know him, but I got a new friend now, I guess."

Nelson explained that changing currents had made it difficult to relocate the school of smallmouth he'd found the previous day. And he believed Robertson would have given him more room had he known how badly Nelson needed a few additional bites.

"If he knows I'd needed a couple bites, he'd have probably slid over," Nelson said.

The mutual respect was especially fitting for a tournament that brought together competitors from rival professional fishing circuits.

Robertson and Nelson had never met before The Champions. Three days later, they'd walked away with substantial paychecks and a new friendship.

'The Super Bowl of bass fishing'

Robertson was also quick to credit his faith and a little help from the lock operators for his historic victory.

He recalled learning that the navigation lock was undergoing maintenance early in the tournament, threatening to keep him from reaching the area where he'd found his fish.

Knowing how much his strategy depended on getting through, he said he prayed for the lock to reopen.

When he checked his phone, he discovered the lockmaster had already tried calling him twice. The lock was open, and the operators were waiting for him.

"From that moment, I was stressing before that," Robertson recalled. "And after that, I just ... gave it all to Him and let it go."

Once on the water, Robertson relied on his familiarity with the Tennessee River and its navigation locks to adjust to changing conditions. He caught his winning fish using a small swimbait on a jighead.

The victory capped a tournament that also saw Bassmaster's Team Stars defeat MLF's Team Stripes for the team championship, giving the inaugural event both individual and team bragging rights.

Robertson had nothing but praise for WBE and CEO Brian Bird, saying the organizers had paid attention to both the big details and the small ones.

"This is the Super Bowl of bass fishing. I don't care what anybody says," Robertson declared.

And while he can now claim the sport's richest championship prize, Robertson says he doesn't plan to change who he is.

A new vehicle for his wife? Absolutely. A couple more trailers? Already on the shopping list.

But moving out of his own? Not a chance.
