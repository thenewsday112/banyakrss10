John T. Shaw: Sen. Dick Durbin is not giving up on immigration reform

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

About two years into his presidency, John F. Kennedy was pressed by reporters to explain why much of his legislative program was stalled in Congress.

Kennedy, who had served six years in the House and eight in the Senate before entering the White House, explained, with more than a hint of frustration, that passing major legislation in Congress is extraordinarily difficult. He reminded his questioners that successful bills must clear both chambers in identical form, avoid or defeat a Senate filibuster, and then be signed into law by the president.

“It’s very easy to defeat a bill in the Congress,” Kennedy said tersely. “It’s much more difficult to pass one.”

Illinois U.S. Sen. Dick Durbin strongly agrees with Kennedy, a perspective that he has gained the hard way during his impressive 44-year congressional career.

Durbin recently told me that trying to pass consequential legislation almost inevitably triggers a “titanic struggle” and years of arduous effort.

Durbin’s quarter-century quest to pass comprehensive immigration reform and his narrower, but highly cherished, Dream Act has indeed been a titanic struggle in which the senator has not prevailed — at least not yet.

The Dream Act would allow immigrants brought to the U.S. illegally as children to eventually become citizens after meeting numerous requirements. Durbin first introduced the measure in 2001 and has offered it in every Congress since then.

As he prepares to leave the Senate early next year, Durbin is anguished that he has not been able to pass this legislation.

“The failure to pass the Dream Act is still pulling at me,” he said. “While I knew it was time to go, I still feel like I’m letting down the hundreds of thousands of Dreamers out there. But I will not forget them. I will keep talking about this issue as a concerned citizen, and I will do everything that I can to support this effort.”

Durbin draws inspiration from his mentor, Illinois U.S. Sen. Paul Douglas, who spent years in the legislative wilderness championing civil rights reform when many saw his efforts as futile. But Douglas never backed down. “Even if every battle was unsuccessful, constant but peaceful struggle would hasten the ultimate coming of needed reforms,” Douglas wrote in his memoir “In the Fullness of Time.”

Likewise, Durbin’s friend, U.S. Sen. Edward Kennedy of Massachusetts, worked relentlessly for decades on healthcare reform before it came to fruition. Sadly, he did not live to see the legislation become law. It was signed by President Barack Obama six months after Kennedy’s death.

Durbin has a long-game strategy on immigration reform that draws from these earlier struggles. He has absorbed their key lessons and adapted them to the challenges he faces on immigration.

First, he continues to keep the issue and imperative of immigration reform alive, frequently discussing it in speeches, at news conferences and in interviews. In his address to the Illinois General Assembly in May, the senator devoted the first half of his speech to immigration. “I’m proud to be the son of an immigrant,” he said, telling the story of his mother’s move to the U.S. from Lithuania when she was only 2 years old. He then shifted to promoting his bill. “I’m still committed to the Dream Act, and I hope you will join me in that commitment. It was a bipartisan measure, and it should be a bipartisan measure.”

Durbin has advocated for the act hundreds of times on the Senate floor over the years, highlighting the individual stories of more than 150 so-called Dreamers whose lives have enriched our nation.

He sees storytelling as essential to his quest. “President Lyndon B. Johnson had an assistant, Jack Valenti, who said that an effective communications strategy can be reduced to six words: ‘Let me tell you a story.’ When I tell the remarkable stories of these Dreamers on the Senate floor, I see the interns and staff sit up and pay attention.”

Second, Durbin has carefully sought out Republican co-sponsors for the Dream Act, beginning in 2001 with Sen. Orrin Hatch of Utah, then Lindsey Graham of South Carolina and now Lisa Murkowski of Alaska.

“This needs to be bipartisan,” he told me.

Former U.S. Senate Historian Betty Koed told me this about Durbin: “He has truly become a ‘Senate workhorse.’ On matters of immigration reform, he has developed bipartisan alliances and has built a foundation for legislative action. When the will to act surfaces, there will be legislative language to be used.”

Third, Durbin has encouraged the rising generation of Capitol Hill lawmakers to concentrate on the issue after he departs next year. Sens. Alex Padilla of California, Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada and Chris Coons of Delaware, and Rep. Delia Ramirez of Illinois have agreed. “We will take support wherever we can get it. But I have consciously reached out to younger members of Congress who can pick up the mantle and work on this in their own way,” he said.

Fourth, Durbin has continued to champion broad immigration reform while also developing narrower bills to address specific aspects of this complex challenge. He constantly searches for opportunities for legislative success. This summer, he and New York Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand introduced a pair of immigration reform bills. One would provide a new and stronger layer of oversight to protect immigrant children in government custody. The other would decrease political influence in the immigration system by transforming the immigration court into an independent judiciary.

“One thing we learned from the civil rights legislative battle is that you should take whatever victory is at hand. Don’t miss an opportunity to make progress. Accept even partial success,” Durbin told me.

Finally, while frustrated by his years of struggle and especially the hurtful impact that this impasse has had on the lives of millions of Dreamers, Durbin projects confidence that this cause will prevail.

“I think that time is on the side of this issue because justice is on the side of this issue. We simply can’t give up,” he said.

John T. Shaw is a professor at Marquette University’s Les Aspin Center for Government in Washington, D.C. His most recent book is “The Education of a Statesman: How Global Leaders Can Repair a Fractured World.”

A stained glass window of the crucifixion was shattered in a church fire. Worshippers transformed the broken pieces into symbols of renewal.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Plumes of smoke billowed from the steep-pitched roof of a northern Illinois church one winter night after its organ caught fire during a practice session.

Everyone was evacuated from the property. No one was hurt in the Feb. 23 blaze at Christ United Methodist Church in Rockford, which firefighters quickly extinguished and determined to be accidental.

Yet the fire caused great damage to the sanctuary.

And to vent the thick smoke, firefighters had to break the window closest to the organ — a beloved stained glass rendering of the crucifixion of Christ, an image at the spiritual and emotional center of the faith.

The nearly 10-foot-tall image of Jesus suspended from the cross was shattered into hundreds of colored pieces, which scattered on the ground inside as well as outside the building.

“It was devastating,” recalled congregant Deb Laskey of Winnebago. “That was one of the first things we knew, that the window had been broken out.”

The fractured panes of glass couldn’t be mended or put back together.

But rather than dispose of the fragments, the congregation gave the remnants of the window a new purpose.

The night of the fire, a church volunteer gathered as many of the shards as he could.

“When I collected the glass, I felt a sense of urgency,” recalled Mark West, church trustee chair. “I had to get it picked up before it got thrown out.”

He took those broken pieces to a local glassblower, who refashioned them into more than a hundred small round pendants, to serve as keepsakes for members of the church.

Now each of these glass stones holds the memory of that depiction of the Passion of Christ.

Worshippers often wear the pieces of glass suspended on chains or chords around their necks at services, a collective symbol of resilience as they work to restore their church after the fire.

“It was part of Christ United Methodist and we all have a little piece of it with us,” said Laskey, who has her own blue pendant and gifted several others to her daughter and granddaughters.

The destruction of the stained glass window is reminiscent of the many ways that religious artwork and sacred items can be lost, marred or demolished amid catastrophe.

Fires, floods and other natural disasters have damaged many houses of worship in the Chicago area as well as across the country, jeopardizing the holy items they contain.

Frequent church closures also raise the predicament of how to salvage, preserve or recycle the sacred objects within.

And around the world, wars and religious persecution have destroyed prayer books, Bibles, Qurans, Torah scrolls, statues, iconography, shrines, relics and many other materials considered venerable.

Humanity has long grappled with the question of what to do with these vestiges of the holy.

Like the window in Rockford, sometimes sacred materials can be given new life or a transformed mission.

Amid widespread church closures and mergers, the Archdiocese of Chicago has strict protocols for the storage and reuse of sacred items after a building is deconsecrated, often transferring devotional items and artwork to other churches within the archdiocese to keep them in service.

Elsewhere in Chicago, themes of recreating and reviving spiritual artifacts and sacred materials permeate the work of a Jewish Iraqi American artist, whose grandparents fled Baghdad decades ago. His art, which has been displayed at museums around the world, often draws on his own family’s experience escaping antisemitism to evoke minority communities that have all but disappeared from their homelands.

And a local professor has spent years gathering used religious items for his collection titled “Secondhand Sacred,” which studies the circulation of vintage Christian materials acquired from flea markets, thrift stores, antique malls and estate sales, as well as online sites like eBay. The objects span denominations, offering insight into how their ownership, value and spiritual significance can shift over time.

Transformation

At the church in Rockford, dozens of congregants wore their glass pendants during a recent Sunday service, as they worshiped in a makeshift space while the main sanctuary continues to undergo restoration.

Sue Spitzer of Rockford had on her pendant made of green glass, which was smooth and glossy on top but laced with tiny white scratches on the bottom.

She frequently touches it, finding the texture on her fingertips to be a calming, almost meditative sensation.

“It went through something terrible,” she said. “And now it’s something beautiful.” Church services are held on Sept. 27, 2026, in a temporary space at Christ United Methodist Church in Rockford. The stained glass had to first endure more destruction before its metamorphosis.

The roughly quarter-century-old window was made of particularly thick panes of glass, so the cullets had to first be smashed into smaller slivers, recalled Laura Johnson, shop owner and artist at Starved Rock Hot Glassblowing and Ceramics Studio in Ottawa, Illinois.

Then Johnson needed to experiment with different temperatures to melt the material.

The glass was slowly heated to 1,475 degrees and then gradually cooled, a painstaking process to make it fusible while preventing cracking.

“That’s one of the greatest properties of glass,” she said. “That it can be constantly recycled and remelted to make something new.”

The artist took care to return to the church any stained glass window pieces that couldn’t be used rather than disposing of them.

She also makes memorial keepsakes, fusing a sprinkle of cremated remains into glass or ceramic pendants for grieving loved ones.

Johnson, who is Catholic, likened the reverent nature of the stained glass window project to crafting these memorial pieces; if there are any extra ashes that go unused, she sends them back to the bereaved for proper disposition.

So often, the reuse of religious items and materials stems from sorrowful circumstances.

Locally, hundreds of Catholic parishes in the Archdiocese of Chicago have closed over the last few decades.

Roughly 50 years ago, there were around 450 parishes across the Chicago area. That number dropped to 351 a decade ago.

Today, there are 216.

Before the building is sold or used for secular purposes, church officials take great care when removing the religious items inside, said Jacob Preciado, director of asset management for the archdiocese.

“We want to make sure the Catholic identity is not defaced in any way,” he said. “If there are other items we feel we can use at another church, that is our main focus and our priority.”

The process can be painstaking and take months, Preciado said.

“We don’t go in to remove the items immediately, because the community is still in mourning. So we have to respect that,” he said.

For many Catholics, life revolved around their local parish, he added. The neighborhood church often anchored the transition to a new country for generations of immigrants from Poland, Mexico, Italy, the Philippines, Germany, Ireland and other nations.

“That’s their grandparents and their parents and all the baptisms and weddings and funerals they attended in that church,” Preciado said. “That all comes flooding back … We understand the pain that communities are going through.”

Sacred materials that can’t immediately be relocated to other churches are kept in a secure storage facility on the West Side, which currently houses hundreds of religious pieces. Stained glass windows. Candlesticks. Altars. Tabernacles. Kneelers. Statues. Murals.

There’s a formal process for requesting items from shuttered parishes or those kept at the storage site, Preciado said. Priority is given to churches within the archdiocese, but his office does get inquiries from Catholic churches nationwide.

Relocation can be challenging at times. Some statues are over a century old. Various items are quite large, like altars that might need to be taken apart and put back together in a process akin to an intricate jigsaw puzzle.

One of the most dramatic local examples of reuse is St. Raphael the Archangel Catholic Church in northern Lake County, which opened in 2014 and includes recycled parts of multiple closed churches.

The entire gray Indiana limestone facade of the now-defunct St. John of God Church in the Back of the Yards neighborhood was meticulously salvaged, transported about 60 miles north and rebuilt as St. Raphael’s exterior.

Designed by architect Henry Schlacks and erected more than a century ago, the Renaissance-style structure includes a front balcony, bell towers, sweeping archways and 20 wooden doors, each 11 feet tall.

Much of the interior of St. Raphael — including marble altars, pews and Tyrolean stained glass imported from Austria — came from St. Peter Canisius Catholic Church in the Austin neighborhood, which closed about two decades ago.

Bells and other features incorporated at St. Raphael were also brought from several closed or merged parishes.

During homecoming and prom seasons, droves of local high school students — Catholic and non-Catholic alike — flock to take formal photos in front of the ornate exterior, said the Rev. Matthew Kowalski, the church’s pastor.

Parents often have their babies baptized at St. Raphael and couples frequently ask to be married there because of historical family ties to either St. John of God or St. Peter Canisius, he said.

A group of elderly former parishioners of St. John of God would occasionally travel to worship at St. Raphael, though it’s been a while since they’ve visited, Kowalski recalled.

They were “trying to keep the memory of their closed church alive,” the priest added.

‘A second life’

As the pastor watched smoke emanating from his church, he wondered how Christ United Methodist would continue to hold services, especially during the busy season of Lent.

The Rev. Caleb Hong was standing outside the building the night of the blaze, the fire trucks still on the scene, when he received a text.

It was the rabbi from Temple Beth-El, the synagogue down the street.

“She offered her condolences and right away asked, ‘do you need a place to worship?'” he recounted.

Christ United Methodist held services at Temple Beth-El through early April, celebrating Easter there before returning to modified spaces in their own building the following week.

In August, the synagogue and church held an outdoor worship service and picnic together on the grounds of Christ United Methodist Church.

The rabbi gave the sermon.

“We formed a bond with them that didn’t exist before,” Rabbi Binah Wing said in a phone interview with the Tribune.

While the fire was horrible, Hong said some blessings have emerged from the tragedy.

New ecumenical connections with a neighboring house of worship.

A sense of communal renewal through renovating the sanctuary.

Revitalizing the stained glass window in an alternate form.

“Don’t waste a crisis. Because there’s always something good that can come out of it,” he said after church on a recent Sunday. “If there is a fire, use it as fertilizer.”

But there are times when religious artwork and artifacts cannot be recovered, salvaged or repaired.

Much of Jewish Iraqi American conceptual artist Michael Rakowitz’s work focuses on re-creating or giving new life to religious materials that were stolen, damaged or destroyed amid war and persecution.

About two decades ago, the Northwestern University art professor began an ongoing project to reconstruct more than 8,000 items looted from the National Museum of Iraq after the 2003 U.S. invasion, as well as archaeological sites later decimated by the Islamic State group.

Artist Michael Rakowitz prepares his exhibition "Backstroke of the West" at the Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago in 2017. (Terrence Antonio James/Chicago Tribune)

The series, titled “The Invisible Enemy Should Not Exist,” includes many ancient Sumerian and Assyrian votive statues with clasped hands, which were once placed in shrines to serve as proxies in constant prayer to the gods.

Rakowitz re-creates these antiquities using a papier-mâché made from Middle Eastern food packaging, labels and wrappers used by diaspora Iraqi communities. In this way, the replicas are imbued with the modern events tied to their materials.

“They’re made from the packaging of food that’s made at communal dinners and festivals of communities that continue to survive and still practice their rituals and still engage with joy, whether it’s food that’s cooked for weddings or Rosh Hashanah or iftar during Ramadan,” Rakowitz said. “It relates directly to the life that’s not only threatened along the archaeological sites and museums, but also the resilience and refusal to disappear.”

So far, he and his studio have crafted thousands of items, some housed at the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures at the University of Chicago, as well as various other museums around the world.

For years, Rakowitz — whose maternal grandparents fled Baghdad in 1941 — had gathered pieces of centuries-old torahs, damaged Jewish prayer books and spiritual texts that hailed from Iraq.

In 2013, he turned that personal archive into a project called “The Geniza at Ostia.”

According to Jewish tradition, worn out sacred texts can’t be casually discarded; instead, they are stored in a geniza, a repository usually located within a synagogue or buried at a Jewish cemetery.

He interred his collection inside a clay amphora, which was buried at the Ostia Synagogue, an ancient archaeological site in Ostia Antica, Rome.

An Iraqi date palm — a Mesopotamian symbol of life and prosperity — was planted on top of it, so the roots of the growing plant could draw nutrients from the pages of those holy writings as they decay.

“The text has a second life as a kind of compost for this tree,” Rakowitz said.

Renewal through art

For months, the destroyed stained glass window — one of nine illustrating various scenes from the life of Christ at the church in Rockford — remained boarded up.

The sight saddened many congregants.

“We were missing something,” Laskey recalled. “Something had been taken from us.”

But in late September, a new stained glass window of Christ’s crucifixion was finally installed in the frame.

Many church members viewed it for the first time after the Sept. 27 service. A replacement stained glass window, right, shown Sept. 27, 2026, was installed to replace one destroyed by a fire at Christ United Methodist Church in Rockford back in February. (Ryan Rayburn/for the Chicago Tribune)

As rainbow-hued sunlight streamed through the translucent glass, some congregants marveled that they couldn’t really tell the difference between the replacement window and the original.

The sanctuary was still far from restored.

The carpeting remained torn. Bolts were coming up from the floorboards.

A wooden choir pew was badly singed on the side closest to where the fire started.

Kathy Wight of Rockford said the blackened portion was where she used to sit; all of their choir notebooks and hymnals kept underneath the pews had been reduced to ash, she added.

At first, Wight had hesitated to get a pendant.

“Because I didn’t want to be reminded of the fire,” she said.

But she ultimately decided to wear one, and soon found the necklace to be consoling.

All of the stained glass windows in the sanctuary, and the New Testament events they retell, are important to her.

Yet as she gazed at the newly replaced window for the first time, Wight explained why the crucifixion was particularly poignant in the life of Christ.

“It’s the most important, most moving thing in the Christian faith,” she said. “It’s renewal. It’s that hope. It’s … the hope in his resurrection. And now, the resurrection of our church, too.”

Some faiths have rules or procedures guiding the proper disposition of religious items and blessed objects that can no longer be used.

Often this involves methods of returning their elements to the earth, such as ritual burning, burial or pouring liquids like holy water into the soil.

Yet for many Christian denominations, there’s no such formal guidance, said James Bielo, associate professor of religious studies at Northwestern University.

The items then fall into a theological gray area of sorts, he said.

“So they are ambiguous. They are still recognized as sacred,” said Bielo, author of multiple books including “Anthropology of Religion: The Basics” and “Materializing the Bible: Scripture, Sensation, Place.” “But they are also recognized as kind of spent, used up. Unable to throw them away but still unable to be used.”

A massive number of church closures across Christian faiths, along with an aging Boomer population, has led to an excess of retired religious objects around the country, he said.

He’s found that the solution, in many cases, is retail donation.

“There’s that sentiment that I shouldn’t just throw this away,” he said. “Some people donate to a thrift store as a way to keep something in circulation, for example, as a way to keep a Bible in the world and available for reading. They would rather donate it and have its potential continue through the context of a thrift store.”

Since 2020, Bielo has scoured estate sales, flea markets, thrift stores, antique malls and online shops searching for pre-owned Christian religious items for his project “Secondhand Sacred,” which studies the circulation of these materials.

His collection includes many serious items that might be blessed or venerated. Statues of the Virgin Mary. Wall crucifixes. Icons of saints. Aging Bibles.

There are also more playful, pop-culture pieces.

A cassette of rapper M.C. Hammer’s 1990 Gospel-infused hit song “Pray.”

A View-Master — a vintage handheld stereoscopic toy — that shows scenes from the Holy Land.

“Because View-Masters are a type of virtual pilgrimage,” Bielo said.

There’s also a packet of grow capsules, small pill-shaped novelties that, when immersed in warm water, emerge as colored sponges comprising a Nativity scene.

“I want to say, as an anthropologist and as a religious studies scholar, they are sacred because they are part of this sacred network that is someone’s devotional life,” Bielo said.

Through this work, he’s encountered folks who are also searching for similar objects.

One was a woman from Chicago, “who really saw herself as a rescuer of these items,” and would give them away or just charge the cost of shipping; another was a woman he met at a thrift store, who mentioned she was purchasing religious materials to keep them out of the hands of “the occult.”

A separate collection of Bielo’s titled “Materializing the Bible,” a project on biblical tourism sites, is housed at the Newberry Library in the Gold Coast neighborhood.

An exhibit of “Secondhand Sacred” is on display this semester at the University of Georgia; last year, it was at the Martin Marty Center at the University of Chicago Divinity School.

But these were short term.

Bielo longs to eventually find a permanent place for “Secondhand Sacred” at a museum or other educational institution, noting the divine yet simultaneously temporal nature of its components.

He knows he won’t be able to store and keep these items forever. The same societal challenge that spurred his research looms as an existential threat to the collection of devotional items he has accumulated.

“It’s a temporary kind of archive,” Bielo added. “I am able to protect and care for it, in a way. But a long-term home is what it needs.”

Those initial, flirty conversations are great, don't get us wrong, but most of us aren't jumping onto Tinder or Hinge just to endlessly text. And yet, in modern dating, where both people often feel afraid to make the first move and take the match into the real world, endless texting is all too often the only result of a once-promising match. 

But it doesn't have to be this way. Dating app Pure concocted some clever solutions to the never-ending chatting, and in our own experimentation, they work like a charm. 

Here's how Pure introduced a sense of urgency into its dating mechanics, and how that helps users translate matches into real-life encounters. 

Opens in a new window Credit: Pure Pure   Learn More

How Pure leverages a sense of urgency to benefit its users

One unique feature of Pure is that it doesn't use traditional dating profiles, which means its matches operate differently than on basically every other dating app. 

Instead of having a permanent, public-facing profile, Pure users can post "ads," temporary personal advertisements shouting out who they are or what they want, whether that's for a long-term relationship or a short-term connection. It's only when someone has posted an ad that they're discoverable to other users, and only then are your basic profile elements (age, gender, sexual orientation, and photos) visible. Once your ad expires, so does access to your profile.

The result of this mechanic is a sense of urgency, the very real possibility that you're seeing someone you may never see again, so if you want a connection, you have to act fast. That's a very welcome change from the dating app norm, where profiles are always visible, and you can browse them at your convenience, whether on your morning commute or between sets at the gym.

But Pure takes things a step further with how you actually interact with other people. 

The disappearing chat mechanic

From the moment you initiate a chat on Pure, you're under an artificial time constraint, since every chat automatically self-deletes after 24 hours, unless both parties agree to suspend the chat timer. 

While at first this seems like an unnecessary constraint, in practice it's actually very healthy and helps daters avoid the endless banter that plagues so many online dating interactions. 

Think about what would happen if you approached a cute stranger in the real world. You may see them at a bar or coffee shop, going about their day, but no matter how smooth your introduction, you know in the back of your mind that you can't monopolize this person's time and attention for very long. 

For an in-person approach, you have to be concise in your communication: I think you're cute and want a chance to get to know you better — would that interest you? But on an app, where there's no sense of urgency, you can endlessly waffle, delaying the most important question of all in favor of a never-ending series of mundane questions. 

This is why Pure's disappearing chat mechanic is so clever, even when both parties agree to disable it, since even that act forces a mutual acknowledgment of the connection. And after using the app over an extended period, we can confirm that Pure's culture really encourages in-person interactions at the expense of endless chatting or swiping.

Working with outside collaborators can add more work for a repository owner. You need to confirm who still needs access, change permissions as projects evolve, and archive repositories once they’ve served their purpose.

To keep up, repository owners need to track collaborators, deadlines, and follow-ups for each repository. As a project grows, that manual housekeeping becomes harder to keep up with and easier to defer.

We faced this problem in a shared organization where GitHub employees (“Hubbers”) collaborate with customers and partners on product trials and professional services engagements. Our answer was Evergreen, a GitHub App built with native GitHub features. The workflows below exhibit a pattern you can adapt for your own organization.

Meet Evergreen

Evergreen is a GitHub App that automates organization hygiene through two workflows:

  1. Outside collaborator access reviews. Every quarter, each repo’s outside collaborators are reviewed. Keep who you need, and everyone else is removed.
  2. Stale repository detection and archival. Repos with no human activity past a threshold get flagged, warned, and (if still untouched) archived.

Two design principles shaped everything:

Fail secure. If nobody responds to an access review, we don’t leave access in place and hope for the best. We revoke it. The safe outcome is the default outcome.

Never destroy anything. Evergreen will never delete a repository or its contents. Archived repos are recoverable, and every access change is written into the repo’s tracking issue, so reverting a mistake is as easy as a copy-paste to Copilot.

How it works: One brain, many hands

Under the hood, Evergreen is a small Go webhook server running in a container. But the interesting part is the choreography. Every moving piece is a stock GitHub feature: a GitHub App, scheduled actions, webhooks, issues, custom properties, and plain files in a repo. Nothing exotic to reinvent.

A controller that keeps time

A dedicated controller repo acts as Evergreen’s clock and its memory. Four scheduled GitHub Actions workflows fire repository_dispatch events on a quarterly cadence, and the app wakes up and does the work:

Reviews open two weeks before their cleanup runs, and there’s a hard grace period baked in so a stray manual run can never fast‑forward the guillotine. Each workflow also supports workflow_dispatch, so we can trigger any step by hand as needed.

State you can git blame

Evergreen tracks every repo’s status as a JSON file (one per repository) committed to the controller repo under state/repos/<repo_id>.json. The snapshot of who had access, when the review opened, the deadline, who decided, and what happened all lives there. Here’s a completed review where a Hubber kept Alice’s access and let Bob’s lapse:

The best part: because state is just files in git, the commit history is the audit trail. Want to know why someone lost access last spring? git log the state file. There’s no separate database or bespoke audit pipeline to track.

The interaction model is delightfully simple: check the boxes for who stays, then close the issue. That’s it. When the issue closes, Evergreen listens for the issues.closed webhook event, reads the checkboxes, retains the checked collaborators, and revokes the rest, then posts a receipt comment listing exactly what it kept and what it removed.

Only the right humans can pull the lever

Closing an access review issue is a privileged action, so Evergreen guards it. If someone who isn’t a Hubber closes an issue, the app quietly reopens it and posts a note explaining why. Decisions only count when they come from the people authorized to make them.

Informing the right people

Here’s a subtle problem: we know the people who need to respond to a review are Hubbers, but we didn’t always know which Hubber(s) owned a given repository.

Evergreen resolves the right people to notify through a fallback chain:

  1. If a repo sets an evergreen_repo_owner custom property (a @user or @org/team), use it.
  2. Otherwise, look at recent issue and commit authors and cc the ones who are on the access review team, i.e., the Hubbers actually working in that repo.

The result: the right owners get pinged automatically, without anyone maintaining a spreadsheet of “who owns what.”

Fail secure, and fail closed

When a review deadline passes with no response, cleanup revokes every collaborator in the snapshot. We were careful about a subtle edge case: what if some removals succeed while others hit a transient API error? Marking the review “done” while a removal is still outstanding would be a quiet inconsistency, and we didn’t want that.

So Evergreen fails closed. If any removal fails, the review stays pending and the issue stays open, and the next run retries the stragglers. It only records “revoked” once every removal actually succeeded. And whatever gets removed is written into the issue, so restoring access is a matter of re‑adding the handles it listed.

The user experience: automation that feels considerate

Automated housekeeping has a reputation for being the office smoke alarm: loud, easy to ignore, and usually going off while you’re cooking something perfectly fine. We worked hard to make Evergreen feel like the opposite:

  • It comes to you. The work happens as an issue in your repo.
  • It tags the right people. No “who owns this?” archaeology.
  • It’s crystal clear about the ask. Every issue states what to do, the exact deadline, and precisely what happens if you ignore it.
  • It nudges before it acts. A reminder comment lands before the deadline: no surprise revocations.
  • It has an escape hatch. Org owners (Hubbers) can opt out by setting a custom property (evergreen_exempt_from_access_review or evergreen_exempt_from_archive).

Rolling it out carefully

Automation that manages access and archives repositories deserves a careful rollout, especially into a large, active org (ours has 1,400+ Hubbers, and many more customers and partners). We wanted the very first run to be a complete non‑event. A few practices got us there:

Dry‑run first. Evergreen ships with a DRY_RUN mode that logs every write it would make without touching a single collaborator or repo. We ran it against the real org and read the logs like tea leaves until we trusted the decisions it wanted to make.

Overcommunicate. Before flipping the switch, we broadcast what was coming, when, and what people needed to do, so the first issue anyone saw wasn’t a surprise. We found that when you’re telling a large group the same thing for the seventh time, it’s often when many are still hearing it for the first time.

Watch everything with Splunk. Evergreen emits structured logfmt logs to a dedicated Splunk index. That observability is how we caught edge cases in the wild (empty collaborator lists, unusual permissions, repos with issues disabled) and tightened the logic before they mattered.

Respect the platform’s speed limits. When you open issues and post comments across every repo in a big org, you slam into GitHub’s secondary rate limits for content creation: 80 requests/minute and 500/hour. While this is a thoughtful preventative control to avoid system abuse, it requires a bit of intention when automating IssueOps at large scale.

So Evergreen paces itself by construction. It processes repos in batches, spaces out each content‑creating call by a couple of seconds, and sleeps between batches to let the rolling window clear, and it only counts the repos that actually create content, so a quiet quarter where nothing’s eligible still runs fast. We opted for proactive avoidance over reactive back‑off.

Lessons we’re taking with us

  • Make the safe path the default path. Fail secure, then make responding easy enough that failing secure rarely triggers.
  • Use the primitives you already have. Issues, webhooks, custom properties, and git history did the work of a bespoke UI, a job queue, and an audit database.
  • Meet people where they work. An issue in your repo beats a dashboard you’ll never open.
  • Logs are a revert button. Writing every change into the issue turned “oops” into “paste these three handles into a Copilot session.”
  • Respect the limits. At org scale, rate limits aren’t an edge case: they’re the design constraint.

Keep your org evergreen

Routine hygiene is the kind of work that’s easy to defer and perfect to automate. The trick isn’t heroic cleanup sprints: it’s making the boring, safe thing happen on a schedule, with a friendly face and an undo button.

That’s Evergreen. And because it’s built entirely from native GitHub features, you can stand up the same pattern yourself.

Maintainers look at star history to answer a practical question: did a launch, release, or event change a project’s trajectory? To reconstruct that curve from the GitHub API, tools had to request pages of stargazer records (each person who currently stars the repository and when they starred it), keep the timestamps, and throw the identities away.

Over time, that design created problems at both ends. Identities were available for bulk collection, so bad actors started scraping public stargazer lists for spam and phishing campaigns. For highly starred repositories, the endpoint could also make the database process many thousands of entries to return a single page.

The fix seemed clear: return counts instead of people. But returning less data didn’t actually make the query any cheaper.

Now maintainers have a direct way to get the curve: we built an endpoint that returns up to 30 weeks of daily star counts in a single request, with no identities attached. Keeping that cheap to serve meant reducing how much data the database processed, not just how much it returned, and the endpoint does that by filtering stars by time before it counts anything.

What maintainers need from star history

We first addressed the immediate risks to developers and to our database by restricting stargazer and watcher lists to repository administrators and collaborators. After the access change, callers without the required permission could receive an empty response or a 403 Forbidden response.

That change also cut off tools that had used the old response to calculate star history, often without displaying or otherwise using the usernames it returned. So we talked with maintainers and tool builders to understand what they still needed. They used star history as an approximate signal of interest: to compare activity before and after a release, or to see whether an announcement reached new developers. What they cared about was the curve.

The old API response had made identity look like part of that contract because every timestamp arrived attached to a user. In practice, tools such as Star History needed counts and dates, not usernames or other user data. Once identity was no longer part of the requirement, we could design an aggregate endpoint for the actual job: retrieving changes in star counts over time at a useful resolution.

When the API change disrupted Star History, users rallied around the project. GitHub’s Community team listened and connected us with product engineering, who dug into our use case and worked alongside us to design a purpose-built endpoint. A difficult situation became a constructive collaboration between GitHub, Star History, and the broader community—ultimately leading to a better solution.

Tianzhou Chen, star-history.com maintainer and Bytebase co-founder

Why the original endpoint was expensive

Once we knew what maintainers needed, the next step was understanding why the original design couldn’t provide it cheaply. The original GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stargazers endpoint was built to answer a simple question: who starred this repository? It returned a paginated list of people, and if the request specified a custom media type, each entry also included when the star was created.

The star model was built for the same job. It records the current set of people who have starred a repository, which keeps showing a star count or a list of stargazers simple. It was not designed as a historical event log: when someone removes a star, that record is removed. When tools began using the list as a time series, reconstructing a growth curve meant retrieving individual identities and processing every current star. Because each page held at most 100 people, the work grew with the repository’s popularity: building the full curve for react/react, with about 251,000 stars, took about 2,511 requests, so tools often ran into rate limits or sampled pages instead of reading them all.

Serving a page required the database to query the repository’s current stars. Due to a bug in the pagination implementation, every request also counted all of those stars to work out how many pages existed, even though the caller received only one page of up to 100 people. On a highly starred repository, that meant counting many thousands of entries on every request. In the week before we restricted access, nearly 37,000 of these count queries ran slowly enough to be logged, averaging nearly 3 seconds each, and database safeguards killed more than 1,000 of them, which contributed to request timeouts.

The chart below shows what all that counting cost. After we restricted access, the database served about 60% fewer stargazer requests, and the total time it spent on the count query fell by about 80%.

Approaches we considered

Removing identities handled the privacy requirement. The harder part was keeping each request from scanning the repository’s entire current star set.

Keeping events without identities

Our first spike kept one record per star and returned the starred_at value without the stargazer’s identity for callers who lacked collaborator access. At first, this looked like the quickest path forward, because it directly served the request we had heard from tool builders. It also closed the identity-harvesting path with a relatively small change to the existing serialization and authorization flow.

This approach addressed identity exposure and would have allowed existing tools to keep working. But API consumers would still have to paginate through and process potentially many thousands of individual records to build an aggregate history. It also tied the new use case to an endpoint and pagination model designed for listing people rather than retrieving trends.

Aggregating, then paginating

Aggregation seemed like the obvious next step. Instead of returning one row per star, the query could group stars into daily or weekly buckets, sort those buckets, and paginate the much smaller result.

The problem was where pagination happened. In a straightforward SQL query, the database first finds all stars for the repository, groups them into buckets, and sorts those buckets. Only then does it apply LIMIT, which controls how many buckets are returned, and OFFSET, which determines where the page begins:

all matching stars -> bucket every row by day or week -> sort every bucket -> OFFSET/LIMIT -> response

For a popular repository, an anonymous request could still scan and aggregate many thousands of index entries to return a handful of buckets. Returning fewer buckets would shrink the JSON response, but it would not reduce the expensive grouping and sorting the database had already performed. For react/react, reading the full history that way would have meant examining roughly 6 million rows, because every one of the 24 requests would have reprocessed all of its stars. To reduce the query cost, we had to narrow the rows before aggregation began.

Filtering by time before grouping

The design we shipped paginates star history by calendar week. For each request, it translates page and per_page into an indexed created_at range, filters the stars to those weeks, and then groups the matching records into daily counts. A response includes up to 30 weeks, and increasing page moves backward through the repository’s history.

indexed date range -> group rows in that range -> daily buckets -> response

With the date range applied first, each request processes the weeks being returned instead of every current star in the repository. For tools, that means one request now covers up to 30 weeks of history, no matter how many stars the repository has. For react/react, that’s about 24 requests instead of about 2,511, and the database examines each star about once (roughly 251,000 rows) across the full history. This fits the primary use case because maintainers usually inspect growth across weeks or around a known release, launch, or event. Week-based ranges also align with other repository activity APIs: the response follows the same shape as the weekly commit activity endpoint, GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stats/commit_activity.

The time-range design limits each request to a defined period, but it does not limit the number of stars within that range. A week with an unusually large number of stars can therefore still produce a slow or expensive query. The normal request path no longer queries every current star, but exceptionally dense weeks remain a scaling limitation.

Comparing the performance of the old and new API paths also surfaced other places where GitHub was counting stars on demand. We found the same pattern on the repository stargazers page on GitHub.com and updated it to use the repository’s cached star count. Slow queries from that page, which had numbered in the hundreds each week, dropped to zero. We recently shipped a similar fix to other stargazer API endpoints.

The chart below shows slow and killed stargazer count queries each week, by where they came from.

What we learned

  1. A smaller response does not always mean a cheaper query. LIMIT and OFFSET reduced the number of results returned, but they did not reduce the grouping and sorting the database had already done. We had to look at the order in which the work happened.
  2. Start with what people are actually trying to do. Maintainers needed to compare trends. A list of identities was simply how the old endpoint happened to provide the underlying data.
  3. Return only what the use case needs. Anything more is data someone else can misuse. Once we understood the job, we could remove individual identities without taking away the information maintainers relied on.
  4. Be clear about the tradeoffs that remain. The new endpoint is safer and more predictable, but calendar boundaries, removed stars, and unusually dense weeks still affect what it can return. Those details are part of the API contract too.

Restricting the original endpoint addressed the immediate problem. Listening to maintainers gave us something better than what we started with: star history that’s easier to get, cheaper to serve, and safer for everyone who stars a project.

Image via Eremite Games

In order to address this, they’re adding in new systems for unlocking things, such as relying on reputation from villager species as requirements. When I reviewed Against the Storm (which I’ll remind you was one of my favorite games of the year) at its full launch, one of the things I praised most was its ability to juggle so many systems and facets without it weakening the overall experience. I can absolutely see how adding more into it could make it unbalanced, but, at this point, Eremite Games has my faith that they can do it.

They also announced that they will announce the release date of patch 1.4 at that time. They also remarked that this is an announcement of an announcement. Having worked in this role for a while now, I recognize that announcing an announcement is pretty standard, but it’s still rather weird.
